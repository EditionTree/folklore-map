Ghosts Slaghtaverty, County Londonderry

Abhartach

OW-ur-takh

Patrick Weston Joyce recorded the legend of Abhartach, a cruel dwarf-magician who repeatedly returned after burial. A neighbouring chieftain finally subdued his power by burying him upside-down beneath a stone monument associated with Slaghtaverty.

The Legend

Abhartach was a cruel and tyrannical dwarf-chieftain of County Londonderry folklore, some say a magician, hated enough by his own people that a neighbouring chief named Cathán killed and buried him. The antiquarian Patrick Weston Joyce recorded the story in The Origin and History of Irish Names of Places in 1869: the next day Abhartach returned, more terrible than before, demanding a bowl of blood from his subjects' veins to sustain him.

A cruel dwarf-magician who repeatedly returned after burial.

The Magician Who Would Not Stay Buried

Twice more he was slain and twice more he rose, until Cathán sought the counsel of a saint or druid, who revealed that Abhartach was one of the marbh bheo, the 'walking dead', and could not be truly killed, only restrained. He had to be slain with a sword of yew, buried upside down, and covered with thorns and a great stone. That is how he remains, at a townland still called Slaghtaverty, 'the dwarf's grave', where a leaning stone and thorn tree mark the spot local people still call the Giant's Grave, near Glenullin outside Dungiven.

The story resurfaced in the national press in the 2010s largely through Dr Bob Curran, a lecturer at Ulster University specialising in Celtic and Irish folklore, who has argued that this blood-drinking revenant, fixed in the earth by yew and stone, may have fed into Bram Stoker's conception of the vampire decades before Dracula was written. The theory has no direct documentary link to Stoker and rests on circumstantial parallels rather than proof, but it has become the detail most often repeated about Abhartach in modern retellings, layered on top of the older, better-attested core of Joyce's nineteenth-century record.

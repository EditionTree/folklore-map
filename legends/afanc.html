Beasts Gwynedd, Wales

Afanc

AH-vank

A shape-shifting water monster that haunted Welsh lakes, the Afanc was lured ashore by a sleeping maiden's lap and bound in chains, only to kill her the instant it woke.

The Legend

Welsh tradition tells of several Afancs lurking in deep pools and lakes across Wales, sometimes described as crocodile-like, sometimes beaver-like, sometimes simply a 'demon of the lake'. The earliest hint of the creature may be Gerald of Wales, who in his Itinerarium Cambriae (1191) describes strange beaver-like animals in Welsh rivers, though the fullest medieval version of the story appears later in the Mabinogion. The most famous tale tells how the creature could only be captured through trickery: a young woman was persuaded to let it rest its head in her lap while it slept, allowing local men to bind it in chains. When the Afanc woke and felt itself trapped, it thrashed so violently that it killed the maiden before finally being subdued.

The Monster Drawn from the Lake

Other versions give the story an Arthurian twist. At Llyn Barfog in the Dyfi valley, King Arthur's horse Llamrei is said to have dragged the bound Afanc from the water using chains looped around its neck, hauling it across the mountains to a lake so deep and cold that it could never climb out again. A still older account, recorded by the antiquarian Iolo Morganwg in the early 19th century, has the oxen of Hu Gadarn perform the same feat, dragging the beast to Llyn Llion after it caused a great flood, though scholars now regard much of Iolo's version as his own invention rather than a genuine survival. In the Mabinogion tale of Peredur, a similar 'Addanc of the Lake' is overcome only when the hero is given a stone that renders the invisible creature visible, allowing him to behead it.

The legend's staying power is written into the map. Llyn yr Afanc near Betws-y-coed takes its name directly from the creature, and several other lakes claim to be its former lair. The modern Welsh word afanc has since settled down to mean simply 'beaver', a tame fate for a monster once blamed for floods, drownings, and the deaths of those careless enough to wade into the wrong lake.

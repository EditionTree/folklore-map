Aimee Lake, the Last Witch of Guernsey

As late as January 1914, a Guernsey fortune-teller was convicted of practising witchcraft, three centuries after the island's last burnings, under the same old law.

The Legend

On 20 January 1914, Aimée Lake, a fortune-teller living at Robergerie in St Sampson's, was formally charged before the Royal Court of Guernsey with "fortune telling, the explanation of dreams and practising the art of witchcraft" between August 1913 and January 1914, making her the last person tried for witchcraft on the island, and one of the last in the British Isles under a genuinely old statute rather than a modern fraud law.

The Story

The complaint came from a farm widow of St Martin's whose cattle had died and who had begun suffering severe headaches. Desperate, she consulted Mrs Lake, who read her tea leaves, told her the deaths were the result of sorcery worked against her, and sold her charmed packets of powder for £3 10s to counter the curse, with a demand for a further £3 when the trouble didn't lift. Frightened, the widow went to the police, who dug up the buried packets from her garden and had them analysed: the "charms" turned out to be nothing more than baking powder, brown starch and cornflour, the everyday ingredients Mrs Lake bought to make blancmange.

Despite the harmlessness of the powders, the Royal Court under Bailiff William Carey convicted her and sentenced her to eight days' imprisonment, the maximum available, with the court reportedly expressing regret it could not impose something harsher. The case is remembered today less as a genuine brush with the supernatural than as a vivid glimpse of how long Guernsey's witchcraft law, and the folk belief that sustained it, survived into the twentieth century.

Deities Knockainey (Cnoc Áine), County Limerick, Munster

Áine

AWN-ya

Ireland's radiant goddess of summer and sovereignty, Áine presided over love, cattle, and the harvest from her hill at Knockainey in County Limerick; on Midsummer's Eve, farmers climbed her hill with burning torches of hay and swept the fire through the fields and over the herds to secure her blessing for the year.

A visual interpretation of Áine and the midsummer rite at Knockainey
The Legend

Áine is one of the most fully realised sovereignty goddesses in Irish mythology, closely associated with the hill of Cnoc Áine (anglicised Knockainey) in County Limerick and with the nearby waters of Lough Gur. She belongs to the Tuatha Dé Danann and is identified in medieval sources as both a goddess and a fairy queen — the distinction was not sharp in the Irish tradition — and sometimes as the daughter of Manannán mac Lir, the sea god. Her spheres were summer, love, fertility, crops, cattle, and the abundance of the earth.

Local families climbed Cnoc Áine carrying torches of hay and straw tied to hazel poles.

The Torches on Cnoc Áine

The primary documentation of Áine's cult comes from John O'Donovan's nineteenth-century letters for the Ordnance Survey of Ireland and from the Irish Folklore Commission fieldwork in Limerick in the early twentieth century. O'Donovan recorded an annual ritual on St John's Eve (Midsummer) in which local families climbed Cnoc Áine carrying torches of hay and straw tied to hazel poles. The torches were lit at the top, and people ran through the fields and herds driving the flames among the crops and cattle — a sympathetic magic of summer fire to invoke Áine's blessing for the coming harvest and the health of the livestock. The ritual continued in attenuated form into the late nineteenth century.

Áine's mythology intersects with the Geraldine Earls of Desmond: she appears in one tradition as the mother of Gearóid Iarla, the Fourth Earl of Desmond, by the human lord Gearóid Mór — who seized her cloak while she bathed in Lough Gur, making her his wife. Gerald — who in legend was a shape-shifter who could transform into any creature — was said to ride around Lough Gur on a white horse once every seven years, waiting to return to power. Áine was also known as the Lady of the Lake of Lough Gur, whose waters hold their own tradition of a drowned town audible to those who listen on certain nights.

Alice Kyteler

Dame Alice Kyteler was Ireland's first convicted witch, accused in 1324 of poisoning four husbands with potions brewed from their nightgowns and consorting with a demon called Robin Artisson.

The Legend

Alice Kyteler (c.1260, after 1324) was a wealthy Flemish merchant's daughter from Kilkenny who outlived four husbands, and in 1324 her own stepchildren accused her of poisoning each one with potions concocted at night using ingredients taken from their own nightgowns. The case, brought by Richard de Ledrede, the papally-appointed bishop of Ossory acting on Pope John XXII's concerns about sorcery as heresy, was among the earliest formal witchcraft prosecutions anywhere in Europe, predating the continental witch panics by over a century.

Her own stepchildren accused her of poisoning each of her four husbands.

The Case That Shook Kilkenny

The formal charges eventually ran to seven counts: denying Christ and the Church, sacrificing animal parts at crossroads to a demon, stealing the keys to the parish church for nocturnal meetings, brewing potions from macabre ingredients to cause love, hatred or death, maintaining carnal relations with a demon who appeared as a cat, a shaggy black dog or a black man, named in most tellings as Robin Artisson, and using sorcery to murder or manipulate her husbands for financial gain. Kyteler and her circle were said to make him offerings of cockerels and peacock feathers in exchange for wealth and power. As the bishop closed in, Alice fled to England and was never seen again, escaping the fate that befell her household.

Her maidservant Petronilla de Meath was not so fortunate. Tortured until she confessed to sorcery and implicated her mistress, Petronilla was flogged six times and burned at the stake in Kilkenny on 3 November 1324, the first recorded burning for witchcraft in Ireland, and one of the earliest in the British Isles.

The prosecution was not straightforward even at the time. Arnold le Poer, the Kilkenny seneschal and a relative of Alice's fourth husband, actively obstructed Ledrede's inquisitorial authority and briefly had the bishop himself imprisoned, so the case became as much a struggle between secular and church power in Ireland as it was a trial of witchcraft. Alice's son William Outlaw was made to do penance for his part in the affair, including a pilgrimage and funding repairs to Kilkenny's cathedral, a debt that, by local tradition, left its roof in worse repair than before. Kyteler's own townhouse on St Kieran's Street survives today as Kyteler's Inn, still trading under her name seven centuries later.

Andraste

A warrior goddess invoked by Boudica before her revolt against Rome. Her name survives through Roman accounts, fierce and blood-bright at the edge of history and myth.

The Legend

Andraste is a warrior-goddess of the ancient Britons, known from a single dramatic moment in the historical record: the great revolt of Boudica against Rome in AD 60–61. The Roman historian Cassius Dio relates that Boudica, queen of the Iceni of East Anglia, invoked Andraste before leading her people to war, releasing a hare from the folds of her dress as a means of divination and calling on the goddess for victory. The name is usually understood to mean something like 'the invincible' or 'she who is unconquered'.

The Goddess at the Edge of History

Dio's account makes Andraste a goddess of war, slaughter and victory, and links her cult to a sacred grove in which the Britons made terrible offerings of their Roman captives, mutilating and impaling noblewomen taken prisoner amid feasting and sacrifice. Later in the same narrative Dio names a goddess called Andate, whom he glosses simply as the Britons' word for Victory and ties to the same grove of atrocities; most scholars read Andate as either a scribal variant of Andraste or a second name for the same goddess rather than a wholly separate figure, though Dio's text never says so outright. She may also be connected with the Gaulish Andarta, a similarly named goddess honoured on the Continent.

Because she survives only through the pen of a hostile Roman writing more than a century after the events, Andraste is known to us from that single moment alone, a goddess invoked once, at the last stand the Iceni made against Rome.

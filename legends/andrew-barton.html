Andrew Barton

The Leith privateer Sir Andrew Barton raided Portuguese shipping before his death in battle in 1511. English and Scottish folk songs transformed him into a ballad figure: Sir Andrew Barton and its later relation Henry Martyn both preserve versions of his story.

The Legend

Sir Andrew Barton was a Leith sea-captain of the early sixteenth century, the most famous of a seafaring family who held a Scottish royal licence — a letter of marque — to prey on Portuguese shipping in revenge for an earlier wrong done to his father. To the Scots he was a patriot privateer; to the English, who accused him of attacking their merchants under cover of that licence, he was little better than a pirate.

Fight on, my men, I am hurt but I am not slain.

The Captain in the Ballad

In 1511 an English squadron under the sons of the Earl of Surrey hunted him down in the Channel. Barton fought his ship, the Lion, to the last, and the old ballad that bears his name tells how, mortally wounded, he urged his men on with his dying breath: 'Fight on, my men, I am hurt but I am not slain; I'll lie me down and bleed awhile, and then I'll rise and fight again.' His death helped sour relations between the two kingdoms in the years before Flodden, and the stirring ballad kept his memory alive in both Scotland and England.

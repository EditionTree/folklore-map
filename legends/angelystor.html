Ghosts Llangernyw, Conwy, Wales

Angelystor

an-geh-LUHS-tor

Every Halloween, a disembodied voice by Llangernyw's ancient yew reads out the names of parishioners who will die within the year, and anyone who goes to listen risks hearing their own.

The Legend

At St Digain's Church in the Conwy village of Llangernyw, a yew stands whose trunk measures more than ten metres round, a girth that puts it among the handful of British trees old enough to be classed simply as 'ancient.' Tradition holds the church itself was founded in the fifth century by St Digain, and in 2002 the yew was named one of Fifty Great British Trees to mark the Queen's Golden Jubilee. Eight younger yews have since been planted around the same churchyard, but none carries the weight of legend borne by the old one beside the porch.

Each Halloween night, the spirit announces the names of parishioners who will die in the coming year.

The Names from the Churchyard

On Halloween night, and again on the eve of St John's, a solemn disembodied voice was said to be heard from beside the ancient tree, naming aloud in Welsh those of the parish who would die before the year turned. Anyone bold enough to go and listen took a risk. Tradition warned that disturbing the Angelystor could see your own name added to its list.

The best-known story tells of a sceptical local tailor, Siôn ap Robert, who scoffed at the belief and went to the church on Halloween to disprove it, only to hear his own name called by the Angelystor. He protested that he was not ready, the story goes, but died within the year as foretold. The yew that framed his fate is still there, one of the oldest living things in Wales, sealed off from casual visitors now but still visible from the churchyard path.

Origins & Dating

Approximate OriginUncertain oral origin; the term 'Angelystor' is attested in print by 1832 and the narrative legend (the death-prophecy at Llangernyw) by 1896
Historical PeriodIn print by 1832 (term); narrative form recorded by 1896
Earliest Written RecordWilliam Owen Pughe's A Dictionary of the Welsh Language (1832) records the term 'Angelystor'; the fuller narrative (including the Siôn Ap Rhobert death-prophecy story) is recorded in Rev. Elias Owen's Welsh Folk-Lore (written for the 1887 National Eisteddfod, published 1896)

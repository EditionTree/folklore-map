Asrai

A delicate water spirit said to melt away if captured or exposed to sunlight. The Asrai belongs to the colder, sadder edge of fairy lore, all moonlit water and vanishing hands.

The Legend

The Asrai are a delicate and sorrowful kind of water-fairy from English folklore, particularly associated with the meres, pools and lakes of the Cheshire and Shropshire borders and the north-west. They are described as small, slender and beautiful female spirits who dwell deep underwater, rarely seen by mortals, rising to the surface only at long intervals, once a century, in some tellings, perhaps to bathe in the moonlight or to glimpse the moon they so seldom see.

A delicate water spirit said to melt away if captured or exposed to sunlight.

The Spirit That Vanished at Dawn

Their legend is almost always one of fatal fragility. The classic tale tells of a fisherman who, drawing his nets one night, catches an asrai in them, a frail, lovely creature who pleads piteously for her freedom in a voice he cannot understand, and whose touch is icy cold. Determined to keep his prize, he binds her and lays her in the bottom of his boat, covering her with wet weeds, and rows for home; but as the night wears on she moans and weakens, and where her cold body touched his arm the flesh is marked forever. By the time he reaches the shore she has melted entirely away, leaving nothing in the boat but a little pool of water.

Unlike most of the site's water-fairies, the asrai is a Victorian literary invention rather than inherited folk belief: the legend first appears in Robert Williams Buchanan's poem 'The Asrai' (1872), followed the next year by a companion poem about a male asrai seeking a soul. Buchanan wrote in the style of older water-fairy tradition and the story has since been retold and anthologised as though it were ancient folklore, but no earlier oral or written source for the asrai as a distinct English fairy type has been found.

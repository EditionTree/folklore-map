Avalon

The Isle of Apples where Arthur sleeps and Morgan le Fay governs — first named by Geoffrey of Monmouth in 1136, later claimed by Glastonbury monks who 'discovered' Arthur's grave to fund their rebuilding appeal.

A visual interpretation of Arthur's journey to Avalon
The Legend

Avalon makes its first appearance in Geoffrey of Monmouth's Historia Regum Britanniae, completed around 1136. Here Geoffrey records that after Arthur's defeat at the Battle of Camlann, the mortally wounded king was carried to the island of Avalon for his wounds to be healed. Geoffrey elaborated in the Vita Merlini (c.1150), calling it 'Insula Pomorum' — the Isle of Apples — and describing it as a fortunate island far in the Atlantic, ruled by nine enchantresses, the greatest of whom was Morgan, Arthur's half-sister and a skilled healer. The name Avalon derives from the Celtic root *aballo (apple), cognate with the Welsh afal and the Old Irish aball; Geoffrey was adapting an existing Celtic concept of a blessed island of the dead reached by sea, related to the Irish Tír na nÓg and the tradition of Emain Ablach ('Emhain of the Apple Trees'), the island realm of the sea god Manannán mac Lir.

Arthur sleeps here still, waiting to return when Britain needs him most.

The Isle of Rest

The Glastonbury connection was made decisively in 1191, when monks at Glastonbury Abbey claimed to have unearthed, in their graveyard, a stone slab inscribed 'Hic iacet sepultus inclytus rex Arthurus' — Here lies buried the renowned King Arthur. Glastonbury sits on a natural hill that was once nearly surrounded by the Somerset marshes and could genuinely be described as an island; the Welsh name for Glastonbury, Ynys Wydryn (the Isle of Glass), was conveniently suggestive of Avalon. Modern archaeological consensus holds that the discovery was a fabrication by the monks, who needed funds to rebuild the abbey after a catastrophic fire in 1184. King Henry II, who had political interest in promoting Arthur as permanently dead rather than a sleeping king awaiting return, may have encouraged the story.

The 'once and future king' tradition — that Arthur does not die but sleeps in Avalon, waiting to return when Britain needs him most — was current in Welsh and Breton poetry before Geoffrey's account and proved far more resilient than the Glastonbury grave. Thomas Malory's Morte d'Arthur (completed c.1469, printed 1485) preserves both strands: Arthur is carried to Avalon by three queens, and the hermit who claims to have buried him cannot say whether his grave is real. Avalon has been identified with the Canary Islands, the Scilly Isles, and multiple sites in Wales and Scotland, but its power as mythological geography — a borderland between death and continued existence — has never required a physical address.

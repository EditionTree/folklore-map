Sacred Sites Wiltshire

Avebury

The largest stone circle in the world — so vast a village was built inside it. The Devil is said to have danced here, one stone crosses the road at midnight, and a medieval barber lies crushed beneath the megaliths where he fell trying to bury them.

A visual interpretation of Avebury at dawn
The Legend

Avebury in Wiltshire is the largest stone circle in the world, its outer ring enclosing an area so vast that the medieval village of Avebury grew up inside it, with houses, a pub, and a church nestled among the megaliths. The stones have attracted folklore for centuries, and the legends cluster around a single theme: the circle is dangerous, alive, and not entirely of this world.

At night the stones are said to move, drink from the stream, and return before dawn.

The Stones That Move by Night

The best-known tale is the petrified wedding party. The stones of the Great Circle are said to be the remains of revellers who were tricked by a fiddle-playing Devil into dancing on a Sunday and turned to stone for their sin — a legend shared with the Stanton Drew circle in Somerset and the Merry Maidens in Cornwall. A particular stone known as the Devil’s Chair, shaped like a rough throne with a natural seat, is said to be the spot where the Devil himself sat to watch his handiwork. Local tradition holds that running anticlockwise around the Devil’s Chair one hundred times will summon him. The Diamond Stone, a smaller megalith near the northern entrance, is said to uproot itself and cross the road at the stroke of midnight, returning to its position before dawn — one of several ‘walking stone’ legends attached to British megalithic sites.

The most historically grounded legend concerns the Barber Surgeon. In the fourteenth century, the medieval Church ordered the stones buried to suppress the pagan rituals that had revived around them. During the burial campaign, a stone fell on one of the workers and crushed him. When archaeologist Alexander Keiller re-excavated the circle in the 1930s, he found a skeleton pinned beneath a fallen megalith, accompanied by a pair of scissors and a lancet — the tools of a barber-surgeon. The discovery confirmed a folk memory that had persisted for six hundred years and may explain why the stone-felling campaign stopped abruptly: the villagers believed the Devil had taken his revenge.

Barns of Ayr

Blind Harry's Wallace tells how the English lured Scottish nobles to Ayr's barracks under a flag of peace, hanged them — and condemned Wallace as an outlaw. He came back that night with fire.

The Legend

The legend of the Barns of Ayr comes to us from Blind Harry's The Wallace, a late 15th-century verse epic written around 1477, more than a century and a half after the events it purports to describe. In Harry's account, English soldiers gathered Scottish nobles of Ayrshire — including Sir Ronald Crawford, Sheriff of Ayr, Sir Bryce Blair of Blair, Sir Neil Montgomerie, Crystal of Seton, and Sir Hugh Montgomerie — to a supposed meeting at a large building in Ayr being used as an English military garrison. When the nobles arrived, they were set upon: the more prominent among them were hanged on the spot. The massacre, in Harry's telling, was deliberate treachery by an occupying power, a violation of the peace flag under which the meeting had been called.

Blind Harry made the burning barns one of Wallace's great acts of revenge.

The Night of Fire

The act of revenge that followed became one of the defining moments of Wallace's legendary biography. Wallace assembled a force, surrounded the barracks at night, and set the building on fire with the English garrison inside. In The Wallace, this act of burning is presented not as atrocity but as righteous retribution — the English had used the law against Scotland's nobles; Wallace used fire. Modern historians have found no corroborating documentary evidence for a mass killing of Scottish nobles in Ayrshire at this time, and Blind Harry is now regarded as an unreliable historian who drew on oral tradition and political motivation to create a Wallace who embodied the full extent of Scottish grievance against the English crown.

The historical kernel is real: Edward I's Scottish campaign of 1296–1307 was brutal, and the Barns of Ayr legend functions as a compression of genuine oppression into a single memorable image of treachery and fire. The legend passed into Scottish popular culture with considerable force, eventually appearing in Braveheart as the film's emotional turning point. The site at Ayr — no longer standing — appeared in early maps of the town, and the legend was preserved locally long before Blind Harry committed it to verse, suggesting it had circulated as oral tradition in Ayrshire for generations.

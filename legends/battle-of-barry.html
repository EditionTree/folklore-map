Battle of Barry

Malcolm II's army supposedly crushed a Danish fleet at Carnoustie in 1010 — a legend so compelling it shaped the town's identity, though modern archaeology suggests the 'Danish graves' were Pictish long-cists all along.

The Legend

The Battle of Barry occupies a specific place on early Ordnance Survey maps of Angus, marked near the eastern boundary of the old parish of Barry, close to the Lochty burn at what became the town of Carnoustie. The tradition holds that King Malcolm II of Scotland defeated a Danish invasion force here in 1010, five years before his decisive victory at Carham that established Scottish control over Lothian. Malcolm II had a substantial military reputation, and the existence of multiple battle traditions around his reign gave the Barry legend a plausible dynastic context. The site's marked position on early OS maps conferred a degree of official recognition on what was in origin a local oral tradition.

The mounds once taken for Danish graves proved to be older Pictish long-cists.

The Danish Graves

The legend was first written down by the 16th-century Scottish historian Hector Boece in his Scotorum Historia (1527). Boece was a native of Dundee — close enough to the supposed battlefield to draw on local oral tradition — and his account was widely read, though he is now regarded as an unreliable narrator who embroidered royal chronicles with popular memory. His account centred on a Danish fleet entering the Tay estuary and a Scottish force under Malcolm meeting them at Barry. Burial mounds on the edge of the parish were identified as Danish warrior graves, giving the legend physical anchoring in the visible landscape.

The decisive challenge came from archaeology. Investigation of the burial mounds — some opened during 19th-century building work in Carnoustie — revealed a Pictish long-cist cemetery rather than a mass grave of fallen warriors. The goods found included no weapons; the skeletal remains included the body of a middle-aged woman who had died of tuberculosis, a profile wholly inconsistent with a battlefield burial. The 'Danish graves' were domestic Pictish burials whose anonymous mounds had been pressed into service as evidence for a battle that almost certainly never took place at Barry. The legend survives intact in Carnoustie's local heritage, continuing to function as the town's foundational origin story even after its historical basis dissolved.

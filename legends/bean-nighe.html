Bean Nighe

pen NYEE-uh

The washerwoman at the ford, a small, webbed-footed spirit found scrubbing blood-stained shrouds in running water. To see her is to know that death is coming.

The Legend

The Bean Nighe washes at the ford, scrubbing the blood-stained grave-clothes of the soon-to-be-dead in a lonely stream or pool. Small, often described with webbed feet, a single nostril or pendulous breasts, she is a form of the banshee found across Scottish Highland and Hebridean tradition, and to come upon her was to receive a warning of mortality, sometimes one's own.

To see her is to know that death is coming.

The Washer at the Ford

Highland lore held that the Bean Nighe could be compelled to grant wishes or to name the doomed, but the method was strange and specific: a bold person who crept up on her from behind and, before she noticed, took hold of her breast (said on Mull and Tiree to be so long that she slung them over her shoulders as she worked) and pretended to suckle as a foster-child would be granted three questions and a promise that she would spare anyone whose shroud she was seen washing. The nineteenth-century folklorist John Gregorson Campbell recorded the belief among Highlanders still living the tradition, in his Superstitions of the Highlands and Islands of Scotland. But to be seen by her first, before you could act, was perilous.

Some held her to be the ghost of a woman who died in childbirth, doomed to wash until the day she would naturally have died, haunting the running water that so often marks the threshold between this world and the next in Celtic belief.

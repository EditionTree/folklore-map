Beast of Bodmin

A large phantom cat said to roam Bodmin Moor, blamed for mutilated livestock since the 1990s. Official investigations found no conclusive evidence, yet sightings persist to this day.

The Legend

The Beast of Bodmin Moor is the most famous of Britain's 'alien big cats', a large, black, panther-like creature reported prowling the wild uplands of Cornwall since the 1970s and blamed for slain and mutilated livestock. Witnesses describe a powerful cat three to five feet long with a heavy tail and glowing eyes, glimpsed at dusk among the granite tors and bracken. Grainy photographs and plaster casts of paw-prints have fuelled the legend without ever settling it.

Official investigations found no conclusive evidence, yet sightings persist to this day.

The Cat in the Cornish Dusk

In 1995 the government commissioned an official investigation, led by Simon Baker and Charles Wilson for the Ministry of Agriculture, Fisheries and Food. Reporting on 19 July 1995, it found no verifiable evidence of an exotic cat on the moor, concluding the livestock deaths could be explained by known British predators and scavengers. Within days, on 24 July, a boy walking beside the River Fowey found a large cat skull. The Natural History Museum confirmed it was genuine, the skull of a young male leopard, its back cleanly cut away in the style used to mount a head on a rug, and that the animal had certainly never lived wild in Britain. Sightings carried on regardless. One theory traces the whole phenomenon to 1978, when the animal trainer Mary Chipperfield is said to have released three pumas into the wild after closing her Plymouth zoo, part of a wider modern wave of British cryptid sightings often linked to exotic pets released after the Dangerous Wild Animals Act of 1976; whether flesh, escapee or imagination, the Beast has made Bodmin Moor a byword for the watchful dark.

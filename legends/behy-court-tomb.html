Behy court tomb

Local turf cutters near Belderrig knew this court tomb's buried chamber long before archaeologists did, and gave it a name of their own: the Roomeens, once pressed into service as a poteen still.

The Legend

Behy court tomb sits half swallowed by peat on the slope of Maumakeogh hill above Belderrig, County Mayo, and long before it drew archaeologists it had a name of its own among the people who cut turf around it. They called the buried chamber the Roomeens, the "little rooms," and at some point its sunken hollow was put to a practical use, as a still house where poteen was distilled out of sight of the excise men who might otherwise have come looking.

The court tomb looks north across the Atlantic from the Céide Fields landscape.

The Tomb Above the Sea

The tomb came to formal attention when Ruaidhrí de Valéra and Seán Ó Nualláin surveyed it in 1952, part of their wider study of Ireland's court tombs. It stands within what is now known as the Céide Fields, the oldest known field system in the world, and is the most westerly of some thirty court tombs recorded in the Killala group. From its position 150 metres above sea level, the site commands wide views north across the sea to the Stags of Broadhaven.

Today the Roomeens lies a short walk from the Céide Fields visitor centre, still mostly buried under the same blanket bog that once hid its use as a still, its cruciform gallery a rare survival from a landscape farmed since the Neolithic.

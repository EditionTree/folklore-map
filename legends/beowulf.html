Beowulf

Anglo-Saxon hero who slew the monster Grendel with his bare hands in the Danish hall of Heorot, then killed Grendel's mother in the depths of her mere, and finally died old, fighting a dragon alone, for the sake of his people.

The legend

Beowulf is the great epic poem of Old English literature — over three thousand lines of alliterative verse, preserved in a single manuscript dated to around 1000 AD but describing a world of the fifth and sixth centuries, set in Scandinavia among the Geats, Danes, and other Germanic peoples. It is the foundational text of English literature: the oldest surviving major poem in the language.

The story

The hero Beowulf crosses the sea to help Hrothgar, King of the Danes, whose great mead-hall Heorot has been terrorised for twelve years by the monster Grendel. In the most famous scene in the poem, Beowulf waits in the darkened hall and fights Grendel bare-handed, tearing the monster's arm from its socket. Grendel retreats to die in the fens; his mother comes in the night for revenge. Beowulf dives into her mere and kills her in the depths.

The poem's final section takes place decades later. Beowulf is now an old king whose land is threatened by a dragon woken by a theft from its hoard. His companions flee; one returns. The old king and the dragon kill each other. He is buried in a barrow on the headland where his people will see it from the sea. The poem ends not in triumph but in elegy — a meditation on transience, loyalty, and what it means to lead well in a world that cannot be saved.

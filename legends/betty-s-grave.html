Betty's Grave

A lone Gloucestershire crossroads is named for a woman hanged as a witch, gypsy, poisoner or sheep-thief depending who you ask — and villagers left her flowers for two centuries so she wouldn't return.

The Legend

A quiet rural crossroads a quarter-mile from the Gloucestershire village of Poulton, near Cirencester, has been marked on maps since at least 1830 as Betty's Grave, named for a woman called Elizabeth Bastre said to have been buried there in 1786. What Elizabeth actually was depends entirely on which version of the story is told: a witch hanged or burned for her craft, a highwaywoman who lured travellers to their doom, a suicide denied burial in consecrated ground, a gypsy, a murderess who poisoned her employer, or a housemaid poisoned by hers, or simply a thief hanged for stealing sheep. Crossroads burial was itself the traditional English punishment for suicides and executed criminals considered unfit for a churchyard, which may explain why so many conflicting explanations have grown up to fill in an anonymous grave's story.

The Story

The most vivid version of the tale casts Betty as an outright witch, dancing naked under the moon, reciting spells, and coughing up crows and sharp needles — powers she was said to use to lure and rob travellers on the lonely crossroads. Whatever she was, local custom held that her spirit would return to trouble the village unless her grave was kept decorated with fresh flowers, a practice that persisted, remarkably, until the 1970s, when it stopped as abruptly and inexplicably as it had apparently been observed for nearly two centuries.

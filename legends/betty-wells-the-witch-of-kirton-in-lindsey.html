Betty Wells, the Witch of Kirton in Lindsey

An elderly neighbour swore for decades that Betty Wells was a 'real witch' — a belief villagers still repeated a century after her supposed heyday.

The Legend

In the village of Kirton in Lindsey, folklorist Ethel Rudkin (Mrs 'Peter' Rudkin), the pioneering Lincolnshire archaeologist and writer, collected first-hand testimony about a reputed witch named Betty Wells. A neighbour, Jenny Andrew, insisted to Rudkin that Betty had been a 'real witch', a conviction that reportedly persisted among villagers into the 1930s — nearly a century after Betty's supposed period of activity in the 1830s. The tale is notable less for a single dramatic incident than for the durability of village conviction: an ordinary woman's reputation for witchcraft outliving her by generations, passed down as accepted local fact rather than a story told for entertainment.

The Story

Rudkin's fieldwork in and around Kirton in Lindsey, later distilled into her academic article 'Lincolnshire Folklore: Witches and Devils' (Folklore journal, 1934) and her landmark 1936 book Lincolnshire Folklore, is among the most respected primary sources for the county's witch-belief, built from real conversations with villagers rather than secondhand retellings. Kirton in Lindsey's folklore is further coloured by its 'Boggle' of Low Road — a distinct, unfriendly spirit said to haunt a sunken lane on the edge of the village — showing a community with a genuinely active supernatural tradition beyond Betty's case alone.

Together these anecdotes illustrate how deeply and specifically English witch-belief persisted into living memory in rural Lincolnshire, long after the county's formal witchcraft prosecutions had ended.

Betty Wells, the Witch of Kirton in Lindsey

An elderly neighbour swore for decades that Betty Wells was a 'real witch,' a belief villagers still repeated a century after her supposed heyday.

The Legend

In the village of Kirton in Lindsey, folklorist Ethel Rudkin (Mrs 'Peter' Rudkin), the pioneering Lincolnshire archaeologist and writer, collected first-hand testimony about a reputed witch named Betty Wells. Rudkin lived in the village herself, in a house on Long Lane she filled with the objects and stories she gathered on her rounds, and it was there that a neighbour, Jenny Andrew, insisted to her that Betty had been a 'real witch,' a conviction that reportedly persisted among villagers into the 1930s, nearly a century after Betty's supposed period of activity in the 1830s. The tale is notable less for a single dramatic incident than for the durability of village conviction: an ordinary woman's reputation for witchcraft outliving her by generations, passed down as accepted local fact rather than a story told for entertainment.

The Story

Rudkin's fieldwork in and around Kirton in Lindsey, later distilled into her academic article 'Lincolnshire Folklore: Witches and Devils' (Folklore journal, 1934) and her landmark 1936 book Lincolnshire Folklore, is among the most respected primary sources for the county's witch-belief, built from real conversations with villagers rather than secondhand retelling.

Kirton in Lindsey's folklore runs beyond Betty's case alone. The village also has its own 'Boggle' of Low Road, a distinct and unfriendly spirit said to haunt a sunken lane on the edge of the settlement, a stretch that already carried a grim reputation in its own right: local memory holds that at least one Victorian-era murder took place along its deep, hollow-sided dips. Villagers seem to have folded the two associations together, a real crime and an unfriendly haunting, into a single road best avoided after dark.

Birnam Wood and Dunsinane

Shakespeare's Macbeth is told he cannot be defeated until Birnam Wood marches to Dunsinane Hill, a prophecy he takes as proof of his invincibility since forests cannot move. His enemies fulfil it literally by cutting branches from Birnam Wood to camouflage their advancing army as they approach Macbeth's stronghold at Dunsinane.

The Legend

The prophecy and its fulfilment are Shakespeare's own dramatic invention, first staged around 1606, built onto a real historical setting. Dunsinane Hill, near the village of Collace in Perthshire, carries the remains of an Iron Age hillfort that later tradition identified as Macbeth's castle, and Birnam Wood lies a few miles away near the village of Birnam on the River Tay. The real Mac Bethad mac Findlaích did rule Scotland and was defeated in battle near Dunsinane in 1054 by forces loyal to Máel Coluim (the future Malcolm III), though he survived that battle and continued to reign for three more years before his eventual death in 1057, a considerably less immediate downfall than the play depicts.

The Story

Shakespeare drew his account chiefly from Holinshed's Chronicles rather than any surviving Scottish oral tradition of a moving wood, and the moving forest motif itself has folkloric echoes elsewhere in world storytelling, suggesting Shakespeare may have adapted a known dramatic device to the Macbeth material rather than recording a pre-existing local Perthshire legend. Once staged, however, the play's own version became attached to the real landscape, and Dunsinane Hill and Birnam Wood (including the ancient Birnam Oak, claimed as a survivor of the historic wood) remain popularly associated with the story today, regardless of the thin historical basis for the prophecy itself.

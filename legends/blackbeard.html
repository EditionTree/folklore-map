Blackbeard

Edward Teach, better known as Blackbeard, may have been born in Bristol. His fearsome image outgrew his short career: tales of buried treasure, ghostly lights at sea and his headless spirit searching the coast made him one of the defining figures of pirate folklore.

A visual interpretation of Blackbeard's fearsome image at sea
The Legend

Edward Teach, remembered as Blackbeard, became the most fearsome image of the whole pirate age though his actual career lasted barely two years. Tradition, following the 1724 General History of the Pyrates, makes him a Bristol man who learned the sea before turning to piracy in the Caribbean and along the American coast, capturing the great ship he renamed Queen Anne's Revenge and at one point blockading the harbour of Charleston itself.

His fearsome image outgrew his short career.

The Pirate Who Became a Legend

His power lay in terror as much as force: he cultivated a monstrous appearance, his great black beard braided and twisted, and was said to go into battle with lighted slow-matches smouldering under his hat, wreathing his face in smoke like a devil from hell. Killed in 1718 in a ferocious fight with a Royal Navy party at Ocracoke, he reportedly took five shots and twenty cuts before he fell; his severed head was hung from the victor's bowsprit. From that grisly end the legend only grew — of buried treasure never found, and of his headless ghost said to search the Carolina shore for it still.

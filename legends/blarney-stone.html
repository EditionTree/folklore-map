Blarney Stone

On the battlements of Blarney Castle, a limestone block offers the gift of eloquence to those who kiss it backwards over a sheer drop — but its power may come from Clíodhna, the Munster goddess of the waves.

The Legend

The legend of the Blarney Stone centres on Cormac Laidir MacCarthy, lord of Blarney, who is said to have been facing a legal case in the 15th century that he feared he could not win. He appealed to Clíodhna, the Munster goddess of love and beauty, for aid. She instructed him to kiss the first stone he found in the morning on the way to court. He did so, pleaded his case with extraordinary eloquence, and won. In gratitude he built the stone into the castle's parapet, where it has endured since the fortification was completed in 1446. A separate tradition credits Robert the Bruce with sending the stone as a gift to Cormac MacCarthy in thanks for Irish support at Bannockburn in 1314; a third legend involves a wandering old woman who revealed the stone's power to a Lord of Blarney nervous about a meeting with Queen Elizabeth I.

According to legend, kissing the stone endows the kisser with the gift of the gab.

The Stone That Grants Eloquence

The origin of the phrase 'the gift of the gab' (or simply 'blarney', meaning flattering and persuasive but not quite trustworthy talk) is itself disputed. William Henry Hurlbert, writing in 1888, noted that the legend of the stone's powers appeared to be less than a hundred years old at that point, suggesting it coalesced in the late 18th century. Whatever the legend's age, the castle and stone are documented far earlier: Blarney Castle stands in good condition and the limestone block is original to the upper battlements. Kissing the stone requires the visitor to lie on their back and lean out backwards over a significant drop, held by an attendant, to press their lips to the underside of the parapet.

The stone is now visited by roughly a million tourists annually and has become one of Ireland's most enduring cultural exports, lending the word 'blarney' to dictionaries worldwide as a common noun. Yet the Clíodhna tradition preserves an older and more specifically Irish framework: Clíodhna ('the shapely one') was the fairy queen of Munster, associated with the sea caves near Glandore in County Cork, and her blessing transforms flattery into a goddess-given gift rather than a mere tourist trick. The MacCarthy lords of Blarney and Muskerry were prominent Munster figures for four centuries, and the castle's standing today bears out a family that had, at the very least, a gift for political survival.

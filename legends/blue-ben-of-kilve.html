Blue Ben of Kilve

A blue dragon said to have lived in the cliffs at Kilve and served as the Devil's steed. When it fell into the sea, locals later pointed to fossil bones as proof of the beast.

A visual interpretation of Blue Ben falling from the fossil-rich Somerset cliffs
The Legend

Blue Ben of Kilve is a dragon of Somerset folk-legend, said to have made his lair in the cliffs and caves of the coast at Kilve, on the shore of the Bristol Channel beneath the Quantock Hills. The tale gives him an infernal connection: Blue Ben was reputed to be the personal steed of the Devil, who would ride the great blue dragon down to the fires of Hell — and to spare his master's hooves from the heat, Ben built himself a causeway of stones out across the mudflats so that the Devil might mount him cleanly from the shore.

When it fell into the sea, fossil bones in the cliffs were pointed to as proof of the beast.

The Blue Dragon in the Cliffs

His end came on those same mudflats. Crossing the treacherous tidal mud of the Kilve foreshore, the dragon is said to have become mired and trapped, unable to free himself, and to have sunk and drowned in the ooze, lost beneath the sea and silt. There the story might have ended — but the Kilve coast is part of the fossil-rich Jurassic strata of West Somerset, and its cliffs and beaches yield the bones and remains of ancient marine reptiles, ichthyosaurs and ammonites.

When such fossil bones were turned up, local tradition pointed to them as the very remains of Blue Ben, proof that the Devil's dragon had indeed met his end in the Kilve mud. The legend is a fine example of folklore grown up to explain a striking natural feature — the great stony fossil-bearing ledges and the dragon-like bones of the Somerset coast given a story to account for them.

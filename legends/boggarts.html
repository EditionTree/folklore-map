Boggarts

Malevolent household and landscape spirits afflicting the north of England, curdling milk, tangling hair, and tormenting livestock. Unlike the helpful brownie, a boggart cannot be appeased, only avoided.

The Legend

The boggart is the malevolent house- and field-spirit of the north of England, especially Lancashire and Yorkshire, the dark counterpart of the helpful brownie. Attached to a household or a particular patch of ground, it sours milk, tangles horses' manes, snatches food, and torments the family with knocks and clatter in the night; a 'boggart-hole' or boggart-haunted clough was a place to be avoided after dark.

Aye, we're flitting.

The Thing in the Churn

The most famous tale captures the boggart's stubborn attachment to a home. A family so plagued by their boggart that they resolve to flee load up the cart, only for a neighbour to ask if they are moving, and the boggart's own voice pipes up from the churn, 'Aye, we're flitting.' Realising they can never escape it, the family unloads and resigns itself to living with the creature. The story makes the point that a boggart is bound to its people as much as to its place, and cannot simply be left behind.

The name itself likely comes from the same Old English and Norse root that gives 'bogey' and 'bugbear', a word family for things that frighten, and the boggart shares its territory with several close cousins known by other names: the 'boggle' further north, and the 'hob', a term shared uneasily with the friendlier Scottish brownie, since the same household spirit could apparently turn from one into the other depending on how it was treated. This overlapping, inconsistent naming is itself part of the folklore: a spirit this local and this variable was never going to fit one tidy label.

Bomere Fish

A great pike of monstrous size said to inhabit Bomere Pool near Shrewsbury, guardian of a drowned village said to lie beneath its waters. The fish surfaces only to herald disaster for the local gentry — and the pool has a dark reputation that survives to this day.

The Legend

Bomere Pool is a deep, glacial lake near Condover, a few miles south of Shrewsbury in Shropshire, long wrapped in legend and a reputation for menace. The most famous of its tales concerns a great fish, a pike of monstrous size, said to dwell in its dark waters as guardian of a drowned village believed to lie beneath the surface. In the story the people of that village were sinners who scorned the warnings of a holy man; the waters rose and engulfed them, and their bells, it is said, can still be heard tolling beneath the pool.

The most famous of its tales concerns a great fish — a pike of monstrous size — said to dwell in its dark waters.

The Fish in the Mere

The giant pike is bound to a tale of a buried sword. The fish was reputed to wear, or to guard, the sword of a drowned warrior or lord, and it was said that no one could master the great fish or recover the blade. The pike was an omen as much as a creature: it surfaced, tradition held, to herald death or disaster for the local gentry and the families of the surrounding estates, a fish whose appearance was never good news.

Bomere's sinister reputation extends beyond the fish — the pool was said to be bottomless, to claim swimmers, and to be haunted by the ghost of a heartbroken lover. Such layered legends are typical of deep, still meres, where dark unfathomed water naturally drew stories of drowned settlements, guardian monsters and doom. The drowned-village motif in particular links Bomere to a wider British folklore of submerged lands, from Welsh Llyn Tegid to Cornish Lyonesse.

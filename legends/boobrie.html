Beasts Argyll, Scotland

Boobrie

BOO-bree

Off Argyll's sea lochs a shapeshifting water-demon could turn from black bull to gigantic bird in an instant, bellowing like cattle as it dragged a ploughman's whole team under the surface.

The Legend

On the Isle of Mull, a farmer ploughing near Loch Freisa watched one of his own oxen rear up mid-furrow, transform into an enormous black bird, and bellow before plunging into the loch, dragging the plough and the rest of the team down with it. That is the Boobrie of Argyll and the Hebridean sea lochs, a shapeshifting water-demon collected by John Francis Campbell of Islay in the 1860s and reproduced by the Celtic scholar George Henderson in Survivals in Belief among the Celts (1911).

Its cry, witnesses agreed, was not birdlike at all. It bellowed like a bull.

The Cry at the Lochside

Most tellings give it the shape of a gigantic black waterbird, taller than any crane and built for swimming rather than flight: a hooked beak reckoned at eleven inches wide and seventeen long, a neck nearly three feet in length, and short powerful legs ending in webbed, clawed feet whose print was said to match the spread of a stag's antlers. Gaelic speakers called it tarbh-boidhre, 'monster' or 'demon', though the related word boibhre, 'cow-giver', hints at an older identity closer to the cattle-stealing water-bulls of the same lochs. Whatever the name, witnesses agreed its cry was no birdcall. It bellowed like a bull, loud enough to carry across the water.

At Loch nan Dobhran on the west coast, the Boobrie took the shape it shared with the tarbh-uisge, the water-bull. A man named Eachann is said to have found a great black bull lying injured by the shore; months afterward, the same bull carried his sweetheart Phemie to safety from a rival suitor, bearing her off at impossible speed before it vanished into the loch with a Gaelic verse about three hundred years of bondage still owed. In high summer the creature was said to shrink to its smallest form altogether, a striped, many-legged gobhlachan no bigger than an earwig, which fed on the blood of grazing horses through August and September.

Drovers moving cattle through Argyll were warned to give the lochs a wide berth rather than let thirsty beasts wade in to drink. A hunter who once tracked a bird-shaped Boobrie to within eighty-five yards on a sea loch waited it out from a hide for more than six hours after it dived, and never saw it surface again.

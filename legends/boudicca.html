Boudicca

Queen of the Iceni who burned Camulodunum, Verulamium and Londinium to the ground in revolt against Rome, then chose her own end rather than face capture. Her spirit is held never to have left East Anglia.

The Legend

Boudicca, or Boudica, was queen of the Iceni tribe of what is now Norfolk and Suffolk. Her name most likely derives from the Proto-Celtic word for victory, boudā, an irony given how her rebellion ended. When her husband Prasutagus died, Rome ignored his will and seized the kingdom, flogged her, and violated her daughters. She united the Iceni with the Trinovantes of Essex, and what followed was one of the most devastating revolts in the history of Roman Britain.

The Queen Who Defied Rome

In 60 or 61 AD her forces destroyed Camulodunum (Colchester), Verulamium (St Albans), and Londinium (London) in sequence, burning them to the ground and killing an estimated 70,000 to 80,000 people, according to Tacitus. Archaeologists still find the physical evidence of that destruction today: a distinct reddened layer of scorched debris known as the Boudican destruction layer, turned up beneath the City of London, Colchester, and St Albans wherever excavation reaches deep enough. The Roman governor Suetonius Paulinus, who had been campaigning in Wales, marched back to meet her. The final battle's location is unknown; she died there, either by poison, illness, or her own hand.

Her legend grew after her death far beyond what the Roman sources record, and so did the search for her grave. A persistent modern claim places her burial between platforms nine and ten at King's Cross station in London, a story with no evidence behind it and probably a twentieth-century invention rather than a genuine old tradition. Victorian Britain made her a national symbol instead: the bronze statue at Westminster Bridge, queen in her war chariot with anachronistic scythed wheels, was sculpted by Thomas Thornycroft over more than a decade and finally erected in 1902, though family papers suggest his wife Mary and other relatives did much of the uncredited work. From 1906 the suffragette movement adopted Boudicca as one of its own symbols, a warrior queen who had fought back against an empire that wronged her. In East Anglian tradition she never left: the flat fields of Norfolk have been held to still contain something of what she put into them.

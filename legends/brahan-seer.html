Legendary Figures Brahan, Ross-shire

Brahan Seer

Burned alive in a tar barrel at Chanonry Point for revealing his patroness's unfaithful husband, the seventeenth-century seer Coinneach Odhar had already foretold the railways as 'long strings of carriages without horses'. As the flames rose, he pronounced the doom of the Seaforth line itself.

The Legend

Coinneach Odhar, the Brahan Seer, is the great prophet of the Scottish Highlands, a seventeenth-century man of Ross-shire said to have possessed the taibhsearachd, the Second Sight, which he received, tradition says, by looking through a holed stone given him by the fairies. From the lands of the Mackenzies of Seaforth around Brahan Castle he uttered a stream of prophecies, many couched in riddling terms, foretelling events centuries off: the coming of the railways ('long strings of carriages without horses'), the Caledonian Canal, the desolation of the Highlands in the Clearances, and the ruin of the Seaforth line itself.

Long strings of carriages without horses.

The Stone and the Prophecy

His own end, as the legend tells it, came when he offended Lady Seaforth by revealing, at her demand, that her absent husband was dallying with another woman in Paris. In her fury she had him condemned for witchcraft and burned in a barrel of tar at Chanonry Point on the Black Isle. As the flames rose he pronounced his final and most famous prophecy, the detailed doom of the Seaforth family, ending in the extinction of the male line, which later generations recorded as having come precisely true. A stone at Chanonry Point marks the place of his death.

The written record of the seer is thinner than his fame suggests. Thomas Pennant's A Tour in Scotland (1769) contains the first known reference to a Highland prophet called 'Kenneth Odhar', more than a century after his supposed death. The name 'Brahan Seer' itself only enters print in 1877, when Alexander Mackenzie published The Prophecies of the Brahan Seer, an expansion of a paper he had read to the Gaelic Society of Inverness two years earlier, drawing together prophecies that had circulated in Gaelic oral tradition for generations. It is Mackenzie's book, more than any earlier source, that fixed the seer's story in the form still told today.

Bran the Blessed

A giant king of Britain so vast no house could contain him. He carried his armies across the Irish Sea on his own back. His severed head, buried beneath the Tower of London, was said to protect Britain from invasion, until Arthur dug it up.

The Legend

Bran the Blessed, Bendigeidfran, is the giant-king of Britain at the heart of the Second Branch of the Mabinogi, a figure so vast that no house could ever contain him and so mighty that he waded the Irish Sea at the head of his host, his musicians playing on his back. When his sister Branwen, married to the King of Ireland, was abused and set to drudge in his kitchens, Bran led an army across the water to avenge her.

He waded the Irish Sea at the head of his host, his musicians playing on his back.

The King No House Could Contain

The war was catastrophic, all but annihilating both sides, and Bran himself was wounded in the foot by a poisoned spear. Knowing he was dying, he commanded his seven surviving companions to cut off his head, which went on talking and feasting with them for many years in an enchanted timelessness, before they bore it at last to London and buried it on the White Hill (the site, tradition holds, of the Tower of London), facing France, so that as long as it lay there no invader could conquer Britain. King Arthur, the legend adds, later dug it up in his pride, trusting only his own strength to defend the land; and the ravens that are still kept at the Tower are said to be the lingering guardianship of Bran, whose name means 'raven'.

The tale survives in the Second Branch of the Mabinogi, 'Branwen ferch Llŷr', preserved in the White Book of Rhydderch (c. 1350) and the Red Book of Hergest (c. 1382-1410); scholars agree the story itself predates both manuscripts by an uncertain margin, rooted in pre-Norman Welsh oral tradition.

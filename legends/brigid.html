Brigid

Goddess of fire, poetry, healing and smithcraft, and one of the most beloved figures in Celtic tradition. Her festival Imbolc marks the first breath of spring. Christianised as Saint Brigid, her flame was tended for centuries at Kildare.

The Legend

Brigid is among the greatest and most enduring of the Celtic deities, a goddess of the Tuatha Dé Danann whose dominion spanned fire, poetry, healing and the craft of the smith. Daughter of the Dagda, she was honoured as a patroness of the filidh, the learned poets, and of all who worked transformation through flame, whether at the forge, the hearth or the sickbed. Medieval Irish tradition sometimes speaks of three sisters all named Brigid, a triple goddess of poetry, smithcraft and leechcraft, hinting at the breadth of her cult across the island.

Christianised as Saint Brigid, her flame was tended for centuries at Kildare.

The Flame of Spring

Her great festival is Imbolc, kept on the first of February, which marks the quickening of the year, the first stirring of spring, the lactation of the ewes, and the return of light after winter's dark. On Imbolc eve people would set out a cloth or ribbon, the Brat Bríde, for the goddess to bless as she passed, and weave the distinctive rush crosses still called Brigid's crosses for protection against fire and ill fortune.

So deeply rooted was she that the Church absorbed rather than erased her, and Brigid the goddess flows seamlessly into Saint Brigid of Kildare, one of Ireland's patron saints. At Kildare a perpetual sacred flame was tended by her holy women, kept burning for centuries and ringed by a hedge no man might cross, a clear inheritance of an older fire-cult. Wells across Ireland and Britain still bear her name, and her feast remains a living marker of spring's first breath.

Historical Context

Origins & Dating

Historical PeriodEarly Christian Ireland (as recorded); presumed pre-Christian Iron Age in origin as a deity
Earliest Written RecordCogitosus's Latin 'Vita Sanctae Brigitae' (Life of St Brigid), written not much later than 650 AD and among the earliest specimens of Irish hagiography, addresses the Christian saint; the goddess is first explicitly described in Cormac's Glossary ('Sanas Cormaic'), compiled c. 908 AD, which calls her 'the goddess whom poets adored' and names two sisters connected with healing and smithcraft

Folklore dates are often approximate, especially where a story predates any surviving written source.

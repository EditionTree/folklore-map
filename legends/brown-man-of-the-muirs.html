Brown Man of the Muirs

A small, fierce guardian spirit of the Border moors, protector of moorland animals from hunters who killed more than they needed. He appeared in terrible form to warn the greedy. Those who ignored him met with accidents, madness, or worse before they reached home.

The Legend

The Brown Man of the Muirs is a guardian spirit of the wild moorlands of the Anglo-Scottish border, a small, fierce, dwarf-like being with a shock of frizzled red-brown hair and great glowing eyes, dressed in withered bracken. He is the protector of the beasts of the moor — the birds, the hares, the wild creatures — and the enemy of hunters who kill more than they need or trespass on his ground.

The Brown Man stands among the oldest expressions of a folk idea that the wilderness has its own keeper, who exacts a price from those who abuse it.

The Warning at the Well

The classic tale, recorded by the antiquary Robert Surtees and used by Sir Walter Scott, tells of two young men who, hunting on the moors, sat to rest by a well. One saw the Brown Man appear and warn them angrily off his domain, offering only the moor's own wild fruits as fit food; the youth was minded to heed him, but his bolder companion scoffed and went on killing game — and within the year was dead.

Brown Man of the Muirs

A small, fierce guardian spirit of the Border moors above Elsdon, the Brown Man protects moorland animals from hunters who kill more than they need. Those who ignore his warning meet accidents, madness, or worse before they reach home.

The Legend

The Brown Man of the Muirs is a guardian spirit of the wild moorlands of the Anglo-Scottish border, a small, fierce, dwarf-like being with a shock of frizzled red-brown hair and great glowing eyes, dressed in withered bracken. He is the protector of the beasts of the moor, the birds, the hares, the wild creatures, and the enemy of hunters who kill more than they need or trespass on his ground.

The Brown Man stands among the oldest expressions of a folk idea that the wilderness has its own keeper, who exacts a price from those who abuse it.

The Warning at the Well

The classic account traces back to a letter the antiquary Robert Surtees sent Sir Walter Scott, citing as his source an old woman named Elizabeth Cockburn. She told of two young men from Newcastle who, sporting on the high moors above Elsdon the year before the Great Rebellion, sat down to rest by a stream. One of them saw the Brown Man appear and heard him warn them angrily off his ground, offering nothing but the moor's own wild fruit as fit food for a hungry man. That youth was minded to obey, but his bolder companion scoffed, went on killing game regardless, and was dead within the year, a fate the story lays squarely at the Brown Man's door.

The tale was first printed in Moses Aaron Richardson's Local Historian's Table Book of Remarkable Occurrences in 1843 and repeated by William Henderson in his Notes on the Folk-Lore of the Northern Counties in 1866 and 1879.

Brownie

A household spirit that works by night in exchange for food, respect, and privacy. Offer clothes or insult its labour and the brownie may vanish, or become something much less helpful.

The Legend

Scotland and the north of England cherished the Brownie as their household spirit, a small, rough, brown-clad and shaggy figure who attaches himself to a farm or house and, by night while the family sleeps, labours at the work left undone: threshing, reaping, churning, cleaning and tending the beasts. Tradition credited him with prodigious strength for the task, said to thresh as much grain in a single night as twenty men could manage between them. He asks no wage, only a bowl of cream or a fresh bannock left quietly by the hearth, and he is fiercely loyal to a household that treats him with respect.

The Work Done by Night

But the Brownie was easily offended. To leave him formal payment, and above all a gift of clothes, was the surest way to drive him off for good, and many a tale ends with the spirit vanishing in a huff at the offer of a coat. Mistreated or mocked, he could turn troublesome, souring into a destructive boggart. The Reverend Robert Kirk, minister of Aberfoyle, described brownies and their kin in his 1691 manuscript The Secret Commonwealth of Elves, Fauns and Fairies as part of an ancient, 'abstruse' people living alongside humankind unseen, visible only to those with the second sight.

Recorded across the Borders and Highlands, and remembered in tales such as the Brownie of Bodsbeck, he is the gentlest and most domestic of British fairies, the very spirit of the well-kept home.

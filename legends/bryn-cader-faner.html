Bryn Cader Faner

Jagged stone pillars jut from a moorland cairn like a dark crown — a Bronze Age burial above Talsarnau whose Welsh name means ‘the hill of the throne with the flag,’ hinting at forgotten power and prestige.

The Legend

High on the remote hills of Ardudwy in Gwynedd, Bryn Cader Faner rises from the heather like something not quite of this world. Its fifteen remaining stone slabs — each roughly two metres long — lean outward from a low circular mound at dramatic angles, earning the monument its popular nickname: the Crown of Thorns. The Welsh name translates approximately as ‘the hill crowned with the throne of the flag,’ a title suggesting this was once a place of considerable ritual or political significance, perhaps marking the burial of a chieftain whose authority demanded visible commemoration in the landscape.

The Story

The cairn’s centre shows clear evidence of disturbance. Nineteenth-century treasure hunters dug into its heart, leaving a depression where a cist or grave once held its contents. Whatever offerings or remains accompanied the original burial were lost to these early antiquarians, who rifled the site long before any systematic archaeology could record what they found. The damage continued in the twentieth century when, before the Second World War, the British Army used the cairn for gunnery practice, destroying stones on its eastern side and reducing the original complement of perhaps thirty pillars to the eighteen that survived.

Despite these violations, Bryn Cader Faner retains a power that draws visitors across difficult terrain to reach it. The monument stands in a landscape dense with prehistoric remains — stone circles, standing stones, and cairns dot the surrounding hills — suggesting that this stretch of upland Wales was deeply sacred to Bronze Age communities. In recent years, Buddhist pilgrims have conducted rituals at the site, recognising in its ancient stones a spiritual resonance that transcends any single tradition.

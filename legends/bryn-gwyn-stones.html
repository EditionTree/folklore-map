Bryn Gwyn stones

The tallest standing stones in Wales (nearly fourteen feet of imported chloritic schist, framing Snowdon across the Menai Strait) were once part of a lost Neolithic circle destroyed by treasure-seekers.

The Legend

The Bryn Gwyn stones rise from a field boundary in Brynsiencyn on Anglesey, the taller blade-stone reaching almost fourteen feet high and ten feet across, its companion standing over ten feet. Both are composed of chloritic schist, a stone not native to the island, which means they were deliberately transported here during the Neolithic period, hauling stone that heavy across open country without wheels or draft animals.

The tallest standing stones in Wales still command the view toward Snowdonia.

The Stones That Frame Snowdon

Historical records tell of a larger monument now lost. In the seventeenth century, writers described twelve stones standing in a circle; by the early 1700s, Reverend Henry Rowlands recorded only three stones and the base of a fourth. An account of 1797 explains the destruction: 'ignorant country people supposing money was hid under them tore them up.' The quest for buried treasure beneath standing stones is a recurring motif in Welsh and British folklore, and at Bryn Gwyn it consumed all but the two survivors, which were later incorporated into the gable ends of a cottage.

Despite their scale (the tallest standing stones in all of Wales), modern archaeology has found no evidence of their original purpose. Their proximity to Castell Bryngwyn, which has yielded Neolithic, Iron Age, and Roman-era artefacts, suggests the stones belonged to a broader ceremonial landscape. They frame a view of Snowdon across the Menai Strait, a sightline that may or may not have been intentional but lends them an enduring dramatic presence.

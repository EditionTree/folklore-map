Bucca

A Cornish spirit of the sea, mines, and weather, sometimes treated as a hobgoblin and sometimes as something older and darker. Fisherfolk left offerings to keep Bucca friendly.

The Legend

Bucca is an ancient and ambiguous spirit of Cornish folklore, a being of the sea, the weather and the tin-mines whose nature shades from a helpful sprite to something far older and darker — a figure some have argued was once a sea-god of the old Cornish, demoted over time into a hobgoblin. Among the fishing communities of west Cornwall, especially around Newlyn and Mousehole, Bucca was a power of the sea and storm whom the fisherfolk were careful to keep on side, leaving a portion of the catch on the sand, or a few crumbs of bread and a splash of beer, as an offering to ensure good fishing and safe weather.

The Otherworld at the Heart of the Tale

Tradition came to distinguish two Buccas: Bucca Gwidder, the white or good Bucca, and Bucca Dhu, the black or malevolent Bucca, a division that hints at the splitting of an older, more powerful being into bright and dark aspects under Christian influence. In the mines, like the related Knockers, a bucca might be heard in the workings, while on the coast he was blamed for storms and wrecks and credited with control over the winds.

The very word survives in the Cornish dialect insult 'bucca-boo' and stands behind the wider family of 'bogey' words for a frightening spirit. Bucca is one of the clearest cases in British folklore of a deity dwindling into a domestic and coastal sprite — a once-great power of sea and weather still owed his small offerings on the Cornish shore long after his godhood was forgotten.

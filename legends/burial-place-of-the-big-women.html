Burial Place of the Big Women

A farm shed built from the robbed stones of an ancient women's graveyard on Tiree left a servant hearing faint voices repeat: "This is the stone that was at my head."

The Legend

At Hynish on the Isle of Tiree stood a disused burial ground known in Gaelic as Cladh nam Ban Mòra, the Burial Place of the Big Women, likely once linked to a chapel and, by local tradition, associated with a community of religious women. By the nineteenth century the graveyard had long fallen out of use, and its stones were considered fair game for building material: some were taken to construct an outbuilding on the Hynish farm.

The Story

The ghost story attached to that reuse was recorded by the folklorist Rev. John Gregorson Campbell in his 1902 collection Witchcraft and Second Sight in the Highlands and Islands of Scotland. A servant from the neighbouring island of Mull, sent to sleep in the newly built shed, found his dog would not settle, repeatedly leaping onto the bed and snarling at something unseen in the dark. Through the night the servant heard faint voices around the building, each repeating the same complaint: "This is the stone that was at my head." He refused ever to sleep there again, convinced the graveyard's disturbed dead had followed their headstones into the new building.

Gregorson Campbell's account allows that the servant may have been the victim of a hoax, but records the story as it was told and believed locally. The scholar Ronald Black later discussed the tale in his annotated edition The Gaelic Otherworld, situating it within a wider Hebridean tradition of "big women" place-names, which range from this specific haunted graveyard at Hynish to a broader, older strand of legend on the nearby Isle of Eigg (whose Gaelic name likewise translates as the Island of the Big Women) about a vanished race of giant women. The Hynish story is the more concrete and place-anchored of the two: a named graveyard, a named building, and a specific, quotable haunting rather than a general legendary ancestry.

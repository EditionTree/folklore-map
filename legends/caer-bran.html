Caer Bran

An Iron Age hillfort in Penwith named for Bran the Blessed, the giant raven-king of Welsh myth, and long reputed as a sanctuary from evil spirits and home of the Pobel Vean — Cornwall’s fairy folk.

The Legend

Caer Bran crowns a hilltop in the heart of the Penwith peninsula, commanding views of both coastline and countryside from its position near Land's End. The hillfort enfolds three earlier Bronze Age ring cairns within its banks, representing perhaps two thousand years of ritual use at this single elevated site. Its outermost rampart still stands up to four metres high, fronted by a broad ditch, enclosing a space some sixty metres across.

The hillfort's name ties Penwith earthworks to the giant raven-king of Welsh myth.

The Fort of Bran

The name Caer Bran links the hillfort to one of Celtic mythology's most resonant figures: Bendigeidfran, or Bran the Blessed, the giant king of Britain who appears in the second branch of the Mabinogion. Scholar Helaine Newstead traced the etymology through the nearby hamlet of Brane (a corruption of Boswran, possibly meaning 'house of Bran'), making Caer Bran 'the castle near the house of Bran.' In Welsh tradition, Bran's severed head was buried in London to protect Britain from invasion, and his name simply means 'raven' or 'crow.' A related legend attaches to the standing stone Mên Scryfa, several miles north, which commemorates Rialobran ('Royal Bran'), a prince said to have died reclaiming his father's usurped castle.

In Cornish folk belief, Caer Bran was considered a sanctuary from evil spirits and the dwelling place of the Pobel Vean, the 'little people' of Cornish fairy tradition. The surrounding landscape is threaded with fogous, holy wells, and megaliths, each carrying its own cargo of legend, making the hillfort the centrepiece of one of Cornwall's most densely mythologised stretches of countryside.

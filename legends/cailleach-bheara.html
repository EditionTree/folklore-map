Cailleach Bhéara

KAL-yakh VAY-ra

The ancient hag of the Beara Peninsula, one of the oldest figures in Irish and Scottish mythology. She has lived through seven human lifetimes, each renewed by marrying a young husband who aged and died. She shaped the mountains, drove her cattle across the sky as clouds, and now sits as stone at the tip of the peninsula, waiting.

The Legend

The Cailleach Bhéarra, the Hag of Beara, is the divine hag of the Beara Peninsula in south-west Ireland, a goddess of the land, of winter and of sovereignty, kin to the Scottish Cailleach. She is said to have lived seven lifetimes and outlasted seven husbands, watching generations and even mountains rise and pass away, and is credited with shaping the country itself, letting stones fall from her apron to raise hills and cairns.

The Divine Presence

Her most celebrated expression is the medieval poem 'Aithbe damsa bés mora', known in English as 'The Lament of the Old Woman of Beare', composed in Old Irish around the ninth or tenth century and preserved in the twelfth-century Book of Leinster. In it the once-young queen, now aged and newly Christian, mourns her lost beauty and the passing of the old pagan world, one of the great poems in the Irish language.

Her reach extends well beyond the peninsula that bears her name. At Slieve Gullion in County Armagh, folklore places her inside the passage tomb crowning the summit, built between roughly 3500 and 2900 BC and aligned to catch the last light of the midwinter sunset. In 'The Hunt of Slieve Cuilinn', the Cailleach lures Fionn mac Cumhaill to the mountain's lake disguised as a weeping woman who has lost a golden ring in the water. When he dives in to retrieve it for her she vanishes, leaving him aged and grey. Only after the Fianna break open her tomb and force an antidote from her does his youth return, though his hair never regains its colour.

On the Beara Peninsula itself, a weathered rock above the sea, An Chailleach, is said to be the Hag turned to stone, still gazing out across the water as she waits for her return.

Historical Context

Origins & Dating

Historical PeriodEarly Medieval Ireland (poem composition, c.9th-10th century); the Cailleach figure herself is understood by scholars as a much older pre-Christian goddess-of-the-land archetype, with the poem representing her Christianised literary treatment
Earliest Written Record'Aithbe damsa bés mora' ('The Lament of the Old Woman of Beare'), an Old Irish poem composed c. late 9th-10th century; the earliest surviving manuscript witness is the Book of Leinster (c.1160), with four further later manuscripts

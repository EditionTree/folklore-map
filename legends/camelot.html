Camelot

Britain's most enduring legendary fortress — identified by centuries of local tradition with South Cadbury hillfort in Somerset, where a great Dark Age feasting hall was rebuilt at exactly the period Arthur is said to have lived, and where the lane at the hill's foot is still named Arthur's Lane.

A visual interpretation of Camelot as a post-Roman hillfort at South Cadbury
The Legend

Camelot is the legendary court of King Arthur, the centre of the Round Table fellowship, and the seat from which Arthur ruled Britain. The name appears first in Chrétien de Troyes's twelfth-century French romance Lancelot, le Chevalier de la Charrette, and became firmly embedded in the tradition through Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur (1485), where it is Arthur's principal residence.

The legendary seat of Arthur's kingdom is most often identified with South Cadbury hillfort in Somerset.

The Kingdom on the Hill

Of the many proposed locations for Camelot, the strongest local tradition in England attaches it to South Cadbury hillfort in Somerset. John Leland, the royal antiquary, wrote in the 1540s that 'at the very south ende of the church of South-Cadbyri standith Camallate, sumtyme a famose toun or castelle … the people can tell nothing thar but that they have hard say that Arture much resortid to Camalat.' He recorded that local people called the hill Camelot and dug up coins and horseshoes on its summit. When John Camden visited in the late sixteenth century the tradition was already established. A lane running below the north side of the hill is still called Arthur's Lane.

The legend received dramatic archaeological support from the Camelot Research Committee's excavations of 1966–1970, led by Leslie Alcock. On the summit of South Cadbury they found evidence of massive post-Roman refortification — a great feasting hall and a new circuit of timber-and-stone ramparts — reliably dated to around 470–500 AD, exactly the period in which Arthur is placed by the ninth-century Welsh text Historia Brittonum. The site also yielded imported Byzantine pottery of the type found at several important sub-Roman power centres. While no inscription named Arthur, the coincidence of a major feasting hall refortified in the Arthurian period at a place locally called Camelot since at least the sixteenth century made the identification impossible to dismiss.

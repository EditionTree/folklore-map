Canute and the Tide

King Cnut is said to have had his throne carried to the shore and commanded the incoming tide to halt, to prove a point to flattering courtiers who claimed he could command even the sea. When the water soaked his feet regardless, he told them that the power of kings is empty compared to God's. The earliest surviving account is in Henry of Huntingdon's twelfth century Historia Anglorum, which sets it near the sea without naming a single fixed spot.

The Legend

Henry of Huntingdon, writing in the 1130s, tells the story as one of three proofs of Cnut's humility rather than his arrogance, a point often reversed in later retelling. According to Huntingdon, Cnut set his chair at the water's edge as the tide came in and commanded it, saying that the land he sat on was his and no one had ever resisted his orders unpunished. The sea rose over his feet and legs regardless, and Cnut leapt back, declaring that all the world's inhabitants should know how empty and worthless royal power is, since no king deserves the name except Him whose will heaven, earth and sea obey. He is then said to have hung his crown on a crucifix and never worn it again.

The Story

Henry of Huntingdon gives no exact location. Several English places later claimed the event for themselves, most persistently Bosham on the Sussex coast, where Cnut is known to have held land and where a tradition (separately attested, concerning a royal infant's grave found under the chancel arch of Holy Trinity Church) already tied his family to the village, and Southampton, where a monument on the waterfront commemorates the story with a date of 1028. No source earlier than Huntingdon survives, and the contemporary Encomium Emmae Reginae, commissioned in Cnut's own lifetime, does not mention the episode at all.

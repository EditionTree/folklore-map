Carrownlisheen Wedge Tomb

A 6,000-year-old tomb on windswept Inishmaan capped by a ten-ton stone — known as the Bed of Diarmuid and Gráinne, where the doomed lovers are said to have spent their last night together.

The Legend

The Carrownlisheen Wedge Tomb stands on the eastern lowlands of Inis Meáin, the quietest of the three Aran Islands in Galway Bay. Dating to around 4000 BC, this single-chambered tomb measures 3.5 metres long and 2 metres wide, its vertical stone slabs bearing a massive capstone weighing approximately ten tons. When R.A.S. Macalister excavated the site in 1934, he found fragments of pottery and human bones inside — tangible evidence of the rituals once performed here.

Local tradition names the tomb as the Bed of Diarmuid and Gráinne.

The Lovers' Last Bed

Locally, the tomb is known as Leaba Dhiarmada agus Ghráinne — the Bed of Diarmuid and Gráinne. In Irish mythology, Diarmuid and Gráinne eloped from the ageing warrior Fionn mac Cumhaill, fleeing across Ireland and sheltering at megalithic sites along the way. Some versions of the tale claim Carrownlisheen as the place where they spent their last night together before Diarmuid was killed by a magical boar sent by Fionn.

The tomb was designated a National Monument in 1937. On an island where limestone karst and ancient walls define every horizon, it sits within a concentrated landscape of prehistoric remains that speak to Inishmaan’s importance as a sacred site long before the early Christian monks arrived.

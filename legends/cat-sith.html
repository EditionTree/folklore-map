Cat Sìth

kat SHEE

A great spectral black cat with a white spot on its breast, the Cat Sìth of the Highlands was feared as a fairy or a witch in disguise, and could steal the soul of a corpse before burial.

The Legend

The Cat Sìth, the 'fairy cat', is a creature of Scottish Highland and Gaelic folklore, a large black cat the size of a dog with a single white spot upon its breast, seen slipping through the night with its back arched and fur bristling. Some held it to be a fairy; others believed it was a witch who could take cat-form nine times, but who would be trapped in the shape forever if she changed a ninth time.

The Cat at the Threshold

The Cat Sìth was most feared around death. It was believed that the fairy cat would try to steal the soul of a corpse before it was buried, by passing over the body, and so the Highlanders kept the 'Late Wake' or Fèill Fhadalach, watching the dead night and day, distracting the cat with games, riddles and music, and keeping no fire in the room, since warmth was thought to draw it. At Samhain a saucer of milk left out would win a household the Cat Sìth's blessing, while those who offered none would find their cows dry.

The creature is documented across several generations of Highland folklore collectors. J. F. Campbell gave an early printed account in 'Popular Tales of the West Highlands' (1860-62), John Gregorson Campbell added fuller detail in his 'Superstitions of the Highlands and Islands of Scotland' (1900), and the folklorist Alexander Carmichael recorded a specific protective charm against 'the black cat of the mound', taken down from a crofter in Benbecula around 1875. The creature is often linked to the King of the Cats tales told across the Highlands.

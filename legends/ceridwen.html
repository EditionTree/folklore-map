Ceridwen

keh-RID-wen

The Welsh enchantress who brewed the cauldron of inspiration and knowledge, Awen. When her servant Gwion Bach accidentally tasted three drops, she pursued him through a cycle of transformations before swallowing him whole and giving birth to the great poet Taliesin.

The Legend

Ceridwen is the great enchantress of Welsh legend, a sorceress of Penllyn dwelling beside Llyn Tegid (Bala Lake) whose story survives in the medieval Tale of Taliesin. She had a son, Morfran (also called Afagddu), so monstrously ugly that she resolved to compensate him with wisdom, and set about brewing a magical draught in her great cauldron, a potion of inspiration and knowledge the Welsh called Awen. It had to be boiled for a year and a day, and only the first three drops would carry the gift; all the rest was deadly poison.

When Gwion Bach accidentally tasted three drops, Ceridwen pursued him through a cycle of transformations.

The Three Drops of Inspiration

She set a blind man to tend the fire and a boy, Gwion Bach, to stir the cauldron. But when the year was almost up, three scalding drops leapt out onto Gwion's thumb, and sucking it to ease the burn he swallowed the very essence meant for Morfran. In that instant the boy knew all things, including that Ceridwen's fury would fall upon him, and he fled. What followed is one of the most vivid shape-shifting chases in Celtic myth: Gwion became a hare and Ceridwen a greyhound; he a fish and she an otter; he a bird and she a hawk. At last he turned into a single grain of wheat, and she, becoming a black hen, scratched through the heap and swallowed him whole.

Made pregnant by the grain, Ceridwen bore the child nine months later, and finding him too beautiful to kill, she sewed him into a leather bag and cast him into the sea. He was drawn from the water by a prince and grew into Taliesin, the greatest of the early Welsh poets, so that Ceridwen's cauldron, brewed for one son, in the end gave Wales its bard. She endures as a potent emblem of poetic inspiration, transformation and the dangerous, generative magic of the cauldron.

Historical Context

Origins & Dating

Historical PeriodEarly Modern Wales (earliest complete surviving text); the tradition itself is judged considerably older
Earliest Written RecordElis Gruffydd's mid-16th-century 'Ystoria Taliesin' (part of his Chronicle of the Six Ages of the World, National Library of Wales MS 5276) is the earliest complete surviving text of the Ceridwen/Taliesin tale, though Gruffydd's own account points to earlier sources, possibly as old as the 9th century. Some of the 56 poems in the related, older 14th-century Book of Taliesin are considered by scholars to originate in the 10th century or earlier

Folklore dates are often approximate, especially where a story predates any surviving written source.

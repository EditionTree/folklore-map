Cermand Cestach, the Oracle of Clogher

A gold-sheathed idol once stood on this hill, consulted like a Delphic oracle by pagan Ulster kings, until a devil's voice was said to speak from the stone itself.

The Legend

Long before Clogher had a cathedral, tradition holds that a gold-covered cult stone called Cermand Cestach (also recorded as Cermand Celstach or Kermand Kelstach) stood on the hill as the oracle of Ulster, consulted through the druids much as Greek petitioners consulted the oracle at Delphi. The place-name itself preserves the memory: Clogher derives from Cloch-Óir, "the golden stone." The earliest reference to the idol appears in the medieval Life of St Macartan, Clogher's founding bishop (c.430-505 AD), which describes it as a sacred stone given to the saint by an elderly pagan nobleman when the old religion gave way to the new.

The Story

An eighth-century gloss on the story is blunter about what the stone represented, recording that "there had once been a stone covered in gold which heathens worshipped: and out of it a devil used to speak", casting the oracle not as a benign wonder but as a mouthpiece for a pre-Christian spirit that Macartan's mission had to silence. According to legend it was consulted by King Conchobar of Ulster himself when he sought guidance on securing his kingship.

What makes the tradition unusually well anchored is that the stone was said to survive as a physical relic long after its oracular days: the Ulster annalist Cathal Óg Mac Maghnusa (Cathal Maguire), who died in 1498, recorded that the idol was still preserved as a curiosity in the porch of Clogher Cathedral in his own lifetime, and a stone answering that description is still kept inside the cathedral today, one of the few places in Ireland where an object from a documented pre-Christian cult is said to have survived, more or less continuously, into the modern church that replaced it.

Cernunnos

ker-NUN-oss

The antlered Celtic god associated with wild nature, animals, fertility, and the deep green world. His image appears on ancient objects, a horned figure seated among beasts and mystery.

The Legend

Cernunnos is the antlered god of the ancient Celts, a deity of wild nature, beasts, fertility and abundance whose image is far better attested than his myth. He is depicted as a mature, often cross-legged figure crowned with the antlers of a stag, frequently holding or wearing the torc, the sacred neck-ring of Celtic nobility, and accompanied by animals, above all the stag and the ram-horned serpent, a strange composite creature that recurs in his iconography. The name itself, 'the horned one', survives on only a single inscription, from Paris, but images of the horned god appear across the Celtic world, including in Britain.

He is depicted as a mature figure crowned with the antlers of a stag.

The Horned God Among Beasts

As a horned lord seated in mastery among the creatures of the forest, Cernunnos seems to embody the meeting of the human and animal worlds, the generative power of wild life, and the wealth that flows from it — the torcs and coins shown spilling from his presence on some monuments suggest a god of plenty as well as of beasts. The most famous depiction, on the great silver Gundestrup cauldron, shows him antlered and cross-legged, grasping a torc and a horned serpent, surrounded by animals.

Because almost no narrative about him survives, Cernunnos remains a figure of suggestion and mystery, reconstructed from sculpture and symbol rather than story. Modern Pagan and folkloric traditions have embraced him as the archetypal Horned God of the greenwood, and he is often linked in the popular imagination with later figures of the wild, the Green Man, Herne the Hunter, and the leaf-crowned spirits of the British woods.

Historical Context

Origins & Dating

Approximate Origin14-37 AD (named inscription); iconographic antlered-figure depictions possibly as early as c. 400 BC
Historical PeriodRoman Gaul (named attestation); presumed pre-Roman Iron Age in iconographic origin
Earliest Written RecordThe Pillar of the Boatmen, erected in Lutetia (Roman Paris) by the Parisii boatmen's guild between 14-37 AD, is the only surviving inscription naming 'Cernunnos' — rediscovered in 1710 during work beneath Notre-Dame de Paris. Earlier, uninscribed antlered-figure iconography appears in Iron Age rock carvings at Val Camonica, Italy (from c. 400 BC) and on the 1st-century BC Gundestrup Cauldron, though these can't be certainly linked to this specific name

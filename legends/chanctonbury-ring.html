Chanctonbury Ring

An Iron Age hillfort ringed with beech trees on the South Downs. Walk seven times around it at midnight and the Devil will appear with a bowl of soup. Roman offerings were found buried within. The trees were blown down in 1987 — and something changed.

The Legend

Chanctonbury Ring is a prehistoric hillfort high on the South Downs of West Sussex, made unmistakable by the ring of beech trees planted within its ramparts in 1760 by Charles Goring. Beneath the trees lie far older remains — Iron Age earthworks and the foundations of Romano-British temples, with Roman votive offerings found buried inside — and the brooding crown of beeches has made it one of the most legend-haunted spots in southern England.

Walk the Ring seven times at midnight, tradition says, and the Devil will appear.

Seven Times at Midnight

The best-known tradition concerns the Devil. It is said that if you run (or walk backwards) seven times around the Ring at midnight — in some versions on Midsummer Eve or Halloween — the Devil himself will appear and offer you a bowl of soup, porridge or milk, in exchange for your soul. A related belief holds that the trees of the Ring can never be accurately counted. The place gathered a dark reputation for ghosts and apparitions, from a phantom white-bearded treasure-seeker to spectral riders, and in the 20th century it became notorious in accounts of the uncanny, including reports of strange experiences among those who dared to sleep there.

The Ring's mood changed dramatically in the Great Storm of October 1987, when the hurricane-force winds tore down most of the ancient beeches in a single night. The clump has since been replanted and is slowly regrowing, but to many who knew it the old enchantment of the place was bound up with those vanished trees — and the legends of the Devil's circuit endure on the bare, wind-scoured down.

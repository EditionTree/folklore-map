Changeling

A fairy substitute left in place of a stolen human child. Across Britain and Ireland, changelings explained sudden illness, unusual behaviour, or children believed to have been taken by the Fair Folk. Welsh traditions particularly associate them with the Tylwyth Teg.

The Legend

The changeling is among the most poignant and disturbing beliefs of British and Irish fairy tradition: the notion that the fairies covet healthy human children and will steal one away, leaving in the cradle a substitute, a fairy, a stock of wood, or a withered, ancient fairy made to look like the child. The 'changeling' was known by its alteration: a baby that had been thriving would suddenly sicken, wail ceaselessly, fail to grow, or take on a wizened, knowing look.

The fairies would steal a human child and leave a substitute in the cradle.

The Substitute in the Cradle

The belief is old and widely recorded. The Middle English text 'Hali Meiðhad', written between about 1190 and 1220, uses the term 'cangun' for a substituted fairy child, one of the earliest documented references anywhere; William of Auvergne, Bishop of Paris from 1228 to 1249, separately described exchanged 'incubus children' who failed to thrive; and the reformer Martin Luther recorded a specific claimed changeling sighting at Dessau in 1532, showing the belief crossed both centuries and confessional lines.

The belief gave a frame to the otherwise inexplicable, the sudden failure of an infant, congenital illness, developmental difference, and, terribly, it licensed cruelty, for the recommended ways to force the fairies to return the true child were harsh: leaving the changeling on the dunghill, holding it over the fire, or making it speak and so betray its great age. A common test was to brew or cook in eggshells, provoking the creature to exclaim that it had never seen the like in all its long years. The changeling tradition persisted with tragic real-world consequences into the nineteenth century.

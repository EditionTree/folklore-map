Cheesewring

A strange granite tor on Bodmin Moor, said in local legend to have been formed during a stone-throwing contest between a giant and a saint. The impossible stack looks less built than argued into being.

The Legend

The Cheesewring is a dramatic natural tor on Bodmin Moor in Cornwall, a precarious stack of great granite slabs, broad above and narrow below, that looks so improbable it seems impossible it was not built or balanced by some deliberate hand. Its name comes from its resemblance to a 'cheesewring', the old apparatus used to press cheese, but Cornish legend gives the strange pile a far better origin — a contest between a giant and a saint.

The balanced stones were read as the frozen record of a contest between old powers and new.

The Saint and the Giant

In the tale, the giants of the moor were affronted by the coming of the Christian saints and the loss of their old dominion, and one great giant, Uther, challenged a saint, usually named as St Tue (Saint Tew), to a trial of strength in rock-throwing, the loser to submit to the other's faith. The two hurled great stones, piling them up, and the contest seemed to favour the giant by sheer brawn until the saint, aided by an angel or by divine power, was able to cast or catch the heaviest rocks with miraculous ease, building the impossible balanced stack that is the Cheesewring and winning the day for the new faith.

The Cheesewring is also linked to other moorland lore, a golden goblet said to have been offered to a thirsty druid or hidden in the rocks, and the general belief that Bodmin Moor's tors and stone-piles were the work or the playthings of giants. As a place where an extraordinary natural form is explained by a saint-and-giant legend, the Cheesewring is a classic of Cornish folklore, the landscape itself read as the frozen record of a contest between the old powers and the new.

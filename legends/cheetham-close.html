Cheetham Close

A Bronze Age stone circle on the Lancashire moors, once imagined as a druidical temple where bards performed rites — its destruction sealed by a Victorian farmer who sledgehammered the stones to keep visitors away.

The Legend

Cheetham Close sits at around 1,060 feet on exposed moorland near Turton, on the old boundary between Lancashire and Greater Manchester. The stone circle originally comprised eleven uprights arranged in a ring roughly fifteen metres across, with a substantial ring-bank cairn nearby, a second, larger enclosure measuring twenty-four yards across with carefully revetted walls four feet thick. Together, the complex testifies to sustained Bronze Age ceremonial activity in these Pennine uplands.

Antiquarians imagined rites and bards here before the stones were shattered.

The Broken Temple

Nineteenth-century antiquarians saw the site through a romantic lens. One writer imagined 'bards in their different orders' conducting 'rites and mysteries' before assembled crowds, and the site was characterised as a 'bardic temple.' A Roman road passed within two hundred yards, prompting speculation that the monument was already ancient when Rome came through. Archaeological finds, including a Bronze Age saddle quern, a British copper axe-head discovered around 1819, and a Celtic bronze tool, lent substance to the antiquarians' sense of deep time, even if their druidical interpretations owed more to imagination than evidence.

The circle's destruction came not from time but from deliberate vandalism. A farmer employed by the nineteenth-century landowner James Kay sledgehammered the stones 'to prevent people visiting the situation.' By 1871 two stones had been broken almost to fragments; a 1983 survey found only two of the original seven megaliths still in place.

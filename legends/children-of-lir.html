Children of Lir

Lir's four children — Fionnuala, Aodh, Conn, and Fiachra — were turned into swans by their jealous stepmother Aoife and spent nine hundred years on Irish and Scottish waters, their voices so heartbreakingly beautiful that all who heard them forgot every grief — until a monk's bell finally broke the curse at the very moment of their death.

A visual interpretation of the Children of Lir on the western Irish coast
The Legend

The Tragedy of the Children of Lir (Oidheadh Chlainne Lir) is one of the Three Sorrows of Storytelling in Irish mythology, and arguably the most emotionally sustained of the group. Lir was a chieftain of the Tuatha Dé Danann; his first wife Aoibh bore him four children before dying. He married her sister Aoife, who grew consumed with jealousy of the children's closeness to their father. Taking them to Lake Derravaragh in Westmeath, she struck them with a druid's wand and transformed them into four white swans, sentenced to spend three hundred years on Lough Derravaragh, three hundred on the Sea of Moyle between Ireland and Scotland, and three hundred on the Atlantic off Erris in Mayo.

Their singing was so beautiful that all who heard it forgot their sorrows.

The Long Exile on the Water

The curse preserved their human minds, emotions, and voices within the swans' bodies. It was their singing that became legendary: when they settled on the waters of Lough Derravaragh, they sang so sweetly that the entire Tuatha Dé Danann came from across Ireland to camp on the shore and listen. Warriors wept, enemies made peace, and the ordinary sorrows of the world dissolved. During the bitter second period on the Sea of Moyle, the four swans endured storms, ice, and violent separation; the oldest sister Fionnuala would warm her brothers under her wings. Their father died, the Tuatha Dé Danann entered their mounds, and the children of Lir were left alone in a world that had moved on.

The transformation was broken when the bells of a Christian monk dissolved the enchantment. The swans returned to human form as ancient, dying men and woman, baptised by the monk Mochaomhóg before they died. The tale is first attested in full in fourteenth-century manuscripts but draws on much older material; it is documented in the work of Jeremiah Curtin, Patrick Weston Joyce, and forms a central text of the Irish Folklore Commission archive. The Children of Lir have become an Irish national symbol — four swans appear on the old Irish fifty-pence coin and form part of the Fairy Chess Institute's emblem for Irish cultural heritage.

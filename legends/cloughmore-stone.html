Giants Rostrevor, County Down, Northern Ireland

Cloughmore Stone

A 50-tonne boulder Finn McCool supposedly hurled clean across Carlingford Lough at a rival giant, gouging a 'wishing chair' where it landed above Rostrevor.

The Legend

High on the slopes of Slieve Martin, nearly a thousand feet above the County Down village of Rostrevor, sits Cloughmore, from the Irish An Chloch Mhór, 'the big stone'. Geologists identify it as a glacial erratic, a fifty-tonne block of granite carried from an island off the Scottish coast and dropped here some ten thousand years ago as the last ice age retreated. Local tradition tells a different story entirely.

The Story

Fionn mac Cumhaill's rival in the tale is named Ruscaire, the Giant of Snow and Ice, sworn enemy of the whole human race. Their feud began, the story goes, when Ruscaire crept up on Fionn as he slept and stole his sword. Furious, Fionn gave chase, and the two fought first with swords, then with clubs, before the battle escalated into a running exchange of boulders hurled across Carlingford Lough that shook the mountains on both sides. Fionn's final throw, the fifty-tonne stone still standing on Slieve Martin today, struck Ruscaire's head and crushed him back into the mountainside, where his body is said to have melted away like the ice he was named for. The effort finished Fionn too: he collapsed where he stood and never woke again, and his own body is said to have turned to stone in turn, its outline still traced today in the profile of Slieve Foy across the water in County Louth, head and face on the peak, body stretching down toward the shore.

The stone's landing on Slieve Martin is said to have carved out a hollow at its base known as Finn's Wishing Chair, and a persistent local custom holds that circling the boulder seven times will bring good luck to the walker who does it. The site now sits within Kilbroney Park and forms part of a National Nature Reserve, drawing hikers up the Cloughmore Stone trail for the view over Carlingford Lough and the Cooley Peninsula as much as for the folklore, a rare case of a scientifically explained landscape feature that has kept its giant's-throw legend fully alive alongside the geology.

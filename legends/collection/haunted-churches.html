Haunted Churches & Churchyards

Consecrated ground draws its own folklore: the grim that guards the first grave, the phantom of a wronged priest, bells that toll for the drowned, and devils held back at the threshold. These traditions cluster where the parish church met older beliefs about the dead, the boundary and what walks after dark.

Churches and their yards sit where the living and the dead meet, and folklore populates them accordingly. The best-attested of these traditions is the church grim: the belief that a newly consecrated churchyard needed a guardian, so a black dog (sometimes a lamb, boar or horse) was buried alive on the north side rather than force the duty onto the first person interred there. The north side had its own reputation quite apart from the grim. Canon law and custom kept the unbaptised, the excommunicated and suicides there, and eighteenth-century writers such as Gilbert White noted parishioners' reluctance to be buried on ground long associated with shadow and the Devil. Drummer and phantom ghosts, corpse-lights and boundary hauntings sit alongside the grim tradition, preserving older beliefs about consecrated ground and the restless dead.

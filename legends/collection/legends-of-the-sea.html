Legends of the Sea & Shore

The coast and the deep water hold their own company — mermaids and selkies, drowned bells and sunken lands, the ghost ships and warning lights of the wrecking coasts. These are the stories of communities that lived by a sea that gave and took in equal measure, and of the shoreline as a threshold between worlds.

The coasts of Britain and Ireland gave rise to a distinctive body of maritime folklore — mermaids and selkies, sea monsters and phantom ships, storm-witches who sold fair winds, drowned bells and lost lands beneath the waves. The wind-selling tradition was real trade as much as story: sailors from Shetland to Cornwall bought a length of rope knotted by a local witch, said to release a favourable breeze with each knot untied, a practice recorded as still active in villages like Sennen and St Ives into the 1930s. Such tales carried the hopes and fears of communities whose livelihoods, and often their lives, depended on an unpredictable sea.

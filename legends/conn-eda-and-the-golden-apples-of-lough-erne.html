Conn-eda and the Golden Apples of Lough Erne

In a Fermanagh fairy tale first published in 1879, Prince Conn-eda dives beneath the waters of Lough Erne to the palace of the Firbolg king, sent by his scheming stepmother to fetch three golden apples, a black steed and a magic hound as a fatal geis.

The Legend

Conn-eda and the Golden Apples of Lough Erne was translated from the Irish by the antiquarian Nicholas O'Kearney and published in Volume 2 of The Folk-Lore Record in 1879, one of the founding academic journals of British and Irish folklore studies. Nine years later W. B. Yeats reprinted the tale in Fairy and Folk Tales of the Irish Peasantry (1888), carrying it to a far wider readership than the original journal could reach, and the manuscript O'Kearney worked from was later catalogued by the National Library of Australia. The story draws on an old layer of Irish mythology in which the waters of Lough Erne conceal the underground kingdom of the Firbolg, one of the peoples said to have settled Ireland before the Tuatha Dé Danann.

The Apples Below the Lake

The tale opens with Conn-eda, a prince beloved of his father's court, falling under the power of a jealous stepmother, herself a daughter of the Arch-Druid and a practitioner of magic. She imposes on him a geis, a binding obligation, requiring him to obtain within a year and a day three treasures guarded by the Firbolg king beneath the lake: three golden apples that grow in the palace garden, an each dubh, a supernatural black steed, and the cuileen con na mbuadh, a hound of supernatural powers. The task is designed to kill him, demanding that a mortal hero descend into the watery Otherworld and return alive.

Conn-eda succeeds with the guidance of a Druid, who gives him a protective apple sent by the ghost of his dead mother, and with the help of a magic horse won along the way. He passes through the Firbolg kingdom beneath Lough Erne, endures tests of strength and hospitality, and comes back with all three treasures intact; his stepmother's schemes are exposed once he returns. The same watery-otherworld pattern recurs across Ulster lake lore, most famously at Lough Neagh nearby, where local tradition likewise tells of a kingdom drowned beneath the surface.

The tale preserves a pre-Christian cosmology of Lough Erne as a liminal portal between the living and the subterranean Otherworld, a belief still echoed in the carved stone figures and island remains scattered across the real lake, and documented today by Enniskillen's own local heritage writers.

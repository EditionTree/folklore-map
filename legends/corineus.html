Corineus

The Trojan hero granted Cornwall as his kingdom after helping Brutus defeat the giants of Albion. He delighted in wrestling giants — his greatest feat was hurling Gogmagog from a Cornish cliff into the sea below. The cliff has been known ever since as Gogmagog's Leap.

The Legend

Corineus is the founding giant-slayer of Cornwall in the legendary history of Britain set down by Geoffrey of Monmouth. A Trojan warrior and companion of Brutus, the supposed first king of Britain, Corineus was famed above all for his delight in wrestling giants, and when the Trojans found the island of Albion held by a race of them, he relished the fight.

Having had three ribs broken, he heaved the giant up and hurled him from the heights.

The Wrestler of Giants

For his prowess he was granted the western land that took his name, Corineia (Cornwall), the corner of Britain where the giants were thickest. His greatest bout was with their champion, Gogmagog, a monster of huge stature: the two grappled on the cliffs above the sea near Plymouth until Corineus, having had three ribs broken, heaved the giant up and hurled him from the heights to be dashed on the rocks below, at a place long called 'Gogmagog's Leap'. Through Corineus the medieval British were given a heroic, Trojan pedigree and an explanation for the giant-haunted reputation of their westernmost county.

Origins & Dating

Approximate Origin1135-1139
Earliest Written RecordGeoffrey of Monmouth's 'Historia Regum Britanniae' (1135-39) is the earliest surviving developed account of Corineus, Brutus, and the Gogmagog wrestling match. The legend went largely unchallenged until Polydore Vergil (1534) and especially William Camden's 'Britannia' (1586) began the antiquarian critique of Geoffrey's British history

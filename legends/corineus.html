Corineus

The Trojan hero granted Cornwall as his kingdom after helping Brutus defeat the giants of Albion. He delighted in wrestling giants; his greatest feat was hurling Gogmagog from a Cornish cliff into the sea below. The cliff has been known ever since as Gogmagog's Leap.

The Legend

Geoffrey of Monmouth made Corineus the founding giant-slayer of Cornwall in his legendary history of Britain. A Trojan warrior and companion of Brutus, the supposed first king of Britain, Corineus was famed above all for his delight in wrestling giants, and when the Trojans found the island of Albion held by a race of them, he relished the fight.

Having had three ribs broken, he heaved the giant up and hurled him from the heights.

The Wrestler of Giants

For his prowess he was granted the western land that took his name, Corineia (Cornwall), the corner of Britain where the giants were thickest. His greatest bout was with their champion Gogmagog, a monster reckoned twelve cubits tall, some eighteen feet, strong enough to tear up an oak by the roots as easily as a man might pluck a hazel wand. The two grappled on the cliffs above the sea near Plymouth until Corineus, having had three ribs broken in the giant's grip, heaved Gogmagog onto his shoulders, carried him to the height of the rock, and hurled him into the waves below, at a place long remembered as Lamgoit, or Gogmagog's Leap.

Through Corineus the medieval British were given a heroic, Trojan pedigree and an explanation for the giant-haunted reputation of their westernmost county, a story that outlasted the antiquarian scepticism of writers like Polydore Vergil and William Camden by centuries.

Origins & Dating

Approximate Origin1135-1139
Historical PeriodHigh Medieval Britain
Earliest Written RecordGeoffrey of Monmouth's 'Historia Regum Britanniae' (1135-39) is the earliest surviving developed account of Corineus, Brutus, and the Gogmagog wrestling match. The legend went largely unchallenged until Polydore Vergil (1534) and especially William Camden's 'Britannia' (1586) began the antiquarian critique of Geoffrey's British history

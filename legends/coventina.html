Coventina

A Romano-British goddess of wells and springs, especially associated with Coventina's Well near Carrawburgh on Hadrian's Wall. Offerings to her were dropped into sacred water.

A visual interpretation of Coventina in Northumberland
The Legend

Coventina was a Romano-British goddess of wells and springs, whose worship is known above all from a single remarkable site: Coventina's Well at Carrawburgh (Brocolitia), a fort on Hadrian's Wall in Northumberland. There a walled basin enclosed a natural spring sacred to her, and into its waters generations of worshippers cast their offerings. When the well was excavated in the 19th century it yielded an extraordinary treasure — many thousands of coins, along with brooches, rings, glass, pottery, carved stones and other votive gifts, dropped into the spring as gifts to the goddess over the centuries of Roman occupation.

A Romano-British goddess of wells and springs, especially associated with Coventina's Well near Carrawburgh on Hadrian's Wall

The Divine Presence

She is shown on carved reliefs from the site as a water-nymph: a reclining female figure pouring water from a vessel, or three nymphs together, holding cups and water-plants — the imagery of a deity of flowing, life-giving and perhaps healing water. Inscriptions from the well name her and record the vows of her devotees, including soldiers of the Wall garrison drawn from across the Empire, showing how a local British spring-goddess gathered worshippers from many lands.

Coventina is one of the best examples of the intensely local water-cults of Roman Britain — deities tied not to a pantheon or a grand temple but to a single sacred spring, honoured by the simple, ancient act of casting an offering into the water. Her well at Carrawburgh, and the great hoard recovered from it, give an unusually vivid picture of personal religion at the very frontier of the Roman world.

Coventina

A Romano-British goddess of wells and springs, especially associated with Coventina's Well near Carrawburgh on Hadrian's Wall. Offerings to her were dropped into sacred water.

The Legend

Coventina was a Romano-British goddess of wells and springs, whose worship is known above all from a single remarkable site: Coventina's Well at Carrawburgh (Brocolitia), a fort on Hadrian's Wall in Northumberland. There a walled basin enclosed a natural spring sacred to her, and into its waters generations of worshippers cast their offerings. When the antiquarian John Clayton excavated the well in October 1876, it yielded an extraordinary treasure: more than twenty altars, roughly 16,000 coins, along with brooches, rings, glass, pottery, carved stones and other votive gifts, dropped into the spring as offerings to the goddess over centuries of Roman occupation.

The Divine Presence

She is shown on carved reliefs from the site as a water-nymph: a reclining female figure pouring water from a vessel, or three nymphs together, holding cups and water-plants, the imagery of a deity of flowing, life-giving and perhaps healing water. Inscriptions from the well name her and record the vows of her devotees, including soldiers of the Wall garrison drawn from across the Empire, showing how a local British spring-goddess gathered worshippers from many lands.

Coventina is one of the best examples of the intensely local water-cults of Roman Britain, deities tied not to a pantheon or a grand temple but to a single sacred spring, honoured by the simple, ancient act of casting an offering into the water. Worship at the well continued for some two centuries before it was ritually sealed and decommissioned around 388 AD, and the great hoard recovered from it nearly fifteen centuries later gives an unusually vivid picture of personal religion at the very frontier of the Roman world.

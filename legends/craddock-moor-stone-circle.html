Craddock Moor stone circle

Overgrown and reduced to sixteen fallen stones near Bodmin Moor's Hurlers, Craddock Moor may carry the name of Caradoc, a British king folded into Arthurian legend.

The Legend

Half a mile north-west of the better-known Hurlers, on Bodmin Moor near Minions, lies a stone circle far less visited and far harder to find. Craddock Moor's circle has fallen entirely: sixteen stones now lie flat in the moor grass, with perhaps one broken stump still upright, the whole site so overgrown that it ranks among the trickiest on the moor to locate.

The stones sit low in the heather near Minions, part of Bodmin Moor's ancient landscape.

The Low Ring on the Moor

Its name may preserve an older story than its ruined state suggests. Some antiquarians have argued that "Craddock" derives from Caradoc, a British king folded into the wider cycle of Arthurian legend, though no local tale about the circle itself survives to confirm the connection. Archaeologist John Barnatt has proposed a more measurable link to the landscape: the circle appears to have been positioned so that the summit of Brown Willy, Cornwall's highest point, lines up with the sunset at midsummer.

Unusually for a Bronze Age circle, every one of Craddock Moor's stones lies flat rather than a mix of standing and fallen, a state some read as evidence the monument was pulled down deliberately or never finished. The archaeologist Christopher Tilley has argued that the circle, a nearby stone row and an embanked enclosure form what he called a "possible axis of movement" running toward the Hurlers, an alignment he considered too consistent to be chance, even though none of the sites can actually be seen from any of the others.

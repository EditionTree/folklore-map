Cribarth, the Sleeping Giant

A mountain silhouette above the Tawe Valley is said to be a giant named Yscydion, lying asleep until Arthur returns and calls him to defend his people.

The Legend

Cribarth rises at the southern gateway to the Tawe Valley in the Brecon Beacons, and from the right angle its ridgeline reads unmistakably as a reclining human figure, head to one side, body sloping away down the hill; this has earned the area the local nickname 'the Land of the Sleeping Giant.' Welsh folklore gives the recumbent figure a name, Yscydion, and holds that he lies in enchanted sleep waiting to be roused when his people need him most, echoing the wider Welsh and British tradition of a sleeping hero-defender kept in reserve, the same role played by Arthur himself in numerous Welsh cave legends.

The Story

Both this giant and a second name attached to the same hill, Cribwr, trace to the same source: an account of Welsh giants and their dwellings compiled around 1600 and attributed to the Welsh scholar Siôn Dafydd Rhys, one of the earliest attempts to catalogue the country's giant-lore systematically. Rhys's Yscydion Gawr is more properly associated with Moel Yscydion in North Wales, suggesting the name may have travelled south and attached itself to Cribarth's equally giant-shaped silhouette rather than originating there, a reminder that Welsh giant legends were rarely fixed to a single hill for long. The related figure Cribwr, whose name is more directly tied to Cribarth itself, belongs to a separate strand of the tradition connected to Arthur's court, where he answers to the names Hot Pottage, Warm Porridge and Morsel of Bread when summoned by three sisters, a comic variant that shows the sleeping-giant tradition here has been retold and reshaped across several centuries rather than fixed in a single telling.

The mountain sits above one of the most folklore-dense valleys in South Wales: at its foot lies Cerrig Duon, a Bronze Age stone circle and stone row complex sometimes called the most sacred pagan site in Wales, and the surrounding Fforest Fawr has its own scattering of ghost and giant tales tied to the old drovers' roads.

Historical Context

Origins & Dating

Historical PeriodAttested in writing by c. 1600; the specific 'Yscydion, sleeping and awaiting Arthur's call' framing found in modern retellings does not appear in the Rhys manuscript (which names the giant Cribwr, not Yscydion, and gives no Arthur-return motif) — it looks like a later elaboration or conflation with the separate Yscydion Gawr of Moel Yscydion in Meirionnydd
Earliest Written RecordSion Dafydd Rhys, manuscript treatise on the giants of Wales, Peniarth MS 118 (National Library of Wales), c. 1600 — names 'Cribwr Gawr' directly and states the place was called Cribarth ('Garth Cribwr Gawr') after him

Folklore dates are often approximate, especially where a story predates any surviving written source.

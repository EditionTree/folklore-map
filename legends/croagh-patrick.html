Croagh Patrick

Saint Patrick fasted forty days on Croagh Patrick in 441 AD, driving serpents from Ireland and wrestling an angel for the right to judge the Irish at the Last Day. Long before Patrick, though, the summit was already sacred to Lugh, and the barefoot pilgrims climbing it each July follow a path three thousand years old.

The Legend

Croagh Patrick (Irish: Cruach Phádraig, 'Patrick's Stack') rises 764 metres above Clew Bay in County Mayo, and the tradition of sacred pilgrimage to its summit predates Christianity by at least two millennia. The mountain was already a focal point for Lughnasadh, the harvest festival of the god Lugh, around which Bronze Age communities gathered on the summit every August. Its Christianisation is one of the most literal palimpsests in Irish religious history.

Saint Patrick's fast on the mountain became one of Ireland's great sacred-place traditions.

The Forty Days on the Mountain

Patrick's own fifth-century document Confessio mentions fasting in isolation, and later tradition located his great Lenten fast of 441 AD on the summit of the mountain, then still known by its older name, Cruachán Aigle. The earliest text to make that specific association between Patrick and the mountain is Tírechán's Breviarium, preserved in the Book of Armagh around 807 AD, already three and a half centuries after Patrick's own lifetime. According to the earliest hagiographies, it was here that Patrick drove the snakes and pestilent creatures from Ireland over the cliff known as Lugnademon (the hollow of the demons) and rang his bell (the Bachall Ísu) so furiously that it cracked. He then wrestled with the angel Victoricus, who had brought him to Ireland in his childhood visions, demanding the right to be Ireland's special intercessor at the Last Judgement. The angel eventually conceded. Patrick threw the cracked bell into the hollow and a new one miraculously appeared on the summit.

The pattern (Irish: turas) of Croagh Patrick, Reek Sunday, the last Sunday of July, draws tens of thousands of pilgrims each year, many barefoot on the quartzite scree. The tradition is documented continuously from the fifth century through medieval pilgrimage accounts to modern ethnographic fieldwork by the Irish Folklore Commission. Archaeological surveys have confirmed evidence of prehistoric activity at the summit, and the oratory of St Patrick (rebuilt in 1905) marks the traditional location of his vigil. The mountain's dual identity, as Lugh's hill and Patrick's purgatory, makes it one of the most layered sacred sites in Ireland.

Earliest Written RecordTírechán's 'Breviarium', preserved in the Book of Armagh (c. 807 AD), is the earliest written record associating St Patrick's forty-day fast with the mountain, then known as Cruachán Aigle.

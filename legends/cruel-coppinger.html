Cruel Coppinger

A fearsome Dane flung ashore by shipwreck on the north Cornish coast, 'Cruel Coppinger' press-ganged the locals into a band of smugglers and wreckers, ruling the cliffs by terror.

The Legend

Cruel Coppinger is the dark legend of the north Cornish coast around Morwenstow and Welcombe, a semi-historical figure swollen by folklore into something near demonic. The story tells of a huge and terrible Dane cast ashore from a wreck in a storm, who seized a local heiress, took over her house and lands, and gathered the smugglers and wreckers of the coast into a gang that he ruled by fear, threatening death to any who showed mercy to shipwrecked sailors.

The Figure Behind the Legend

The legend as it survives was largely shaped by the Reverend Robert Stephen Hawker, vicar of Morwenstow, who collected the local tales and published them (with embellishments of his own) in Charles Dickens's magazine All the Year Round in 1866. Behind the myth lie probably one or two real Coppingers, shipwreck survivors and smugglers active around 1792-93, conflated and magnified into a single monstrous outlaw. He is said to have vanished as he came, carried off the coast in a storm, leaving the cliffs of Hartland and Morwenstow to keep his fearsome name.

Historical Context

Origins & Dating

Approximate Origin1792 or 1793 (claimed shipwreck date; accounts vary)
Historical PeriodGeorgian Cornwall (claimed setting); Victorian Britain (literary popularisation by Hawker)
Earliest Written RecordRev. Robert Stephen Hawker of Morwenstow collected and embellished the tale, publishing it in Charles Dickens's magazine 'All the Year Round' in 1866, and again in his own 'Footprints of Former Men in Far Cornwall'. Hawker's account places Coppinger's shipwreck arrival at Welcombe Mouth in 1792; other tellings give December 1793 at Marsland Mouth. A real Roscoff-based smuggler named John Coppinger, active supplying Cornish smugglers before the French Revolution, may be a partial historical basis, though the connection to 'Cruel Coppinger' specifically is unconfirmed

Folklore dates are often approximate, especially where a story predates any surviving written source.

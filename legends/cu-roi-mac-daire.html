Cú Roí mac Dáire

koo ROH-ee mak DAH-reh

Cú Roí mac Dáire is a supernatural warrior-king of Munster in Irish tradition. His stronghold is associated with Caherconree in the Slieve Mish Mountains, where legend says his fortress revolved at night so that enemies could not find its entrance.

The Legend

Cú Roí mac Dáire is a supernatural warrior-king of Munster in the Irish Ulster Cycle, a figure of immense and uncanny power whose hilltop fortress is identified with Caherconree on the Slieve Mish mountains of the Dingle peninsula in County Kerry. So great was his magic that his stronghold was said to revolve each night, its entrance hidden, so that no enemy could find the door after dark.

The Fortress on the Mountain

He is most famous as the mysterious challenger in the tale of Bricriu's Feast: disguised as a hideous churl, he proposes a beheading game to the heroes of Ulster: he will let a man behead him tonight if that man will bear the return blow tomorrow. Only Cú Chulainn keeps the bargain and bares his neck, and for his courage is named the greatest champion of Ireland. Cú Roí's own end comes through betrayal: his wife Bláthnat, in love with Cú Chulainn, reveals the secret of his life and signals the attack by which he is slain.

The tale, known as Fled Bricrenn ('Bricriu's Feast'), reaches back to the eighth century in its earliest textual form and survives complete in the Lebor na hUidre ('Book of the Dun Cow'), copied around 1106 and the oldest surviving manuscript written entirely in Irish. Its beheading-game challenge, issued by a magical stranger and honoured a year and a day later, is the acknowledged direct ancestor of the beheading test in the medieval English poem 'Sir Gawain and the Green Knight', making Cú Roí one of the deepest roots of that later, more famous story.

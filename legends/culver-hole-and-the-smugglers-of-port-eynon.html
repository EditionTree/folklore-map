Culver Hole and the Smugglers of Port Eynon

A stone-walled sea cave on the Gower coast was said to be smuggler John Lucas's contraband store, reached by a tunnel wide enough to ride a horse through.

The Legend

Culver Hole is a narrow cleft in the cliffs near Port Eynon on the Gower Peninsula, sealed since at least the thirteenth or fourteenth century behind a sixty-foot stone wall pierced with irregular window openings, most likely built originally as a dovecote ('culver' being an old English word for pigeon). Cadw's own scheduled-monument record links its construction to a castle at Port Eynon referenced in a lawsuit dated 1396, giving the structure a firm medieval or post-medieval date independent of anything said about it later. Local legend attached to the site a far more colourful history: that the eighteenth-century smuggler John Lucas, a Gower man reputed to have spent years abroad as a pirate before returning home, refitted nearby Salthouse as his smuggling headquarters and used Culver Hole itself as a hidden store for contraband brandy and tobacco, connected to Salthouse by a secret tunnel said to be wide enough to ride a horse through.

The Story

The legend has a well-documented origin of its own. Research by the National Trust has shown that the Lucas smuggling story was largely invented in the 1830s by the Reverend Dr J. H. Spry, who wrote it up as a family history in connection with a lawsuit over ownership of the property. That the tale is a nineteenth-century fabrication rather than an authentic eighteenth-century tradition does not appear to have dented its staying power. Culver Hole is still presented across Gower tourism and heritage sites today as 'the Smuggler's Cove', and the fictional John Lucas remains the cave's best-known association, making this as much a legend about the manufacture of folklore as a smuggling tale in its own right.

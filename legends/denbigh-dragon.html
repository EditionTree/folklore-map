Denbigh Dragon

A giant knight with eight fingers and two thumbs on each hand slew the dragon nesting in Denbigh Castle's great hall, and the townsfolk's cry of 'Dim bych!' (No dragon!) is said to have given the town its name.

The Legend

According to the folklore attached to Denbigh Castle, the great hall of the castle was once the lair of a dragon that had put the town under siege, dropping from the sky to prey on livestock and people alike. The task of dealing with the beast fell to a knight remembered in some versions as John Salusbury of Lleweni, a documented Denbighshire gentry figure, but in the oldest form of the tale as Sion y Bodiau ('John of the Thumbs'), a giant of a man said to have eight fingers and two thumbs on each hand. The nickname actually belonged to two Salusburys, an uncle who died in 1547 or 1549 and his nephew who died in 1578, and the dragon story has been told of both, so which Sion is meant depends on which telling is followed.

He drove his broadsword into the unscaled skin at the base of the dragon's wing.

The Dragon in the Great Hall

Sion rode to the castle in full armour on a great shire horse, carrying a broadsword built for a giant's grip. Onlookers were said to have heard the awful sounds of battle and seen sparks and arcs of fire thrown off by the dragon's breath as the fight dragged on for hours, sword against claw and scale, neither side giving ground. The turning point came when Sion spotted two bare patches of unscaled skin at the base of the dragon's wings; waiting for the creature to rear up and spread its wings to attack, he drove his broadsword into one of the unprotected spots and, after several more blows, cut off its head.

Riding back into the town with the dragon's severed head held aloft on his sword, Sion was met by townsfolk who had been hiding from the beast. Their relieved cry, 'Dim bych!', Welsh for 'No dragon!', is said in this telling to be the origin of the town's name, Dinbych (Denbigh), a folk etymology that has helped keep the story, and the dragon, alive in local memory for centuries. No early written text for the dragon tale itself has been located; it appears to be a folk giant-slayer motif that became attached, at some later point, to the real Salusbury family name.

Origins & Dating

Historical PeriodSome tellings attach the giant-slayer to John Salusbury of Lleweni (d. 1578), though the 'oldest form' of the tale, which names the giant Sion y Bodiau, cannot itself be firmly dated
Earliest Written RecordNo early written text for the dragon tale itself is known; the tale is a folk giant/dragon-slayer motif that became locally attached to the historical Salusbury family of Lleweni, specifically John Salusbury ('Sion y Bodiau', d. 1547/49), a documented 16th-century Denbighshire gentry figure (see History of Parliament Online and the Dictionary of Welsh Biography)

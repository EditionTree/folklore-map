Devil's Bridge

At Devil's Bridge in Ceredigion, the Devil offered to span a gorge in exchange for the first soul to cross. An old woman agreed, then threw bread across the bridge so her dog ran over first. Three bridges now stand stacked above the falls, the Devil's at the bottom.

The Legend

Devil's Bridge, Pontarfynach in Welsh, 'the bridge over the Mynach', is a village in the Cambrian Mountains of Ceredigion where three bridges stand stacked one above another over a spectacular gorge carved by the Mynach and Rheidol rivers. The lowest and oldest bridge, believed to date from between 1075 and 1200, is the one the legend claims the Devil built.

The old woman sent her dog across first and won the bridge without losing her soul.

The First Soul to Cross

The story follows a pattern found across Europe wherever an improbably difficult bridge exists: the Devil appears and offers to build it in exchange for the soul of the first living creature to cross. At Devil's Bridge the human party to the bargain is Megan, an old woman whose cow has strayed to the far side of the gorge. The Devil constructs the bridge overnight and waits at the far end for Megan to walk across and forfeit her soul. But the old woman, cannier than the Devil expected, pulls a crust of bread from her apron and throws it across the bridge. Her little dog chases after it, becoming the first living creature to cross. Bound by his own terms, the Devil is left with the soul of a dog, which is no use to him at all, and vanishes in fury.

The three-bridge structure is not legendary but architectural fact. The lowest, Devil's, bridge is a single stone arch of Norman or early medieval construction; above it sits a second bridge built in 1753; above that, a modern road bridge erected in 1901. Each generation built over the previous bridge rather than demolishing it, and the result, three arches receding into the spray of the Mynach Falls, is one of the most photographed sites in Wales. The gorge drops nearly 90 metres through ancient oak woodland, and the Devil's Punchbowl at the base of the falls adds to the infernal naming. The English name 'Devil's Bridge' is attested in deeds from 1734, though the devil-built-bridge story itself was not written down until the Hutton family visited in 1787: Catherine Hutton recorded her own account, and her father William Hutton published his version in 1803.

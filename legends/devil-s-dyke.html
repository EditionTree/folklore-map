Devil's Dyke

The Devil carved this gash through the South Downs in one night, set on drowning Sussex's churches — a crowing cockerel tricked him into thinking dawn had broken, and he fled mid-dig.

The Legend

According to legend, the Devil was so enraged by the sight of the Sussex Weald studded with churches that he resolved to dig a channel through the South Downs overnight and let the sea flood in to drown them all. He set to work after dark, hurling great clods of earth aside as he carved out the valley that became known as the Devil's Dyke.

The Devil carved this gash through the South Downs in one night, set on drowning Sussex's churches - a crowing cockerel tricked him into thinking dawn had broken, and he fled mid-dig.

The Gash in the Downs

He was stopped only by a trick. In the most common telling, an old woman living nearby was woken by the noise of his digging; she lit a candle, which in turn woke her cockerel, who began to crow. Seeing the light and hearing the bird, the Devil mistook it for sunrise and fled before dawn could catch him at his work, leaving the dyke unfinished. Another version has the woman hold a sieve in front of her candle so that it glowed like a rising moon, or sun, with the same effect. Either way, the Weald and its churches were saved.

A coda to the story claims that the last clod of earth the Devil threw over his shoulder as he fled landed in the sea off the Hampshire coast and became the Isle of Wight. In reality the dyke is a dry valley shaped by meltwater during the last Ice Age, later fortified as one of the largest Iron Age hillforts in Sussex.

Its eerie, oversized scale was more than enough to convince Victorian visitors, who arrived by the trainload once a branch line reached the foot of the hill in 1887, that nothing short of the Devil could have dug it. By the 1890s the Dyke had become one of the most elaborate tourist attractions in southern England: a fairground, two bandstands, an observatory and a camera obscura all operated on the clifftop, and in 1894 an aerial cableway, Britain's first cable car, carried sightseers 70 metres above the valley floor along a 350-metre span. A steep-grade funicular railway opened in 1897 to spare visitors the climb back up from Poynings after tea in the village below. On Whit Monday 1893 some 30,000 people made the trip. The cable car and the funicular had both closed by 1909, and the branch line followed in 1938, but concrete pylon bases from the cableway still stand in the valley, a second ruin overlaid on the Devil's own unfinished one.

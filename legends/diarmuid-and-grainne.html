Diarmuid and Grainne

DEER-mwid and GRAWN-yuh

Grainne placed a geis on the young warrior Diarmuid to flee with her on their wedding night rather than marry the ageing Fionn. He died on Ben Bulben, killed by the boar Fionn refused to help him fight.

The Legend

One of the great love-tales of the Irish Fenian Cycle, the pursuit of Diarmuid and Grainne begins when Grainne, betrothed to the ageing Fionn mac Cumhaill, instead lays a binding geis on the young and handsome warrior Diarmuid Ua Duibhne, compelling him to carry her away on the eve of the wedding. Diarmuid resists at first, torn between loyalty to Fionn and the geis he cannot refuse, but a ball seirce, a magical love-spot on his forehead placed there by a fairy woman, meant that Grainne could not help but love him the moment she saw him.

For sixteen years they were hunted across Ireland and Scotland

The Lovers on the Run

For years the lovers are hunted the length of Ireland by Fionn and the Fianna, sheltering in woods and cromlechs, many a dolmen is still called 'the bed of Diarmuid and Grainne'. Diarmuid's foster-father, the god Aengus Óg, helps them more than once, on one occasion hiding Grainne beneath his cloak of invisibility while Diarmuid leaps clean over the heads of the pursuing Fianna, and he gives the couple a standing rule for survival: never sleep two nights running in the same place, never enter a cave with only one way out, never land on an island with only one landing point. Place-names across the west of Ireland, from the Beara Peninsula to the wooded island in Lough Key, still carry their names as the sites of these constant, restless shelters.

At last a grudging peace is made, but Fionn's jealousy is not spent. Diarmuid is fated to die by a great boar, and when he is gored hunting the wild pig of Ben Bulben in Sligo, Fionn, who alone could heal him with a drink of water carried in his hands, twice lets the water slip away until it is too late. The tale, with its old king, young lovers and doomed hero, is a clear forerunner of the later romance of Tristan and Iseult.

Historical Context

Origins & Dating

Historical PeriodEarly Medieval Ireland (earliest attestation, 9th century); Fenian Cycle
Earliest Written RecordAn allusion in the Book of Aicill (an Old Irish law tract) indicates the tale already existed by the 9th century; it is also listed among tale-titles in a 10th-century tale-list manuscript. The earliest surviving full text, however, is in Modern Irish and dates only to the 16th century

Folklore dates are often approximate, especially where a story predates any surviving written source.

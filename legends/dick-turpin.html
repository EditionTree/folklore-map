Dick Turpin

The highwayman hanged at York in 1739 whose ghost — or the ghost of his horse Black Bess — is said to ride the old coach roads of Yorkshire still. The real Turpin was a brutal criminal; legend transformed him into a dashing folk hero, one of the great reinventions in British popular mythology.

The Legend

Dick Turpin, the Essex butcher turned poacher, burglar and highwayman, was tried at York Assizes on 22 March 1739 on two indictments of horse theft, convicted, and hanged at the Knavesmire seventeen days later on 7 April. The short-drop method of the day gave him a slow, strangling death, and his body was left hanging for hours before it was cut down, a brutal end that legend would spend the next century smoothing over.

Turpin's ghost, or the ghost of the gallant Black Bess who died under him on that legendary ride, is said to haunt the old coaching roads, glimpsed by night where the highway runs lonely.

Black Bess on the Road

In death he was transformed into the dashing 'gentleman of the road' he never was in life. The romance owes most to William Harrison Ainsworth's 1834 novel Rookwood, which invented the famous overnight ride from London to York on his mare Black Bess, a feat originally told of an earlier robber, John Nevison. In Yorkshire folklore Turpin's ghost, or the ghost of the gallant Black Bess who died under him on that legendary ride, is said to haunt the old coaching roads, glimpsed by night where the highway runs lonely. His grave in York is still shown, and the gap between the brutal reality of the man, a member of the violent Gregory gang, and the gallant phantom of the ballads is itself a study in how folklore launders a villain into a hero.

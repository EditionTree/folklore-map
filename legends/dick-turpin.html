Dick Turpin

The highwayman hanged at York in 1739 whose ghost — or the ghost of his horse Black Bess — is said to ride the old coach roads of Yorkshire still. The real Turpin was a brutal criminal; legend transformed him into a dashing folk hero, one of the great reinventions in British popular mythology.

A visual interpretation of Dick Turpin haunting the old Yorkshire roads
The legend

Dick Turpin, the Essex butcher turned poacher, burglar and highwayman, was hanged at York in 1739, and in death was transformed by legend into the dashing 'gentleman of the road' he never was in life. The romance owes most to William Harrison Ainsworth's 1834 novel Rookwood, which invented the famous overnight ride from London to York on his mare Black Bess — a feat originally told of an earlier robber, John Nevison.

Turpin's ghost, or the ghost of the gallant Black Bess who died under him on that legendary ride, is said to haunt the old coaching roads, glimpsed by night where the highway runs lonely.

Black Bess on the road

In Yorkshire folklore Turpin's ghost, or the ghost of the gallant Black Bess who died under him on that legendary ride, is said to haunt the old coaching roads, glimpsed by night where the highway runs lonely. His grave in York is still shown, and the gap between the brutal reality of the man — a member of the violent Gregory gang — and the gallant phantom of the ballads is itself a study in how folklore launders a villain into a hero.

Dick Whittington and His Cat

English civic legend built around the real medieval merchant and thrice Lord Mayor of London, Richard Whittington, said to have fled a harsh apprenticeship, been turned back at the foot of Highgate Hill by the sound of Bow Bells promising he would one day be mayor, and made his fortune selling his cat to a rat plagued foreign king. The real Whittington was a wealthy mercer of good birth with no recorded cat; the earliest surviving telling of the fortune making cat is a ballad printed in 1612, almost two centuries after his death.

The Legend

Richard Whittington, born around 1354, was a leading London mercer and moneylender who served as Lord Mayor of London three times, in 1397, 1406 and 1419, and left much of his considerable fortune to charitable and civic projects on his death in 1423, including work on Newgate prison and a library at the Guildhall.

The Story

The folk legend that grew up around him tells a very different, poorer origin story. As a boy servant despairing of his harsh treatment, Dick is said to have fled London, only to stop at the foot of Highgate Hill when he heard the bells of Bow Church seem to peal out 'Turn again, Whittington, thrice Lord Mayor of London.' Believing the bells prophetic, he returned to the city, and his fortune was made when his one possession, a cat, was sold for a huge sum to the ruler of a country plagued by rats, after which Whittington rose through trade and civic office exactly as the bells had promised.

No historical evidence connects the real Whittington to a cat, and the wealthy Whittington family had no need of a servant's fortune tale to explain its rise. The earliest surviving version of the cat story is a ballad by Richard Johnson, printed in his collection 'A Crown Garland of Golden Roses' in 1612; a now lost play on the subject was licensed for performance in 1604 to 1605, suggesting the legend was already established in some form by then. Later chapbooks fixed the turning point specifically at Highgate Hill. A stone marking the traditional spot, the Whittington Stone, stands today at the foot of the hill near the Whittington Hospital, topped since 1964 by a stone statue of a cat.

Dinas Emrys

DEE-nass EM-riss

At Dinas Emrys, legend says King Vortigern's walls collapsed each night until the young Merlin revealed two dragons fighting beneath the hill. The red dragon's victory over the white became an enduring symbol of Wales.

The Legend

Dinas Emrys is a small, craggy, wooded hill near Beddgelert in Snowdonia, crowned with the remains of an ancient fortification, and it is one of the most important legendary sites in all of Wales, the place where the red dragon of Welsh nationhood was first revealed. The story is told in the early Welsh tradition and by Geoffrey of Monmouth. The tyrant-king Vortigern, having invited the Saxons into Britain and then fled their treachery, came here to build a stronghold; but whatever his masons raised by day collapsed into the earth each night.

Merlin revealed that two dragons were fighting beneath Vortigern's collapsing walls.

The Dragons Below the Hill

His counsellors advised that the foundations must be sprinkled with the blood of a boy born of no father, and such a boy was found: the young Merlin, here called Emrys (Ambrosius), who gives the hill its name, 'the fortress of Emrys'. The boy exposed the druids' counsel as false and revealed the true cause: beneath the hill lay a hidden pool, and in it two dragons, one red and one white, who woke and fought. Merlin prophesied their meaning: the white dragon was the Saxons, the red the Britons, and though the red would be hard pressed, it would in the end drive the white dragon out. The red dragon's destined victory became the enduring emblem of Wales.

Archaeology has, remarkably, found that the hilltop does contain the remains of an ancient cistern or pool, lending the legend a curious anchor in the ground. Dinas Emrys thus binds together prophecy, the figure of Merlin, and the very symbol on the Welsh flag, and remains a potent place where the foundation-myth of a nation is fixed to a real and atmospheric hill.

Origins & Dating

Approximate Originc. 829-830 AD
Historical PeriodEarly medieval Wales (textual composition, 9th century); archaeological excavations (1910, 1954-56 by H. N. Savory) independently confirmed high-status occupation of the hillfort in the 5th-6th century AD, matching the story's own Vortigern-era setting
Earliest Written RecordHistoria Brittonum (c.829-830), the same text and dating basis as the Benlli Gawr entry enriched in the previous run; Geoffrey of Monmouth's Historia Regum Britanniae (c.1135-39) later retold the story with Merlin in Emrys's place

Folklore dates are often approximate, especially where a story predates any surviving written source.

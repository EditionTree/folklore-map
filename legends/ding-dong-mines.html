Ding Dong mines

An ancient Cornish mining landscape with traditions connecting it to Joseph of Arimathea and old tin-working lore. Mine country often births knockers, hidden spirits, and stories of wealth beneath the hill.

The Legend

The Ding Dong mines lie in the high granite moorland of West Penwith near Madron, in the far west of Cornwall — an ancient and atmospheric tin-mining landscape dominated by the ruined engine houses of the old workings. Reputed to be among the very oldest mines in Cornwall, worked (it was said) since prehistoric times, the antiquity of Ding Dong gathered to it one of the most remarkable legends in Cornish tradition: that it was visited by Jesus Christ himself.

The Story

The legend grows from the real ancient trade in Cornish tin, which reached the Mediterranean world long before the Roman conquest. Folk tradition held that Joseph of Arimathea — the wealthy man of the Gospels, here imagined as a tin-merchant trading with Cornwall — came to these mines, and that on one voyage he brought with him the boy Jesus, who walked among the tinners and spoke to them. Cornish miners of the Victorian age still repeated the belief in the mine's fabulous age; one old miner declared that only Dolcoath was older than Ding Dong, 'which was worked before the time of Jesus Christ'.

Like all Cornish mines, Ding Dong was also the domain of the Knockers — the small mine-spirits whose tapping in the workings might warn of danger or lead to rich lodes, and who expected their due of respect. Between the Knockers in the dark below and the wondrous tale of Joseph and the young Jesus above, the Ding Dong mines hold a special place in Cornish folklore, their great age made the seedbed of a legend that reached all the way to the Holy Land.

Dobhar-chú

DOH-war-khoo

A monstrous water beast resembling a giant hound or otter, said to inhabit lonely lakes and rivers. Tales describe it attacking livestock and people before vanishing beneath dark waters.

The Legend

The Dobhar-chú, the name means 'water-hound' or 'water-dog' in Irish, is a fearsome aquatic monster of Irish folklore, said to haunt lonely lakes and rivers, a creature like a giant otter or a cross between a hound and a fish, swift and savage in the water and deadly to any who disturb it. It is sometimes called the 'king otter', a great white otter with black ear-tips, and is reckoned among the most dangerous of Ireland's water-beasts.

The Water Legend at the Heart of the Tale

The legend is bound above all to County Leitrim and to a famous, gruesome story commemorated on a real gravestone. At Conwall (Conwell) cemetery near Glenade stands the weathered tombstone of a woman named Grace, or Grainne, who, tradition holds, was killed by a Dobhar-chú as she washed clothes or bathed at the edge of Glenade Lough in the 18th century. Her husband, finding her savaged body with the beast still upon it, killed the creature, but as it died it whistled or cried out, and a second Dobhar-chú, its mate, rose from the lough and pursued him in a long and desperate chase across the country before he at last turned and slew it too. The carved stone is said to depict the beast itself.

The Dobhar-chú thus stands at the meeting of folklore and apparent record, its legend anchored to a named woman, a place and a monument that visitors can still see. As a savage water-hound that attacks and kills humans and avenges its mate, it is among the most concrete and chilling of Ireland's lake-monster traditions.

Dragon Hill, Uffington

A low chalk hill below the Uffington White Horse, said to be where Saint George slew the dragon. The bare chalk patch on top marks where the dragon's blood poisoned the grass.

The Legend

Dragon Hill is a curious flat-topped natural knoll standing just below the great prehistoric chalk figure of the Uffington White Horse, on the Berkshire Downs in Oxfordshire. Its most striking feature is a bare patch of white chalk on its summit where, despite the surrounding turf, no grass will grow — and folklore has long fixed on this barren scar with a famous explanation: it is the place where Saint George slew the dragon, and where the dragon's poisonous blood, spilling out as it died, soured the ground forever so that nothing green can cover it.

The bare patch on the hill was said to mark where Saint George slew the dragon.

The Chalk Mark of the Dragon

The legend ties England's patron saint and his dragon-slaying directly into this ancient ritual landscape, recasting the prehistoric monuments around it as the setting for the saint's great deed. The nearby White Horse, itself thousands of years old and of uncertain original meaning, was sometimes reinterpreted not as a horse at all but as the very dragon St George fought, or as a memorial of the victory — folklore weaving the older pagan figures into the Christian story.

Dragon Hill sits within one of the richest concentrations of legend-laden monuments in England, alongside the White Horse, the Iron Age hillfort of Uffington Castle, and the Neolithic tomb of Wayland's Smithy a little along the ridge. Together they form a landscape where prehistoric earthwork, pagan tradition and Christian saint-legend overlap, and the bald chalk crown of Dragon Hill keeps the memory of St George's dragon pinned to the Oxfordshire downs.

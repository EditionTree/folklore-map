Dragon of St Leonard's Forest

In 1614 a nine-foot serpent with two great football-sized lumps was said to lurk in St Leonard's Forest near Horsham — leaving a trail of slime, and killing dogs and a man with its venomous breath.

The legend

In August 1614, a London printer named John Trundle published an eighteen-page pamphlet describing a monstrous serpent discovered in the woods south of Horsham. According to the account, the creature was some nine feet long, coloured red and black, with two great lumps on its sides "as big as a football". It moved swiftly when provoked but otherwise lay still, and everywhere it passed it left behind a glutinous, slime-like trail. The pamphlet named three witnesses — John Steele, Christopher Holder, and a widow living near Faygate — who claimed to have seen the beast at close range, and reported that it had already killed two dogs and a man, apparently by spitting venom rather than biting.

The story

Trundle's pamphlet opens with an unusual disclaimer, admitting that earlier "true reports" of monsters in print had sometimes turned out to be false, but insisting this account could be trusted. Whether genuine sighting, escaped exotic animal, or simply a publisher's bid for a bestseller in an age hungry for wonders, the story took hold. Local tradition held that wherever the dragon's coiled body had rested, white lilies and adder's-tongue fern sprang up afterwards, said to have grown from its shed venom.

The St Leonard's Forest dragon is one of the best-documented "dragon sightings" in English folklore, surviving not as an oral tale but as a printed news pamphlet — a format more usually associated with reports of monstrous births, comets, and other portents of the period. It sits within a wider Sussex tradition of serpent-haunted woods and "knucker holes", and the forest's association with St Leonard, said to have driven out venomous creatures himself, gave the story an extra frisson of local resonance.

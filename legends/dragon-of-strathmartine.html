Dragon of Strathmartine

Nine daughters of a farmer at Pitempton were taken one by one, sent to fetch water from a well where a great serpent lay coiled. A blacksmith named Martin hunted it down and clubbed it to death, and the dragon's dying words are said to have named every place along its death-flight.

The Legend

A farmer at Pitempton, south-west of Strathmartine, sent his nine daughters, one after another, to fetch water from a nearby well. Each went and did not return; when the last failed to come back, the father went to look himself and found a huge, blood-smeared dragon coiled where his daughters should have been. He raised the district, and a blacksmith named Martin, betrothed to one of the dead girls, led the pursuit.

Martin lured the beast to its death with cunning and a spiked ball of dough.

The Spiked Ball of Dough

The dragon fled north-west and was draiglet, soaked, in the bog at Baldragon Moss, then driven on and surrounded at Strathmartine, where the crowd's cries of 'Strike, Martin!' urged on the killing blow. Tradition preserved the beast's own dying words as a rhyme mapping its death-flight: tempted at Pitempton, draiglet at Baldragon, stricken at Strike-Martin, and killed at Martin's Stone. That stone still stands near Bridgefoot, a Pictish symbol stone carved with a serpent that local tradition ties directly to the slain dragon, a real monument doing the work of a gravestone for a beast that never existed.

Dunnie

A shape-shifting Northumbrian spirit that often appears as a horse or donkey. It delights in tricks, carrying riders only to vanish and dump them into mud or water.

The Legend

The Hazelrigg Dunnie is a shape-shifting bogle of Northumberland, haunting the country around Belford and the crags near Hazelrigg. Like the Border bogles generally, it was a creature of mischief rather than real malice, but its favourite trick was a cruel one. It would take the shape of a horse and offer itself to be saddled; the moment the rider or ploughman mounted, the Dunnie would vanish, dropping its victim into the mud or the burn and braying with laughter from a safe distance.

The Otherworld at the Heart of the Tale

The folklorist William Henderson, drawing on an account from a clergyman who knew the area, recorded two particular tricks that became attached to the Dunnie's name. In one, a farmer saddling up to fetch the midwife found his horse carried both riders safely to their errand, only to vanish in the mire on the way home, leaving them stranded in the dark. In the other, a ploughman who thought he had caught his own horse got only as far as buckling on the harness before it slapped to the ground and the Dunnie bolted off laughing, transformed back to its true shape.

It was blamed for leading travellers astray, miscalling the hours, and meddling with the farm work. Tradition tied it especially to a cave in the Hazelrigg crags, where it was said to wander chanting a mournful, half-nonsensical verse about a lost key and hidden treasure: 'Cocken heugh there's gear enough, / Collier heugh there's mair, / For I've lost the key o' the Bounders, / An' I'm ruined for evermair.' Henderson read this as the ghost of a Border reiver mourning plunder he buried and died before he could recover, the Dunnie's endless haunting explained as a dead man's grief rather than simple mischief. The Dunnie is a fine example of the trickster-spirit of the Anglo-Scottish border, half practical joker, half ghost.

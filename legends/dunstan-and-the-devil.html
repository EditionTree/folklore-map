Dunstan and the Devil

Saint Dunstan was said to have seized the Devil by the nose with blacksmith's tongs. The tale explains horseshoes as protection and gives English folklore one of its best infernal workplace accidents.

The legend

Saint Dunstan (c. 909–988) was one of the towering figures of Anglo-Saxon England — Abbot of Glastonbury, Archbishop of Canterbury, reformer of the English Church and adviser to kings — but in folk memory he is best remembered for a homely and comic legend in which he got the better of the Devil himself. Before his rise to high office Dunstan was a skilled craftsman and metalworker, and folklore made him a blacksmith, working at his forge, and it is at the forge that his famous encounter takes place.

The story

The story tells that the Devil came to tempt the saint, in some versions disguised, in others openly, or even (in a bawdier telling) appearing as a beautiful woman whose cloven hoof gave the game away. Dunstan, seeing through the disguise, seized the Devil's nose in a pair of red-hot blacksmith's tongs straight from the fire and held him fast, twisting and burning, while the Devil roared and begged for release. Only when the Devil promised never to enter a place where a horseshoe was hung did Dunstan let him go.

This is the folk-explanation for one of the most enduring of English superstitions: the horseshoe nailed above the door for luck and protection, said to keep the Devil out because of the bargain wrung from him by Dunstan's tongs. The tale, gleefully retold for a thousand years, gives English folklore one of its finest images — the saintly smith catching the Devil by the nose — and a domestic charm still seen on doorways across the country. Dunstan's chief seat at Glastonbury anchors the legend in Somerset.

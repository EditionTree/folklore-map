Elder Mother

The spirit within the elder tree must be asked before the first cut. Burn her wood without asking, tradition warns, and a death follows in the household within the year.

The Legend

The Elder Mother is a plant spirit tied to the elder tree (Sambucus nigra), known as Hyldemor in Denmark and under various local names elsewhere in England. In English folk tradition, the elder was regarded as one of the most supernaturally charged trees in the country, and the customs surrounding it were recorded by 17th and 18th-century antiquaries across the Midlands and the south of England. The most widely attested belief was that before cutting any part of an elder, whether branch, root, or leaf, the cutter must remove their hat and ask permission aloud. Without this permission, the spirit within the tree would take its revenge on the household.

The Divine Presence

The consequences of burning elder wood without asking were considered particularly severe. The tradition held that a death would follow within the household before the year was out. This was not general bad luck but specific retributive logic: the Elder Mother had been killed without her consent and would take a life in exchange. Katharine Briggs, in the Dictionary of British Folk-Tales (1970) and An Encyclopedia of Fairies (1976), documents the elder-spirit tradition as one of the most persistent plant-spirit beliefs in England, continuous from at least the Tudor period into the living memory of her informants. The exact form of the danger varied by county (in some regions it was the children of the household who were most at risk), but the prohibition on burning elder was remarkably consistent across England.

The elder's ambiguous status ran deeper than a single spirit. In Christian tradition, Judas Iscariot was said to have hanged himself on an elder, explaining the tree's drooping habit; in another strand of tradition, the Cross was made from elder wood. These associations with betrayal and execution reinforced the elder's reputation as a dangerous tree to interfere with. Medicinally, however, the elder was one of the most useful plants in the hedgerow: its flowers made anti-inflammatory cordials, its berries produced medicines for colds and fevers. This gave it a double identity, a tree that healed when approached correctly but killed when treated carelessly. The Elder Mother figure reconciles this duality, offering wood and medicine freely to those who ask but exacting full payment from those who take without asking.

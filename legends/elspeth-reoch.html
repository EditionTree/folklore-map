Elspeth Reoch

A Caithness girl said fairies taught her second sight at a lochside meeting when she was twelve. Decades later in Orkney, her confession of a dead kinsman's nightly visits sent her to the gallows within a single day.

The Legend

Elspeth Reoch was born in Caithness, the daughter of a piper to the Earl of Caithness, and spent part of her childhood with relatives on an island in Lochaber before eventually settling on Orkney's mainland. She told her interrogators that at around twelve years old, while staying in Lochaber, she met two men by a loch, one dressed in black and one in green tartan. The man in green told her he could teach her to know "anything she wanted," and instructed her to boil an egg and rub its steam over her eyes and hands, unwashed, to gain the power of second sight.

The Story

Years later, after Reoch had settled in Orkney and borne an illegitimate child, she said the man in black returned, this time identified as a dead relative named Johne Stewart, who visited her bedside on three successive nights and became her lover. He told her he was "neither dead nor alive but was forever trapped between heaven and earth," and commanded her to remain silent about their meetings to preserve her powers. Brought before the court at Kirkwall in March 1616, she confessed to these encounters, to curing illness with the herb melefour (yarrow) while reciting the Trinity formula, and to feigning muteness to protect her gift. She was found guilty, executed by strangulation, and her body burned that same afternoon.

Reoch's case is preserved in the University of Edinburgh's Survey of Scottish Witchcraft database, drawn from the original Lawting Court records of Orkney and Shetland. Scholars who have studied the case, including Diane Purkiss, have noted that Orkney's court clerks, following Bishop James Law's 1614 reforms, routinely rewrote references to "fairies" in testimony as "the devil" or "a demon." That editorial habit means Reoch's own account of a fairy initiation survives only filtered through the language of a demonic pact, making her one of the clearest documented instances of fairy-belief testimony absorbed into a Scottish witch trial.

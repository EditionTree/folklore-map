Epona

eh-POH-nuh

A Celtic horse goddess worshipped across the Roman world, including Britain. She protected horses, riders, cavalry, and journeys, often shown with mares, foals, or a cornucopia.

A visual interpretation of Epona in Roman Britain
The Legend

Epona was the great horse-goddess of the Celtic world, and one of the very few Celtic deities to be taken up so warmly by the Romans that her worship spread right across the Empire — carried above all by the cavalry, who honoured her as the protector of horses, mules and the men who rode and tended them. Her name derives from the Gaulish word for horse, and she is almost always shown in her company: seated side-saddle on a mare, flanked by horses or by foals, or feeding them from a dish, and frequently holding a cornucopia or basket of fruit, marking her also as a goddess of plenty, fertility and abundance.

A Celtic horse goddess worshipped across the Roman world, including Britain

The Divine Presence

In Roman Britain Epona was honoured at cavalry forts and stables, and her images and dedications appear where mounted units were stationed. Uniquely among Celtic deities she was even given a feast day in the Roman calendar, a measure of how thoroughly her cult was embraced. Her care extended beyond the living horse to the journey of the soul, and some evidence links her with guiding and protecting the dead on their final ride.

Epona's deep importance to a horse-keeping people echoes on in the insular Celtic traditions: scholars have long seen her shadow behind the Welsh Rhiannon, who appears riding an unovertakable horse and is bound up with sovereignty and with horses, and behind the Irish horse-and-sovereignty goddesses. As the divine guardian of the horse — central to war, travel, farming and status in the ancient world — Epona was among the most beloved and far-travelled of all the Celtic gods.

Fachan

FAKH-an

A single eye, a single hand bursting from its chest, a single leg: the Fachan is the strangest monster in Highland folklore, haunting Argyll's Glen Etive.

The Legend

The Fachan, also called the Dìreach Ghlinn Eitidh (the 'straight one of Glen Etive'), is among the most grotesque monsters of the Scottish Highlands, recorded by the great folklorist J. F. Campbell in his Popular Tales of the West Highlands. It is a being of terrible single-ness: one eye in the middle of its face, one hand reaching from its chest, one leg beneath it, and a single tuft of dark feathers or hair on its head, of which Campbell wrote that "it were easier to take a mountain from the root than to bend that tuft."

The Straight One of Glen Etive

Half a man cut down the middle, the Fachan haunts the lonely glens of Argyll, and to meet it was to risk being struck down by the mere horror of its aspect; some accounts give it a spiked club or flail with which it lashed at travellers. It belongs to a wider Celtic idea of the uncanny 'one-legged, one-eyed, one-armed' figure, a shape associated with the otherworld and with the posture of cursing.

The Fachan survives today almost entirely through Campbell's collection, published in four volumes in Edinburgh between 1860 and 1862 from stories gathered orally and often translated from Gaelic. That makes it one of the few Highland monsters whose fullest surviving record comes from a single named collector rather than generations of scattered retelling.

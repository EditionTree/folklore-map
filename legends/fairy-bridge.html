Fairy Bridge

A small Isle of Man bridge where travellers traditionally greet the fairies as they pass. Failing to acknowledge the Little People is considered unlucky, especially on journeys across the island.

The Legend

The Fairy Bridge is a small, unremarkable-looking road bridge on the Isle of Man, crossing the Santon Burn on the main road between Douglas and Castletown, and yet it is one of the most observed sites of living folk custom in the British Isles. Manx tradition holds that the bridge is a haunt of the Themselves (the fairies, or 'Little People', whom the islanders have always been careful never to name directly or offend) and that anyone crossing it should greet them politely to ensure good luck and a safe journey.

The Otherworld at the Heart of the Tale

The custom is remarkably persistent. To this day many Manx people, and visitors who have learned the tradition, will offer a greeting as they pass over the bridge: 'Laa mie' (good day) in Manx Gaelic, or simply 'Hello, fairies'. It is widely held that to cross without acknowledging the Little People risks bad luck, a spoilt journey, or worse.

The custom reflects the deep and characteristic Manx attitude to the fairies: a careful, respectful wariness toward an unseen people who must be placated rather than provoked, neither openly named nor lightly mocked. (Folklorists note that the modern roadside bridge may not be the 'true' original fairy bridge, an older crossing lying elsewhere, but it is here that the tradition has fixed itself.) The Fairy Bridge is a rare case of a folk custom still practised daily, a courtesy to the Little People exchanged on a busy island road.

Historical Context

Origins & Dating

Historical PeriodNotable finding, flagged for editorial attention: no written record of the fairy-greeting custom at this specific bridge predates 1800. John Bullock's 'A History of the Isle of Man' (1816) describes the nearby crossing as the 'Devil's Bridge', a place of ill-omen to be avoided — the opposite valence from the modern fairy-greeting custom, and not the same tradition. According to Yvonne Cresswell, former social history curator at Manx National Heritage, the greeting custom in its now-familiar form appears to date from around 1997-98, coinciding with a documented sharp increase in visitor awareness of it, with wider promotion during the island's tourism boom in the 20th century

Folklore dates are often approximate, especially where a story predates any surviving written source.

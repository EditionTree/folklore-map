Fairy Flag of Dunvegan

The MacLeods of Dunvegan preserve a silk banner known as the Fairy Flag. Clan tradition says it can be unfurled in times of peril to bring victory, but that its protective magic can only be used three times.

The Legend

The Fairy Flag, known in Gaelic as Am Bratach Sìth, is the most treasured heirloom of the Clan MacLeod, a fragile fragment of ancient silk, yellowed and worn, kept at Dunvegan Castle on the Isle of Skye, the clan's seat for some eight hundred years. Clan tradition holds that it carries the power to save the MacLeods in their direst need: unfurled in battle or disaster, it will bring them victory or deliverance, but only three times, after which its magic is spent. Twice, tradition says, it has already been raised in battle; once remains.

Clan tradition says it can be unfurled in times of peril to bring victory.

The Banner Kept for the Darkest Hour

The stories of its origin are several and lovely. The best-loved tells that a MacLeod chief loved a fairy woman who had to return to her own people after a year and a day; parting from him at the Fairy Bridge, she left the flag as a token, or wrapped it about their infant son as a protective blanket, and to this day the clan held that the flag must never be mistreated. A second version has a nurse leaving the chief's baby son alone in the castle's Fairy Tower, where a fairy comforts the restless child with a silken shawl while unseen voices sing him a lullaby. A third, grimmer account makes it a relic of the Crusades: a MacLeod crusader, aided by a hermit in the Holy Land, slew an evil spirit called the Daughter of Thunder, who in dying revealed the clan's future and gave him her girdle to be made into a banner.

Whatever its true history, the silk itself has been examined and dated. Alan J. B. Wace, textile curator at the Victoria and Albert Museum, concluded in the 1920s that it was woven in Syria or Rhodes in the fourth to seventh century AD, far older than any of the legends attached to it. Thomas Pennant, touring the Hebrides in 1772, was the first to record the story in writing, describing it as 'Braolauch Shi' and a gift from the fairy queen Titania. During a castle fire in 1939, the wind reportedly dropped as soon as it was carried to safety, and Royal Air Force pilots from the clan carried its photograph with them as a talisman through the Second World War. It remains at Dunvegan today, sealed in a glass frame, still MacLeod's to raise should the need ever come again.

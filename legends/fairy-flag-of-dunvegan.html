Fairy Flag of Dunvegan

The MacLeods of Dunvegan preserve a silk banner known as the Fairy Flag. Clan tradition says it can be unfurled in times of peril to bring victory, but that its protective magic can only be used three times.

The Legend

The Fairy Flag, known in Gaelic as Am Bratach Sìth, is the most treasured heirloom of the Clan MacLeod, a fragile fragment of ancient silk, yellowed and worn, kept at Dunvegan Castle on the Isle of Skye, the clan's seat for some eight hundred years. Clan tradition holds that it carries the power to save the MacLeods in their direst need: unfurled in battle or disaster, it will bring them victory or deliverance, but only three times, after which its magic is spent. Twice, the tradition says, it has already been raised, turning the tide at the battles of Glendale and Trumpan; once remains.

Clan tradition says it can be unfurled in times of peril to bring victory.

The Banner Kept for the Darkest Hour

The stories of its origin are several and lovely. The best-loved tells that a MacLeod chief married, or loved, a fairy woman who had to return to her own people after a set time; parting from him at the Fairy Bridge, she left the flag as a token, or wrapped it about their infant son as a protective blanket, and to this day the clan held that the flag must never be mistreated. Others say it was a holy relic brought back from the Crusades, or the war-banner of a Norse king. Whatever its true history (the silk may indeed be early-medieval and eastern), the Fairy Flag remains one of the most famous and genuinely cherished objects of Highland clan legend.

Historical Context

Origins & Dating

Earliest Written RecordThomas Pennant recorded the flag during his 1772 Hebridean tour, published as A Tour in Scotland, and Voyage to the Hebrides, 1772, describing it as 'Braolauch Shi' and gifted by the fairy queen Titania
Historical PeriodEarliest written record of the legend is Pennant's 1772 account; the physical silk was separately dated in the 1920s by Alan J. B. Wace, textile curator at the Victoria & Albert Museum, to Syria or Rhodes, 4th-7th century AD; firmly associated with Clan MacLeod by the late medieval period

Folklore dates are often approximate, especially where a story predates any surviving written source.

Fairy Invasion of Guernsey

A fairy prince stole a Guernsey woman for his queen, then came back with an army for the rest; the fighting on Rouge Rue ran the road red with blood.

The Legend

One of Guernsey's most dramatic folk tales tells of a fairy prince who arrived on Vazon Beach through the magical gate at L'Erée known as the Creux es Faies, a Neolithic passage grave long associated with the otherworld. The prince discovered a beautiful local woman, traditionally named Michèle De Garis, and fell instantly in love. Using fairy enchantment, he spirited her away to his realm and made her queen.

A fairy prince stole a Guernsey woman as his queen, then returned with an army — the bloody battle gave Rouge Rue its name.

The Otherworld at the Heart of the Tale

The fairies were so delighted with their new queen that they resolved to capture more Guernsey women, and a fairy army invaded the island. In one version of the tale, recorded by the nineteenth-century Guernsey writer Lane-Clarke, it was a young woman out fishing who first raised the alarm, spotting "a multitude of little men dressed in green, and armed with long bows and arrows" crossing towards the shore. The battle that followed was savage and prolonged; local tradition places the final confrontation at the site now known as Beau Séjour, and by its end only a man and a boy who had hidden together in an oven at St Andrew's had escaped the slaughter. The fairies took the remaining women as their prize.

The legend explains several features of local identity. The road where the heaviest fighting occurred ran red with blood and is still called Rouge Rue, 'the Red Road.' More playfully, islanders say that the mixing of fairy and human blood explains why many Guernsey folk are short: anyone under six feet is said to descend from the fairies, while the tall are descended from the handful of survivors. The nineteenth-century Guernsey folklorist Sir Edgar MacCulloch, whose collected material was published posthumously as Guernsey Folk Lore (1903), traced the tale to a real event: the 1372 raid on the island by Owain Lawgoch, a Welsh claimant to the principality fighting in French service and remembered locally as Yvon de Galles, whose foreign mercenaries appear to have been refashioned in island memory into an invading army from fairyland. Later nineteenth-century retellings softened the ending considerably, having the fairy conquerors eventually recalled to "the kingdom of the Invisible Beings" and leaving their human wives and children behind on the island rather than carrying them off for good. A related tale tied to the same fairy gate at Creux es Faies explains the origin of the Guernsey lily: a fairy suitor who carried off another island girl is said to have given her the flower's bulb as a keepsake, so that her family would always have something to remember her by.

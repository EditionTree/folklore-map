Sacred Sites Shantemon Mountain, County Cavan

Finn McCools Fingers

Five standing stones on Shantemon mountain in County Cavan — said to be the fingers of Fionn mac Cumhaill’s severed hand, lost in battle and turned to stone where they fell. The hill was also the inauguration place of the O’Reilly chieftains.

The legend

Finn McCool’s Fingers are a Bronze Age stone row on the north side of Shantemon Hill, about four kilometres from Ballyhaise in County Cavan. The row consists of five stones aligned on a northeast-southwest axis — four massive menhirs, the tallest nearly two metres high, and a smaller fallen boulder at the northeastern end. They are classified archaeologically as a stone row dating from between 1750 and 500 BC, but the name they carry belongs to a different and more vivid tradition.

The story

The legend says the stones are the fingers of the giant warrior Fionn mac Cumhaill, severed from his hand in battle and turned to stone where they landed on the hillside. An alternative version holds that Fionn carried the five stones from Cuilcagh Mountain in west Cavan and planted them on Shantemon as territorial markers. Either way, the naming anchors the Cavan landscape to the Fionn cycle — the body of mythology centred on the hero and his warrior band the Fianna — and gives a remote Border county a direct claim on one of Ireland’s most celebrated legendary figures. The Dúchas Schools’ Collection, gathered from Cavan schoolchildren in the 1930s, preserves local variants of the tradition.

Shantemon Hill itself carries additional layers of significance. It was the traditional inauguration site of the O’Reilly chieftains, the ruling Gaelic family of East Bréifne (roughly modern Cavan), and the hill was the scene of an annual harvest gathering on Bilberry Sunday — a survival of the pre-Christian festival of Lughnasadh. The convergence of megalithic archaeology, Fenian mythology, and Gaelic political ceremony on a single hilltop makes the site unusually dense in meaning, even by Irish standards. The stones are a protected National Monument and are accessible via a signposted walk from the Ballyhaise road.

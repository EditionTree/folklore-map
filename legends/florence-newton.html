Witches Youghal, County Cork, Ireland

Florence Newton

Florence Newton, the 'Witch of Youghal', was tried for witchcraft in County Cork in 1661 after allegedly bewitching a servant girl with a kiss — one of Ireland's best-documented witch trials.

The Legend

Florence Newton, the 'Witch of Youghal', was the central figure in one of the most important and best-documented witch trials in Irish history, held at the Cork assizes in 1661. She was a poor beggar-woman of Youghal in County Cork with a fearsome local reputation, and her troubles began at Christmas 1660 when she was turned away from the house of John Pyne and refused a piece of beef. Muttering and discontented, she shortly afterwards met Pyne's young maidservant, Mary Longdon, in the street and kissed her violently — and from that kiss, it was claimed, all the bewitching flowed.

Florence Newton's case became one of Ireland's best-documented witch trials, beginning with an alleged bewitching kiss.

The Witch of Youghal

Soon Mary Longdon fell into violent fits and trances, vomited pins and needles, saw visions of Newton and a veiled spectre, and had small stones and objects pelt her unseen. Newton was arrested and imprisoned, and the trial that followed turned on the classic tests of the age: she was said to be unable to recite the Lord's Prayer correctly, and the court applied 'scratching' — letting the afflicted girl draw blood from the suspected witch to break the spell. The drama reached its height when, as Mary finished her evidence, Newton pointed at her and said 'now she is down', and the girl instantly collapsed in a fit before the whole court.

A further charge accused Newton of bewitching to death a man named David Jones, who had jeeringly asked her to teach him the Lord's Prayer through the prison bars and let her kiss his hand — falling ill and dying soon after. The records of the trial, preserved by the magistrate Sir William Aston, give an unusually vivid picture of seventeenth-century witch-belief in Ireland. Florence Newton's ultimate fate is not certainly recorded, but she is presumed to have died in prison or been executed, and she remains the most famous witch of Irish tradition.

Folkestone White Horse

England’s youngest white horse, carved into the Kent chalk in 2003 by Gurkha soldiers and local volunteers — a millennium landmark inspired by the 3,000-year-old Uffington Horse and the Iron Age fort on Horse Hill below.

The Legend

The Folkestone White Horse stretches ninety metres across Cheriton Hill, overlooking the English terminal of the Channel Tunnel. Designed by local artist Charlie Newington and inspired by both the ancient White Horse of Uffington and a nearby Iron Age fort on a ridge known as Horse Hill, it is the youngest hill figure in England — completed in June 2003 as a millennium landmark intended to regenerate the Folkestone area.

The Story

The project was born in controversy. Planning permission was first sought in April 1998, but English Nature, the Green Party, and Friends of the Earth opposed it: the hillside was protected under the European Habitats Directive as a Site of Special Scientific Interest. The horse was finally approved in March 2002 by the Secretary of State, who ruled that its ‘emotional and symbolic value’ outweighed the possible environmental damage. Construction was completed entirely by hand: volunteers staked out a canvas template directed by the artist via radio, dug shallow trenches into the topsoil, and filled them with limestone slabs.

A team of locally based Gurkha soldiers spent seven days cutting and positioning the stones. A time capsule containing the names and remarks of supporters was buried at the point marking the horse’s heart. In 2004, the Friends of the Folkestone White Horse was formed to maintain the figure, which requires periodic weeding to keep the chalk visible against the advancing grass.

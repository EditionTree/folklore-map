Francis Drake

On his deathbed, Sir Francis Drake vowed that his drum would summon him back if England faced danger. Phantom drumming from Drake's Drum has been reported at moments of national crisis ever since.

The Legend

Sir Francis Drake, the Elizabethan sea-captain, circumnavigator and scourge of the Spanish, retired to Buckland Abbey on the edge of Dartmoor, and Devon folklore wove dark legends around him. The most famous concerns Drake's Drum, the snare-drum he carried around the world and which, on his deathbed off Panama in 1596, he vowed that if England were ever in danger someone should beat, and he would return to defend her. The earliest written record of the legend is George Lipscomb's 1799 travel account, though the story only became widely known after Sir Henry Newbolt's stirring 1897 poem 'Drake's Drum' carried it to a much wider audience; the drum itself, kept at Buckland, was reportedly heard of its own accord at Trafalgar in 1805 and again in 1914.

The privateer Sir Francis Drake is tied to Buckland Abbey through the legend of Drake's Drum

The Figure Behind the Legend

Other tales darkened him into a near-wizard who had won his sea-craft from the Devil: he was said to have conjured the storm that wrecked the Armada by cutting a stick into chips that became fireships, and to have brought water to Plymouth by charming a stream to follow his horse across the moor. The line between the historical admiral and the Devon folk-sorcerer all but vanished, leaving Drake as a guardian-hero forever waiting, like Arthur, to come again at his country's need.

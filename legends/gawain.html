Gawain

The most courteous of Arthur's knights, whose strange bargain with the Green Knight tested not his sword arm but his honour — and found it, after a moment's weakness, intact. His strength waxed with the morning sun and waned at noon, a hint of something older than chivalry in his blood.

A visual interpretation of Gawain leaving the Green Chapel after his test
The Legend

Sir Gawain is among the oldest and most celebrated of King Arthur's knights, a nephew of the king who appears in Welsh tradition as Gwalchmai before passing into the wider European romances. In the earliest tellings he is the very pattern of chivalry — the most courteous of the Round Table, a champion whose reputation for honour and gentleness stands above his prowess in arms. A curious older trait clings to him: his strength was said to wax with the rising sun and reach its height at noon, then ebb away as the day declined, a solar quality that hints at a mythic, perhaps divine, origin beneath the courtly surface.

His strange bargain with the Green Knight tested not his sword arm but his honour.

The Knight Whose Honour Was Tested

His finest hour comes in the 14th-century English poem Sir Gawain and the Green Knight. When a monstrous green stranger rides into Camelot at Christmas and offers any knight a blow of his axe on condition he receive one in return a year later, it is Gawain who takes up the challenge and strikes off the giant's head — only for the Green Knight to lift it up and ride away, reminding Gawain of his bargain. The poem follows Gawain's lonely journey the next winter to keep his word, knowing it means his death.

At the Green Chapel the test proves to be not of his sword arm but of his honour. Lodged with a lord whose lady tries daily to seduce him, Gawain resists everything but a green girdle he accepts and hides because it is said to protect his life. For that single lapse the Green Knight's axe nicks his neck — no more — and Gawain rides home wearing the girdle ever after as a badge of his shame. It is a deeply human portrait: the perfect knight found honest enough to fail, and brave enough to own it.

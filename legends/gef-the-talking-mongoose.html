Gef the Talking Mongoose

From 1931 the Irving family of Doarlish Cashen, a lonely farm above Dalby on the Isle of Man, said they shared their house with a talking mongoose who called himself Gef. He scratched behind the panelling, sang, swore, ran errands and reported back what the neighbours were saying. The case drew the psychical researcher Harry Price and became the strangest British newspaper sensation of the 1930s.

The Legend

Doarlish Cashen was a stone farmhouse high on the slope above Dalby, exposed, remote and without electricity, where James Irving farmed with his wife Margaret and their daughter Voirrey. In the autumn of 1931 the family began to hear scratching and rustling behind the matchboard panelling of the walls. Within months the noises had become a voice, and the voice had a name and a biography. He was Gef, born in India in 1852, a mongoose, and by his own account an extra clever mongoose.

The Story

What made the case famous was how domestic it became. Gef stayed for years. He killed rabbits and left them for the family, rode the bus to Peel to gather gossip and reported it back, screamed abuse at visitors he disliked and sang hymns. James Irving kept a detailed diary of all of it. The Manx press took the story up, then the London papers, and Gef became known across the island as the Dalby Spook.

The investigators arrived in 1935. Harry Price, the best known psychical researcher in Britain, came with Richard Lambert, editor of the BBC magazine The Listener, and the two published The Haunting of Cashen's Gap in 1936. Neither of them heard Gef. Hair said to be his went to Julian Huxley and then to the naturalist F. Martin Duncan, who identified it as dog hair, probably from the family sheepdog Mona. Voirrey Irving, a shy girl of thirteen when it began, was widely suspected of ventriloquism and denied it for the rest of her life. The farmhouse was pulled down in the early 1970s and nothing of it stands above ground, so the location shown here is the farm's recorded position on the slope above Dalby rather than a surveyed footprint. The case has never been satisfactorily explained and has never quite gone away.

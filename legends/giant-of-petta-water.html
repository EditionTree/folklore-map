Giants Petta Water and Kneefell, Shetland Mainland, Scotland

Giant of Petta Water

A Shetland giant tried to carry off the trows pestering him, but his kishie broke and scattered them across the island. His footprint became Petta Water; where he knelt became Kneefell.

The Legend

Petta Water, a small loch on the Shetland mainland, takes its name from the word for pit or hollow, and local tradition explains the hollow by way of a giant. The loch already had a fearsome reputation by 1899, when the folklorist John Spence recorded it as the chief haunt of trows, the mischievous underground spirits of Shetland and Orkney, noting that to pass Pettawater alone after nightfall required an extraordinary nerve.

His kishie basket broke under the trows' weight and scattered them across the landscape.

The Broken Basket of Trows

The giant himself lived near the water and was plagued beyond endurance by the trows pestering him. He resolved to be rid of them for good, gathered as many as he could into a kishie, the traditional Shetland basket carried on the back, and set off to throw them into the sea. But the trows were too many or too heavy, the kishie broke, and the scattered trows ran back to their underground haunts across the island. In his frustration the giant knelt, and the hollow his knee pressed into the ground became Kneefell; the pit his foot made, or the basin of his footprint, became Petta Water itself. The folklorist Jessie M. E. Saxby recorded this fuller telling of the tale in her Shetland Traditional Lore of 1932.

The legend belongs to a pattern common across Orkney and Shetland of landscape features explained by the actions of giants, trows and other supernatural beings, a geography of folklore that reads every unusual hill, loch or hollow as the trace of something that happened before memory. It also reflects the distinctive Shetland and Orkney tradition of trows as a constant, domestic, almost neighbourly supernatural presence rather than the dramatic terrors of the Highland tradition.

Glaistig

GLAS-tig

The glaistig — grey-clad, goat-legged — haunted Highland bogs and lochs: she could be a cattle-herding guardian who accepted a nightly bowl of milk, or a seductress who coaxed men to dance until she drained their blood; which version appeared depended entirely on whether you left the milk offering.

A visual interpretation of the protective Glaistig tending Highland cattle
The Legend

The glaistig (also glastig, literally 'grey woman' or 'green woman') is one of the most ambivalent supernatural figures in Highland Scottish tradition, combining the attributes of a benevolent guardian and a predatory spirit in a single form. She is described in Gaelic oral sources and in the nineteenth-century collections of J.F. Campbell and Alexander Carmichael as appearing as a beautiful woman of grey or green complexion, wearing a long flowing dress that conceals her most revealing feature: the legs and hooves of a goat. The hybrid form — human from the waist up, goat below — marks her as belonging to neither the world of humans nor the animal world entirely.

She could be protective — herding cattle and caring for children — or predatory.

The Keeper of the Cattle

The glaistig's behaviour fell into two modes depending entirely on context. In her benevolent aspect she attached herself to a farmstead and served as an invisible herder of the cattle, bringing them home, guarding them through the night, and preventing straying. She also watched over children left in her care. Households acknowledging her presence would leave a bowl of milk by the doorstep each evening — an offering too rich or too poor risked offending her. In her malevolent aspect she haunted lonely lochs and river crossings, drawing men with her beauty, luring them to dance with her, and then opening a vein with her nails to drink their blood. The transition between guardian and predator could be triggered by disrespect, neglect of the milk offering, or simply bad fortune.

The glaistig tradition is richest in Argyll and the Western Isles, with named glastigean attached to specific farms recorded in the Scottish Gaelic folklore archive. John Gregorson Campbell's Superstitions of the Highlands and Islands of Scotland (1900) contains some of the fullest documentation. Some scholars have connected the glaistig with the broader class of Irish and Scottish fairy women — the leanan sídhe and bean sídhe — who serve as both muse and destroyer; others see in the milk-offering element a survival of an older pastoral cult of cattle-protection that predates the Christian era.

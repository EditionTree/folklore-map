Glaistig

GLAS-tig

The glaistig, grey-clad and goat-legged, haunted Highland bogs and lochs: she could be a cattle-herding guardian who accepted a nightly bowl of milk, or a seductress who coaxed men to dance until she drained their blood; which version appeared depended entirely on whether you left the milk offering.

The Legend

The glaistig (also glastig, 'grey woman' or 'green woman') appears in Gaelic oral tradition and in the nineteenth-century collections of J.F. Campbell and Alexander Carmichael as a beautiful woman of grey or green complexion, wearing a long flowing dress that conceals her most telling feature: the legs and hooves of a goat. That hybrid form, human from the waist up and goat below, marks her as belonging fully to neither the human world nor the animal one, and her behaviour was just as divided, a household's most useful guardian on one day and its most dangerous predator the next.

She could be protective, herding cattle and caring for children, or predatory.

The Keeper of the Cattle

The glaistig's behaviour fell into two modes depending entirely on context. In her benevolent aspect she attached herself to a farmstead and served as an invisible herder of the cattle, bringing them home, guarding them through the night, and preventing straying. She also watched over children left in her care. Households acknowledging her presence would leave a bowl of milk by the doorstep each evening, and an offering too rich or too poor risked offending her. In her malevolent aspect she haunted lonely lochs and river crossings, drawing men with her beauty, luring them to dance with her, and then opening a vein with her nails to drink their blood. The transition between guardian and predator could be triggered by disrespect, neglect of the milk offering, or simply bad fortune.

The glaistig tradition is richest in Argyll and the Western Isles, with named glastigean attached to specific farms recorded in the Scottish Gaelic folklore archive. John Gregorson Campbell's Superstitions of the Highlands and Islands of Scotland (1900) contains some of the fullest documentation. Some scholars have connected the glaistig with the broader class of Irish and Scottish fairy women (the leanan sídhe and bean sídhe) who serve as both muse and destroyer; others see in the milk-offering element a survival of an older pastoral cult of cattle-protection that predates the Christian era.

Glastonbury Tor

The sacred hill rising from the Somerset Levels — said to be the Isle of Avalon where Arthur was carried after his final battle. Beneath it lies the entrance to Annwn, the Celtic otherworld, ruled by Gwyn ap Nudd.

A visual interpretation of Glastonbury Tor as the island of Avalon
The Legend

Glastonbury Tor rises 158 metres from the flat expanse of the Somerset Levels, a terraced conical hill crowned by the roofless tower of the medieval Church of St Michael. A millennium ago, when the water table was higher and the surrounding marshes flooded regularly, the Tor would have appeared as an island — and it is this island character that anchors its identification with Avalon, the place where King Arthur was carried after his mortal wounding at the Battle of Camlann. Geoffrey of Monmouth named Avalon the ‘Isle of Apples’ and described it as a place of healing presided over by Morgan le Fay; later writers, including the monks of Glastonbury Abbey, cemented the connection by claiming to have discovered Arthur’s grave in the abbey grounds in 1191.

An entrance to the fairy underworld is hidden in its flanks.

The Hill Beyond the Reeds

But the Tor’s mythology runs deeper than Arthur. In Welsh tradition it is the palace of Gwyn ap Nudd, Lord of Annwn — the Celtic otherworld, a realm parallel to the living world where the dead and the fair folk coexist. A hidden cave beneath the hill is said to open into Annwn, and those who enter may find the Cauldron of Rebirth but will struggle to return. St Collen, a sixth-century Welsh saint, is said to have descended into the Tor and confronted Gwyn ap Nudd in his fairy court, flinging holy water at the assembled spirits until they vanished and left him standing alone on the bare hillside.

The terraces that spiral around the Tor’s slopes have been interpreted by some researchers as the remains of a Neolithic processional labyrinth — a ceremonial path winding seven times around the hill to the summit. Whether this interpretation is correct or the terraces are simply medieval strip lynchets, they give the Tor an unmistakable sense of deliberate shaping. The ruined tower of St Michael’s at the summit dates from the fourteenth century, built to replace an earlier church destroyed by earthquake in 1275. The National Trust, which manages the site, records that Glastonbury Tor remains one of the most visited sacred landscapes in Britain.

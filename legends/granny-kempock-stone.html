Granny Kempock Stone

A hooded standing stone above Gourock harbour, walked round seven times by newlyweds and sailors for luck — and, in 1662, the alleged target of a witch coven who tried to topple it into the sea.

The Legend

On a low cliff behind Kempock Street in Gourock stands a six-foot block of mica schist, weathered into a shape that locals have long read as a hooded old woman bent over — hence "Granny" Kempock. Its origins are unknown: suggestions over the years have ranged from a prehistoric standing stone or altar to a memorial marking an ancient battle, but no record explains who first gave it its name or its grandmotherly nickname.

Sailors and newlyweds once circled the stone for luck before facing the sea.

The Luck-Giving Stone

For generations the stone was treated as a source of luck for anyone setting out on a journey by sea or into marriage. Fishermen about to sail, and couples newly wed, would carry a bucket of sand up from the shore and walk around the stone seven times against the sun (withershins) while chanting, in the belief that this secured Granny Kempock's blessing for a safe voyage or a fortunate marriage — a wedding in the district was said to be unlucky if the couple skipped the circuit.

The stone's reputation took a darker turn in 1662, during one of Scotland's many witch-hunts. Mary Lamont, a teenage servant from nearby Innerkip, confessed — after questioning — to having met with a coven of local women who danced around the Kempock Stone on the sabbath and plotted to drag it into the Firth of Clyde, believing this would raise a storm to wreck ships and ruin the local fishing fleet. Lamont and several of her co-accused were burned for witchcraft later that year, a real and brutal episode that has since become inseparable from the stone's folklore, turning a stone for blessing sailors into one supposedly capable, in the wrong hands, of sinking them.

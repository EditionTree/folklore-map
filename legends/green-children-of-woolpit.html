Green Children of Woolpit

In the 12th century, two children with green skin emerged from pits near Woolpit, speaking an unknown tongue. The girl survived and spoke of an underground land called St Martin's Land.

The Legend

In 12th-century Woolpit, Suffolk, harvesters in the fields came upon two children, a boy and a girl, standing bewildered by the wolf-pits that gave the village its name. Two chroniclers of the period, Ralph of Coggeshall and William of Newburgh, separately recorded what happened next: the children were ordinary in every way but one: their skin was entirely green. They wore clothes of an unfamiliar material, spoke no language anyone could understand, and at first would eat nothing but raw broad beans.

The Otherworld at the Heart of the Tale

Taken in by the villagers and by the local knight, the boy sickened and died, but the girl survived. As she learned to eat ordinary food her green colour gradually faded, she was baptised, and in time she learned to speak English. When she could be understood, she told an extraordinary story of where they had come from: a twilit underground country she called Saint Martin's Land, where everything and everyone was green, lit by no bright sun but a perpetual gloaming. She and her brother had been minding their father's flocks, she said, when they followed the sound of bells into a cavern and emerged, dazzled, into the fields of Woolpit, unable to find their way back.

What lies behind the account has been argued over for centuries — a folk memory of fairy-folk emerging from the Otherworld; a garbled tale of lost or migrant children, perhaps Flemish, suffering from the greenish pallor of malnutrition (chlorosis, the 'green sickness'); or a pure marvel-story of the kind medieval chroniclers loved to collect. The girl, in some versions, grew up, married and settled in the area. Whatever its origin, the legend has kept Woolpit on the map of the uncanny, and the village sign today proudly bears the two green children.

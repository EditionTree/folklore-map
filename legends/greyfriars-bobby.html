Greyfriars Bobby

A Skye Terrier said to have kept a fourteen year vigil over the Greyfriars Kirkyard grave of his master, Edinburgh night watchman John Gray, after Gray's death in February 1858. Edinburgh's council granted the dog a licensed collar in 1867 rather than have him destroyed as a stray, and after his own death in 1872 the philanthropist Angela Burdett Coutts funded the bronze statue that still stands opposite the kirkyard gate. A 2011 study of surviving photographs concluded the vigil may in fact have involved two different dogs, cared for by the graveyard's caretaker as a way of drawing tips from visitors.

The Legend

John Gray worked as a night watchman for the Edinburgh City Police and kept a Skye Terrier named Bobby for company on his rounds. When Gray died of tuberculosis in February 1858, he was buried in Greyfriars Kirkyard, and tradition holds that Bobby refused to leave the graveside, sleeping on or near the grave through all weather for the rest of his life.

The Story

By 1867 an Edinburgh bylaw required every dog to carry a licence or be destroyed as a stray, and the city's Lord Provost, Sir William Chalmers, paid for Bobby's licence himself rather than see the graveyard dog put down. The collar he received, inscribed 'Greyfriars Bobby from the Lord Provost 1867, Licensed', survives in the Museum of Edinburgh today. Bobby died in January 1872 and was buried just inside the kirkyard gate, close to Gray. The philanthropist Angela Georgina Burdett Coutts asked the council for permission to commemorate him, and the sculptor William Brodie's bronze statue and drinking fountain was unveiled opposite the kirkyard in November 1873.

The story has not gone unchallenged. James Brown, the kirkyard's caretaker through these years, was the first to tell visitors the loyal dog story, and contemporary newspapers noted he did well from the tips it earned him. In 2011 the historian Jan Bondeson compared surviving photographs of Bobby and found that the earliest, from 1867, shows an elderly, rough coated mongrel with cataracts, while later portraits show a younger, well groomed Skye Terrier, suggesting the dog visitors knew as Bobby across fourteen years may in fact have been two animals, neither of them provably connected to John Gray at all. Whichever version is closer to the truth, the statue, Bobby's own small grave marker, and his collar in the museum have made him one of Edinburgh's most visited small monuments.

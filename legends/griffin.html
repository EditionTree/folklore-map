Griffin

A lion-bodied, eagle-headed guardian of treasure, enemy of horses, and one of the great beasts of British heraldry. Its talons were said to detect poison by changing colour. Medieval bestiaries treated it as real; its image appears on coats of arms, city crests, and pub signs across the country.

A visual interpretation of the griffin as a vigilant guardian of treasure
The Legend

The griffin — lion in body and hindquarters, eagle in head, wings and foretalons — is among the most ancient of legendary beasts, and entered British tradition above all through heraldry. Combining the king of beasts with the king of birds, it was held to be a creature of surpassing strength and vigilance, and so became a favourite emblem of valour and watchful guardianship on coats of arms, gateposts and church carvings across Britain.

The Guardian with Eagle Eyes

Older bestiary lore gave the griffin a fierce character: it was the sworn enemy of the horse, it guarded hoards of gold in the mountains of the far north, and cups carved from its claws (in reality antelope horn or fossil) were prized because they were believed to detect and neutralise poison. In heraldry the male griffin is sometimes shown rayed with spikes and without wings, distinguishing him from the winged female. As guardian and treasure-keeper, the griffin carried into the medieval imagination a memory far older than Britain itself.

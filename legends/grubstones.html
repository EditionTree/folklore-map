Grubstones

Ordnance Survey misheard the local "Rum stones" as "Grubstones," but older tradition called this Yorkshire circle Roms Law, closing its Rogation Day gathering with the words "This is Rumbles Law!"

The Legend

The stone circle on Burley Moor now called the Grubstones owes its name to a mishearing. When Ordnance Survey surveyors recorded the site, they mistook the guttural local pronunciation of "Rum stones" for "grub stones," and the mistranslation stuck. Older tradition knew the circle, an embanked ring or possibly a ring cairn, by a different name entirely: Roms Law, or Rumbles Law, from old words for a "clearing" or "meeting place" and a burial mound, together meaning something like "the place of the dead" or "the moot."

The low stones mark an ancient ring in the exposed Yorkshire heather.

The Moorland Ring

That older name points to how the circle was actually used. Local people once gathered here on Rogation Day, a pre-Christian agricultural festival closely tied to Beltane, where a central stone served as a kind of pulpit. An orator addressed the gathered crowd from it, closing the ceremony with the words "This is Rumbles Law!" A trackway leading to the circle has been read as a ceremonial "road of the dead," along which the ancestors of those gathered were once carried to rest nearby.

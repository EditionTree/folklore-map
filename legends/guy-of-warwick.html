Legendary Figures Warwick and Guy's Cliffe, Warwickshire, England

Guy of Warwick

A steward's son who slew a monstrous cow, defeated a dragon, and beat a giant champion in single combat to save England from Danish tribute, then renounced it all to die a hermit in a cave.

The Legend

Guy of Warwick, a steward's son, won renown and the hand of the noble Felice through years of marvellous feats across Europe and beyond. Knighted and sent abroad under the guidance of his mentor Herhaud, he defeated Duke Otous of Pavia in a Norman tournament, helped Duke Segyn break a siege laid against him, and travelled as far as Constantinople to help fight off an invading Saracen army. Along the way he rescued Sir Tirri of Gormoise and his lover Oisel from the clutches of Duke Loyere, and the two men swore themselves brothers in arms.

he ended his life as a hermit at Guy's Cliffe

The Hero Becomes a Hermit

Back in England Guy added still more to his legend: he slew the monstrous Dun Cow, a giant beast that had long ravaged the country, killed a fearsome dragon in Northumberland, and, fighting as a Saracen king's champion, overcame the giant Amorant. His most celebrated feat came at Winchester, where he met the gigantic Danish champion Colbrand in single combat and felled him, delivering England from the tribute the Danes had demanded.

At the height of his fame Guy renounced it all. Stricken with remorse, he made a pilgrimage to the Holy Land and returned to England unrecognised, keeping his identity hidden even from Felice. Their son Reinbrun, stolen away by merchants as a child, grew up apart from his father and was only reunited with him years later, tracked down through a chain of combat encounters by Guy's old companion Herhaud. Guy himself lived out his last days as a hermit in a cave at Guy's Cliffe near Warwick, revealing who he was to Felice only as he lay dying. The cave and chapel at Guy's Cliffe, and a great rib long displayed as the Dun Cow's, kept his legend rooted in the Warwickshire landscape for centuries, first written down as the Anglo-Norman 'Gui de Warewic' shortly before 1204, and retold in Middle English verse from around 1300 onward.

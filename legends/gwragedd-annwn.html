Gwragedd Annwn

GWRAH-geth AN-oon

Beautiful fairies rise from Welsh mountain lakes to marry mortal men on one condition: strike them three causeless blows, even by accident, and they vanish beneath the water with all their cattle.

The Legend

Wives or maidens of Annwn, the Welsh otherworld ruled by the fairy king Gwyn ap Nudd, the Gwragedd Annwn are the lake-fairies of Wales's mountain tarns, gentler in temperament than the drowning water-spirits found elsewhere in the British Isles. Sightings cluster around isolated upland lakes: Llyn Barfog above Aberdyfi, Crumlyn Lake near Briton Ferry, and, most famously, Llyn y Fan Fach in the Black Mountain.

The Otherworld at the Heart of the Tale

At Llyn Barfog the fairies were remembered together with their herd, the gwartheg y llyn, magical cattle that rose from the water to graze the shore. One story tells how a farmer caught a stray gwartheg cow and kept her; her milk, butter and cheese so far outstripped ordinary cattle's that some said it could heal wounds and lift the spirits of anyone who drank it. When age finally made him think of slaughtering her for meat, a woman in green appeared at the water's edge, called the cow and calf away, and every beast of that herd followed her into the lake save one calf that had already turned jet black. Welsh tradition holds that single black calf as the ancestor of the native Welsh Black cattle breed still farmed today.

The best-known tale by far is the Lady of Llyn y Fan Fach, who agreed to marry a young farmer on one condition: he must never strike her three causeless blows. Years passed, and three light, accidental taps, at a christening, a wedding and a funeral, fulfilled the geas without his ever meaning harm, and she returned to the lake with her entire dowry of cattle. She had already borne him sons, and those sons became the Physicians of Myddfai, a real medieval medical family whose herbal remedies were said to be her parting gift to the mortal world she left behind.

Historical Context

Origins & Dating

Historical PeriodEarly-19th-century Welsh antiquarian tradition (1804-13 earliest record); the associated Physicians of Myddfai were a real medieval medical family
Earliest Written RecordRichard Fenton's 'Tours in Wales' (1804-1813) is the earliest modern written account of the Lady of Llyn y Fan Fach story identified; the tale was further published in 'The Cambro-Briton' periodical (1821) and reached its fullest form as the introduction to William Rees's 'Meddygon Myddfai' (1861), itself based on oral accounts collected in 1841 from three elderly parish residents

Folklore dates are often approximate, especially where a story predates any surviving written source.

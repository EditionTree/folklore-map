Gwy

GOO-ee

The second daughter of the giant Plynlimon, who raced her sisters from the mountain summit to the sea. She became the River Wye — threading a quiet, winding path through borderland woods while her sisters rushed and roared.

A visual interpretation of Gwy in Welsh Marches
The Legend

In the folklore of mid Wales, the mountain Pumlumon (Plynlimon) is father to three daughters — river spirits or Niskai in the Celtic tradition — who were set a challenge: each was given a single day to reach the sea, and whichever territory they covered before nightfall would be their dowry forever. The legend was first recorded by the Celtic scholar John Rhŷs, who heard it told in his youth in 1840s Ceredigion, and was later retold and analysed by Arthur Granville Bradley in 1920.

The Divine Presence

Gwy, the second sister, chose the path of beauty and contemplation. Where Hafren (the Severn) set out ambitiously eastward on a vast 220-mile journey through England, and Rheidol ran wild and short straight to Cardigan Bay, Gwy meandered south through the borderlands of Wales and England. She threaded her way through the wooded gorges of what is now the Wye Valley, lingering in the quiet stretches between Builth Wells and Hay-on-Wye before curving down through Herefordshire and the Forest of Dean to meet her sister Hafren at the Severn Estuary. Her 134-mile journey made her neither the longest nor the shortest of the three sisters, but the most serene.

The three-sisters legend belongs to a broader tradition of mythologising Welsh river origins through kinship narratives, giving landscape features the emotional logic of family relationships. The River Wye itself has been a designated Area of Outstanding Natural Beauty since 1971, and its passage through the border country — neither wholly Welsh nor wholly English — echoes the liminal character that the folklore assigns to Gwy: a spirit who chose to explore the boundary rather than commit to one side.

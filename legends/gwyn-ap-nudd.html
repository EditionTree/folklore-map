Gwyn ap Nudd

GWIN ap NEETH

King of the Tylwyth Teg and ruler of Annwn — his glass castle lies within Glastonbury Tor, where Saint Collen drove him out with holy water. He leads the Cwn Annwn on the Wild Hunt and fights an eternal battle for a woman's hand every May Day until the world's end.

A visual interpretation of Gwyn ap Nudd in Somerset / Wales
The Legend

Gwyn ap Nudd is one of the great shadow-kings of Welsh myth: lord of Annwn, the Celtic Otherworld, and king of the Tylwyth Teg, the fair folk. His name means 'Gwyn son of Nudd', linking him to the old British god Nodens, and the medieval Welsh remembered him as a figure of terrible power whom God set to rule the demons of the underworld lest they destroy mankind. He rides at the head of the Cŵn Annwn, the spectral white hounds with red ears whose baying on a winter night is the sound of the Wild Hunt sweeping souls of the dead toward his realm.

The Divine Presence

In the Welsh tradition Gwyn is bound to an eternal contest. For carrying off Creiddylad, daughter of Lludd, he and his rival Gwythyr ap Greidawl are condemned to fight for her every May Day until the Day of Judgement, when the final victor will claim her at last — a myth that reads as the endless seasonal war of summer and winter. His hunting-grounds were said to lie across the high wild places of Wales and the West, and folk belief planted his Otherworld court within the hollow hill of Glastonbury Tor.

It is at the Tor that his most famous Christian-era legend unfolds. The 12th-century life of Saint Collen tells how the hermit was summoned up the hill to a glittering castle where Gwyn sat enthroned amid music, fine food and courtiers in red and blue. Collen, refusing the fairy feast as a snare, flung holy water about him — and in an instant the whole shining court vanished, leaving only the bare green hilltop and the wind. The tale marks the moment the old pagan king was driven underground by the new faith, yet his name still clings to the Tor as the door to the land of the dead.

Hafren

Britain’s longest river, named for Sabrina — a princess drowned in its waters by a wicked stepmother and transformed into a river goddess. Milton gave her immortal verse in Comus; the Welsh still call the Severn Afon Hafren.

The Legend

Hafren is the Welsh name for the River Severn, and the legend behind it is one of the oldest origin stories in British mythology. According to Geoffrey of Monmouth’s twelfth-century Historia Regum Britanniae, Hafren was the daughter of Locrinus, legendary king of Britain, and his secret mistress Estrildis, a Germanic princess whom Locrinus had concealed in underground chambers for seven years while married to Gwendolen, daughter of the Cornish king Corineus. When Corineus died, Locrinus abandoned Gwendolen and elevated Estrildis to queen. Gwendolen raised an army in Cornwall, defeated and killed Locrinus in battle, then ordered both Estrildis and the innocent Hafren drowned in the river that would thereafter bear the girl’s name.

The Story

The Latinised form Sabrina entered English literature through Geoffrey’s text and achieved its most celebrated expression in John Milton’s masque Comus (1634), where Sabrina rises from the river as a benevolent water goddess to free a maiden from enchantment: ‘Sabrina fair, / Listen where thou art sitting / Under the glassie, cool, translucent wave.’ Milton’s portrayal transformed the drowned princess into a protective nymph, and the name Sabrina has been used for the river in English poetry ever since.

In the separate Welsh folklore tradition recorded by John Rhŷs, Hafren is one of three daughters of the mountain giant Plynlimon, who raced her sisters Gwy and Rheidol to the sea. Hafren chose the longest and most ambitious route — 220 miles eastward through the English Midlands before curving back to the Bristol Channel — covering more territory than either sibling. The two traditions are distinct in origin but have coexisted for centuries, giving the Severn a double mythological identity: a river of tragedy in the Galfridian chronicle and a river of ambition in Welsh mountain folklore.

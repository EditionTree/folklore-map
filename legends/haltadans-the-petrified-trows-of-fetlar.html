Haltadans, the Petrified Trows of Fetlar

A stone circle on Fetlar marks the spot where a band of trows danced so wildly to a captured fiddler's tune that dawn caught and turned them all to stone.

The Legend

On the small Shetland island of Fetlar stands a low stone circle known as the Haltadans, Shetland dialect for 'limping dance'. According to island tradition, the stones are the petrified remains of a band of trows — the mischievous, sometimes giant-like fairy folk of Orkney and Shetland lore — caught by daylight after a night of wild revelry. The story tells how the trows dragged a human fiddler into their circle at the height of a summer night and forced him to play tune after tune, growing wilder with every reel; the trows danced their distinctive 'henking' step, hopping on bent legs with hands locked between knees and calves, moving strangely to human eyes yet keeping perfect time with the music. So absorbed were they in the dance that they failed to notice the approach of dawn, and were turned to stone where they stood — the ring of stones said to be the trows themselves, with two outlying stones representing the fiddler and his wife.

The Story

The name Haltadans was recorded and analysed by the Faroese-Danish philologist Jakob Jakobsen during his pioneering survey of Shetland place-names and dialect at the turn of the twentieth century, who linked the 'limping' element of the name to the traditional belief that trows and fairies danced with an awkward, halting gait. The site sits within a broader Shetland tradition of trow-lore, in which trows are variously described as monstrous giants or as small grey-clad fairy folk, and are blamed for everything from mischief to outright peril for anyone who lingers too long near their mounds after dark.

The Haltadans remains a rare case of a British stone circle whose name and associated legend were transmitted together through direct oral and etymological record, rather than being a modern label applied to an unexplained monument.

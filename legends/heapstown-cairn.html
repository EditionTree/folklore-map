Heapstown Cairn

Heapstown Cairn is a large ancient cairn and protected National Monument in County Sligo, Ireland. It was built in the Neolithic, around 3000 BC, and is believed to cover a passage tomb. The cairn is one of the largest in Ireland and is near Carrowkeel Megalithic Cemetery.

The Legend

Heapstown Cairn is one of the largest prehistoric cairns in Ireland, a vast Neolithic stone mound in County Sligo, believed to cover a passage tomb and standing within the wider sacred landscape of the Cúil Irra peninsula and the Carrowkeel tombs. But to Irish legend it is no mere burial place: it is bound up with the climactic myth of the Tuatha Dé Danann, the Second Battle of Moytura, fought (in tradition) on the nearby plains against the monstrous Fomorians.

The great cairn holds its place in the Sligo landscape like a memory made of stone.

The Cairn in the Sligo Fields

The legend tells of a magical well of healing, the Well of Sláine, tended by Dian Cécht, the great physician-god of the Tuatha Dé Danann, and his children. Into its waters the slain and wounded warriors of the Danann host were plunged during the battle, to be restored to life and health and sent back into the fight — a power that gave the Tuatha Dé Danann a terrible advantage. When the Fomorians discovered the secret of the well, they resolved to destroy it: each of their warriors carried a stone from the river and cast it upon the spring until the healing water was buried and stopped forever beneath a colossal heap of rock — and that heap, the tradition says, is Heapstown Cairn.

In later legend the cairn was also claimed as the upright grave of Ailill, a king of Connacht, buried standing in the manner of Queen Medb on nearby Knocknarea. Whether read as the smothered well of the healing god or the tomb of a king, Heapstown is a place where one of the grandest of Irish mythological battles is fixed to the land — a great silent mound said to hide a spring that once raised the dead.

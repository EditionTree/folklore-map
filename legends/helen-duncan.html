Helen Duncan

Convicted in 1944 as Britain's last accused witch, medium Helen Duncan announced a warship's sinking before the Admiralty did — and was jailed under a 1735 law just years from being scrapped.

The Legend

Victoria Helen Duncan, born in Perthshire in 1897, held séances through the 1920s and 30s in which she appeared to produce 'ectoplasm' — a substance said to let spirits take form. Investigators found it was nothing more than cheesecloth, paper, and egg white, swallowed before sittings and regurgitated during them; in 1933 she was convicted of fraud in Edinburgh after a sitter seized her 'spirit guide' and found it to be a stockinette undervest stretched over her head.

The Séance That Reached the Courts

Despite this, Duncan retained a loyal following, and it was a séance during the Second World War that sealed her fate. She announced that a sailor's spirit had told her his ship, HMS Barham, had been sunk — news the Admiralty was deliberately suppressing to protect morale. Whether she had picked up wartime gossip or genuinely unsettled the authorities, she was arrested in 1944 and, remarkably, prosecuted not for fraud but under the Witchcraft Act of 1735, a statute over two centuries old. Winston Churchill himself complained about the 'obsolete tomfoolery' of tying up the courts with a witchcraft trial in wartime, but Duncan was convicted and served nine months in Holloway prison.

Her case became a rallying point for spiritualists and an embarrassment for the legal system, contributing to the repeal of the Witchcraft Act in 1951 and its replacement with the more narrowly targeted Fraudulent Mediums Act. Duncan continued giving séances after her release, and died in 1956 days after police raided one of her sittings in Nottingham — her supporters maintained that the shock of the raid, mid-trance, had killed her.

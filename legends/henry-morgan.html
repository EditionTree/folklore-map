Henry Morgan

Henry Morgan sacked Panama in 1671, was arrested for it, and still ended up knighted, sent back to Jamaica to govern the colony he had once raided from.

The Legend

Sir Henry Morgan (c.1635-1688) was a Welsh privateer, the eldest son of Robert Morgan, squire of Llanrhymny near Cardiff, who spent two decades raiding Spanish ports and shipping across the Caribbean under commission from Jamaica, and around whom a great deal of buccaneering legend gathered. His early passage to the West Indies is shrouded in tales of kidnap and indenture, and by 1670 he commanded a force of some thirty-six ships and eighteen hundred men, one of the largest privateering fleets ever assembled in the region.

Later retellings turned the historical governor and plantation owner into a larger-than-life pirate figure.

The Privateer Who Became a Pirate Legend

In 1671 he marched his men across the Isthmus of Panama and burned the richest city on the Spanish Main. The sack of Panama made his name ring through Europe.

The raid came after peace had already been agreed between England and Spain, and Morgan was arrested and shipped to London in April 1672 to answer for it. Rather than face punishment, he found the political wind had shifted in his favour: in January 1674 Charles II knighted him and sent him back to Jamaica as lieutenant-governor, where he lived out his life respectable and wealthy. Popular legend, and a century of pirate fiction, recast the privateer as the archetypal buccaneer captain, blurring the careful line he himself drew between licensed privateer and outright pirate.

Hereford Revenant

Recorded by Walter Map around 1190: a dead Welshman returned nightly to call out his neighbours by name, and each one sickened and died within three days, until a knight named William Laudun had the corpse dug up and beheaded with a spade.

The Legend

Walter Map, a Welsh-border cleric who served as a courtier to Henry II and later became archdeacon of Oxford, set down in his De Nugis Curialium ('Courtiers' Trifles'), a miscellany compiled over decades and finished around 1190, a revenant tale from his own country. A knight named William Laudun came to the Bishop of Hereford in some distress, reporting that a Welshman of evil life had died unchristianly enough in his village and, four nights later, had begun returning after dark to wander it, calling aloud the names of his former neighbours one by one. Each man so summoned sickened and was dead within three days.

Recorded by Walter Map c

The Haunting at the Heart of the Tale

The bishop advised that the grave be reopened, the corpse's head struck off with a spade, the body sprinkled with holy water and reburied. When William carried this out, the hauntings ceased. Map, writing at almost the same moment as the northern chronicler William of Newburgh, presents the affair as recent and credible, valuable evidence that belief in the malignant, name-calling walking dead was alive along the English-Welsh border in the twelfth century, and not only in the cold north.

Map's account survives today in a single medieval manuscript, now held in the Bodleian Library at Oxford, among the earliest written records anywhere of the belief that digging up and beheading a suspect corpse could stop the restless dead from walking.

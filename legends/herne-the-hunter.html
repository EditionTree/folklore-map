Herne the Hunter

A spectral huntsman said to haunt Windsor Great Park, horned and rattling chains, leading a phantom hunt through the forest at midnight. First named in Shakespeare's Merry Wives of Windsor; his origins reach back further into the wild-hunt tradition.

The Legend

Herne the Hunter appears by name in Shakespeare's Merry Wives of Windsor (1597), where a character describes him as a gamekeeper who hanged himself from an oak in the park and now haunts it as a ghost with great ragged horns, blasting the trees and taking cattle. Whether Shakespeare invented the figure or drew on a genuine Windsor tradition is unknown; no reference to Herne survives from before the play.

The Ghost Beneath the Oak

Later writers tried to root him deeper in the past. The philologist Jacob Grimm was the first to argue, influentially, that Herne had once been imagined as leader of the Wild Hunt, the spectral chase of huntsman and hounds that crosses the sky in folklore across northern Europe, usually led by a figure such as Woden or Odin. Another theory, put forward by the scholar R. Lowe Thompson, traces the name back through Grimm's Law to the Gaulish horned god Cernunnos, though no firm evidence of Cernunnos worship has ever turned up in Britain itself, only on the continent. Neither theory can be proven, and both are guesswork built backward from a horned ghost first named by a playwright.

The novelist William Harrison Ainsworth gave the legend its most enduring extra detail in Windsor Castle (1843), inventing the story that the historical Herne was gored by a stag while out hunting, and that the Devil saved his life on condition he wear the animal's antlers ever after. It is Ainsworth's version, as much as Shakespeare's, that shaped the horned ghost now fixed in popular imagination.

Herne's Oak, the specific tree he was said to haunt, was pointed to and venerated for centuries, though never quite the same tree twice. One candidate was felled by order of George III in 1796; a replacement favoured by Queen Victoria blew down in a storm on 31 August 1863, and Victoria had a new tree planted in its place. That tree was itself removed in 1906 during a general replanting of the avenue, and one of the oaks planted that year now carries the name, the fourth or fifth tree in two centuries to answer to Herne's Oak.

Hob Hole, Runswick Bay

A hob was said to inhabit a cave at Runswick Bay. Local parents brought children suffering from whooping cough to the Hob Holes and called on the spirit to take the illness away.

A visual interpretation of Hob Hole, Runswick Bay
The Legend

Hob Hole is a sea-cave in the cliffs at Runswick Bay, on the rugged coast of North Yorkshire, and it was the dwelling-place, in local tradition, of a hob — one of the helpful but touchy household and nature spirits of northern England, kin to the brownie. This particular hob, however, was credited with a specific and valued power: he could cure whooping cough, the violent, frightening childhood illness that the old country people called 'kink-cough' or 'chin-cough'.

The Otherworld at the Heart of the Tale

When a child in the fishing community fell ill with whooping cough, its parents would carry it down to the shore and into the Hob Hole, and there call upon the spirit with a set rhyme, beseeching him to take the sickness away. The traditional invocation ran in dialect — addressing 'Hob Hole Hob' and asking him to rid the bairn of its cough — after which the family would carry the child home in hope of a cure. The ritual shows the hob in his role as a folk-healer, a local guardian spirit whose power could be tapped by those who approached him with the proper words and respect.

The Hob Hole tradition is a vivid example of the way coastal and rural communities wove their supernatural beliefs into the practical business of survival — health, healing and the warding-off of disease — and of the hob's place as a being who, properly addressed, might help the people whose coast he shared. Like all hobs he was also to be treated with caution, for these spirits could turn troublesome if slighted, but at Runswick Bay he is remembered above all as the curer of the children's cough.

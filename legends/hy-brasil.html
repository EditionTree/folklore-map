Hy-Brasil

A phantom island off Ireland's west coast, shrouded in mist and visible only once every seven years, appeared on European sea-charts from 1325 clear through to the 19th century.

The Legend

Hy-Brasil, also written Hy-Breasal, O Brasil, or Brazir, is one of the most persistently documented phantom islands in European cartography. It first appears on the portolan chart of the Genoese cartographer Angelino Dulcert in 1325, labelled 'Bracile' and drawn as a perfect circle off the west coast of Ireland, and mapmakers across Europe kept charting it in one form or another into the 19th century, long after most mythological islands had dropped off the charts entirely.

Hy-Brasil was said to appear only rarely before the sea and fog closed over it again.

The Island in the Mist

In Irish tradition the island is bound in mist and surfaces from the Atlantic only once every seven years, briefly visible but impossible to reach before it sinks again. Different tellings make it the dwelling place of the Tuatha De Danann, the divine race of pre-Christian Ireland, a land of perpetual youth and abundance, or the realm of an immortal philosopher-king. The name is thought to derive from Breasal, a mythical High King of the World in early Irish tradition. Several real expeditions went looking for it: a Bristol voyage is recorded as searching for the island in 1480, and in 1674 a Scottish sea captain named John Nisbet claimed to have actually landed there, describing ancient black rabbits and a silent old man whose appearance briefly broke the island's enchantment.

Scholars have offered various explanations for why the sightings persisted so long. Some connect the legend to the Porcupine Bank, a shallow submarine shelf west of Ireland occasionally exposed by extreme low tides, which could plausibly have produced genuine sightings of land that then vanished beneath the waves again. Others point to the deep cultural template already provided by Tir na nOg and the other Otherworld islands of early Irish mythology, Tir fo Thuinn, Mag Mell, Emain Ablach, into which recurring glimpses of Atlantic mirage were readily fitted. The name was later transferred to the country of Brazil by Portuguese navigators, though the exact route of that borrowing remains disputed among etymologists.

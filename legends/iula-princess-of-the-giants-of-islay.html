Iula, Princess of the Giants of Islay

A giant princess named Iula fled Denmark dropping enchanted stones from her apron, Ireland, Rathlin, Texa, until the last stone became Islay itself, and the storm finally claimed her.

The Legend

According to Hebridean tradition, Iula (also rendered Yula) was a princess of the giants who set out from her homeland of Denmark in search of adventure, travelling across the sea and carrying a great apron full of magical stones. As she journeyed, stones fell from her apron and became islands, one became Ireland, another Rathlin, and a third the small isle of Texa off Islay's southern coast.

A giant princess named Iula fled Denmark dropping enchanted stones from her apron.

The Princess and the Island-Stones

More stones spilled out to form the chain of islands stretching from Ardbeg to Kildalton on Islay's south-east shore. Finally, in a storm, Iula dropped her last and largest stone, which became the island of Islay itself, and was swept away and drowned by the same storm. Tradition holds that she was buried afterwards on a hilltop above Loch Cnoc.

The earliest written trace of the story is the (Old) Statistical Account of Scotland's 1794 entry for the Islay parish, which describes a standing stone as the 'grave of a daughter of one of the kings of Denmark'. The stones themselves, known as Clachan Ceann Ile, are classified by Historic Environment Scotland as genuinely prehistoric, though the organisation's own record is explicit that there is no supporting evidence for the Danish-princess legend attached to them, a useful reminder that the monument and the story wrapped around it may belong to entirely different ages.

The legend doubles as a folk-etymology for the island's name, Ila in Gaelic, said to derive from Iula herself, though scholars note the name's true root is pre-Gaelic and of unknown origin, recorded as 'Ilea' by Adomnán in the 7th century and as 'Íl' in Old Norse. The tale nonetheless remains one of the more vivid giant-origin stories of the Inner Hebrides, explaining an entire scatter of islands, not one, as the wreckage of a single giant's journey.

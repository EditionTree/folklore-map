Jenny Greenteeth

A river hag hides beneath the green duckweed of ponds and slow rivers across Lancashire, Cheshire and Shropshire, waiting to drag under any child who strays too close to the water's edge.

The Legend

Lancashire and north Staffordshire knew her as Jinny Greenteeth, a river hag long used to keep children from wandering too close to weed-choked ponds and slow-moving water. Across the county line in Cheshire and Shropshire, the same figure went by Wicked Jenny, Ginny Greenteeth or Jeannie Greenteeth. She lurks beneath the green scum of stagnant pools, the duckweed that gives her her name, and drags under anyone who strays too near with long sinewy arms, pallid green skin and teeth bared sharp behind cracked lips.

She drags the unwary - particularly children - to their deaths in the depths.

The Hag Beneath Still Water

William Henderson recorded her haunting the streams of Lancashire in his Notes on the Folk-Lore of the Northern Counties of England and the Borders (1866, revised 1879); a separate mid-nineteenth-century Preston tradition went further and described her riding a broomstick above the town's Lady Well. Her name is thought to trace back to Grindylow, itself rooted in older words like Grinteeth and Grendith, so that Jenny Greenteeth and the grindylows of Yorkshire may once have been the same creature under different regional tongues.

She belongs to a wider family of British water-bogies invented for a protective purpose rather than a narrative one. Peg Powler prowled the River Tees, the grindylows haunted Yorkshire's ponds, and each did the same job Jenny did, giving a vivid, memorable shape to the genuine danger of drowning. The weed she supposedly hid beneath was itself the real hazard, masking deep water as solid ground, and generations of mothers along the Mersey and its tributaries put her to work keeping their children back from the water's edge.

Jingling Geordie's Hole

A cave at Tynemouth associated with the miserly spirit Jingling Geordie, whose hoarded coins were said to rattle in the dark. A coastal pocket of ghost-story greed.

The Legend

Jingling Geordie's Hole is a cave in the cliffs at Tynemouth, on the Northumberland coast between King Edward's Bay and the headland of Tynemouth Castle and Priory. Originally known as 'Jingling Man's Hole', it takes its more familiar name from the legend of Jingling Geordie, a 17th-century pirate, smuggler and wrecker who is said to have used the cave as his lair and lookout over the treacherous waters off the coast.

The Story

In the tradition, Jingling Geordie was a deliberate wrecker of ships. From his vantage in the cave he would watch for vessels at sea and, on dark nights, set out lanterns positioned to mimic the lights of boats riding safely at anchor, luring ships onto the deadly rocks of the Black Middens. When the tricked vessels foundered and broke up, he would plunder the wrecked cargoes washed among the rocks and carry his booty away to hide in a supposed labyrinth of tunnels running beneath the castle headland.

The 'jingling' of his name is explained by the chains he wore: Geordie is said to have gone about with iron fetters or leg-irons clamped around his legs, which rattled and jingled with every step — and his ghost, still dragging its clinking chains, is reputed to haunt the cliffs and castle walls on dark evenings, keeping its old watch over the headland. (Antiquarians note rival explanations for the name, including a later real-life eccentric on stilts, but it is the wrecker's tale that clings to the cave.) The story is a characteristic piece of north-eastern coastal folklore, binding smuggling, deliberate wrecking and a chain-rattling ghost to a single dark hole in the Tynemouth cliffs.

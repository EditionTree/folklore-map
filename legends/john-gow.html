John Gow

John Gow, raised in Stromness, led a mutiny at sea in 1724 and returned to Orkney under an assumed name. Recognised, captured after running aground near the Calf of Eday, and hanged in London, twice over when the rope broke, he became the model for Walter Scott's novel The Pirate.

The Legend

John Gow was the pirate son of Orkney, raised in the harbour town of Stromness, whose brief and bloody career ended almost where it began. Serving as second mate aboard the merchant ship Caroline, he led a mutiny on the night of 3 November 1724 as the vessel sailed from Santa Cruz for Genoa with a cargo of beeswax, leather and woollens. Gow and his fellow conspirators stabbed and shot the captain, threw him overboard, renamed the ship Revenge, and turned pirate in the waters off Spain and Portugal.

John Gow was raised in Stromness and returned to Orkney under an assumed name after a short pirate career

The Figure Behind the Legend

He then made the fatal decision to sail north and return to Orkney, going under the alias 'Mr Smith' and renaming his ship the George, only to be recognised among people who had known him as a boy. Exposed, he raided the Hall of Clestrain on 10 February 1725 rather than surrender quietly, but his luck ran out soon after: attempting to attack another remote house, his ship ran aground on the Calf of Eday and he and his crew were captured. Sent to London, Gow was tried and hanged in 1725, and by the Newgate Calendar's account he was slow to die. Friends pulled at his legs to hasten it, the rope broke, and he had to be gathered up from the ground and hanged a second time.

The lurid tale of the local lad turned pirate caught the imagination of Sir Walter Scott, who made Gow the model for Captain Cleveland in his novel The Pirate, fixing John Gow forever in the legend of the Northern Isles.

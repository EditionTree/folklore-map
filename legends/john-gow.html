John Gow

John Gow was raised in Stromness and returned to Orkney under an assumed name after a short pirate career at sea. His attempt to pose as a prosperous trader collapsed into capture near the Calf of Eday, ending in a London hanging that inspired Walter Scott's novel The Pirate.

The Legend

John Gow was the pirate son of Orkney, raised in the harbour town of Stromness, whose brief and bloody career ended almost where it began. Serving as an officer aboard a merchant ship in 1724, he led a mutiny in which the captain and loyal officers were murdered, seized the vessel, and turned pirate in the waters off Spain and Portugal before sailing north and rashly returning to Orkney under a false name.

The Figure Behind the Legend

There he was recognised among the people who had known him as a boy. His attempt to plunder the Hall of Clestrain and raid local houses went awry, and after his ship ran aground on the Calf of Eday he and his crew were captured, sent to London, tried and hanged in 1725 — Gow, the tradition says, twice over, the rope breaking at the first attempt. The lurid tale of the local lad turned pirate caught the imagination of Sir Walter Scott, who made it the basis of his novel The Pirate, fixing John Gow forever in the legend of the Northern Isles.

John Olliver, the Miller of Highdown Hill

A Sussex windmiller built his own tomb 27 years before he died, then reputedly used it to store contraband while signalling smugglers with his sails.

The Legend

John Olliver was the miller of Highdown Hill, near Worthing on the Sussex coast, in the late eighteenth century. Around 1766, some 27 years before his eventual death in 1793, Olliver had an elaborate tomb built for himself on the hilltop beside his windmill, reportedly keeping the coffin under his own bed for years before he needed it — a piece of eccentricity that made him a local celebrity in his own lifetime, with visitors said to have come from across the region to see the tomb of the miller who had prepared for his own death decades early.

The Story

Local tradition holds that Olliver was also the leader of a gang of local smugglers, using the elevated position of his windmill to watch for revenue officers and setting the angle of his mill's sails as a coded signal to smuggling vessels working the coast below, warning them whether it was safe to land contraband. The tomb itself was said to double as a hiding place for smuggled goods, its raised, monument-like structure providing cover that an ordinary shed would not. A later popular embellishment held that Olliver asked to be buried upside down so that he would be the right way up when the world turned over at the end of days — a detail modern folklorists believe was borrowed from the unrelated and genuinely upside-down grave of Major Peter Labilliere on Box Hill, and mistakenly attached to Olliver's story.

Olliver's tomb and its associated legend have been the subject of sustained antiquarian and academic interest, including a dedicated study in the journal Folklore ('The Miller's Tomb: Facts, Gossip and Legend'), which traces how the historical miller and his real burial monument accumulated smuggling lore and increasingly exaggerated detail over the two centuries since his death — making it an unusually well-documented example of how a smuggling reputation can attach itself to a real, identifiable person and monument.

John Walsh, the Cunning Man of Netherbury

Examined by the Bishop of Exeter's court in 1566, Dorset wise-man John Walsh met fairies on the hills at noon and midnight, and kept a familiar that turned into a grey dog or a man with cloven feet.

The Legend

In August 1566 the church courts of the diocese of Exeter examined John Walsh, a cunning man from the Dorset village of Netherbury, on suspicion of witchcraft and sorcery. Questioned by Thomas Williams, commissary to the Bishop of Exeter, in front of an audience of local gentlemen, Walsh described a working life spent diagnosing bewitchment, tracing stolen goods, and consulting a familiar spirit, all recorded and printed that same year as The Examination of John Walsh, the earliest surviving pamphlet account of an English church court's interrogation of a cunning-folk practitioner.

The Story

Walsh told his questioners that he met with fairies on certain hills between twelve and one at noon, or at midnight, and that he could sort them into three kinds by colour: white, green and black, the black fairies being the most dangerous to deal with. His familiar spirit, he said, appeared to him variously as a grey dog, a dove, or a man in every respect ordinary except for cloven feet, and it was this spirit that told him which of his neighbours were bewitched and helped him recover stolen property, including, he claimed, several missing horses. He also owned what the pamphlet calls a book of circles, used in his consultations.

The pamphlet, printed in London by John Awdely, survives in a single known copy and gives no account of what became of Walsh after his examination: no verdict, sentence or acquittal is recorded, only the interrogation itself. That silence has left him a genuinely open case in the history of English witchcraft prosecution, and modern scholars, including Marion Gibson in her sourcebook Early Modern Witches, treat his testimony as a rare direct record of what a working Tudor cunning-man actually believed and practised, rather than what his accusers assumed about him.

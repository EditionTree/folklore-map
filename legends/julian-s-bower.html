Julian's Bower

A single winding path cut into a Lincolnshire hillside, this turf maze traces back to a legendary Trojan game and once drew villagers to run its coils on May Eve — the last surviving maze of its name in England.

The Legend

Julian's Bower is a turf maze at Alkborough in North Lincolnshire, cut into the grass on a hillside high above the meeting of the Rivers Trent and Ouse where they form the Humber — a single winding path coiled in a circular pattern, the last surviving maze of several in England once known by that curious name. Turf mazes of this kind, with their long looping path leading to a centre, were also widely called 'Troy Towns' or 'Walls of Troy', and a body of tradition and custom grew up around them.

The old turf maze keeps a winding ritual path in the North Lincolnshire grass.

The Maze in the Turf

The name 'Julian's Bower' is traditionally linked to Julius — Iulus, the son of Aeneas of Troy — who, legend held, instituted the 'Troy game', a complex equestrian drill or maze-dance, which the Romans were believed to have brought to Britain; the turf mazes were imagined as relics of this Trojan game, hence 'Troy Town'. At Alkborough the maze was a place of village custom. Young people, especially, would run or thread the winding path, particularly on May Eve, in games and races whose half-remembered ritual purpose blurred between sport, courtship and old magic.

The maze design was clearly cherished by the local community across the religious divide of its history. The pattern of Julian's Bower was copied in the porch of the parish church and set into a window and a memorial there, binding the pagan-seeming turf labyrinth to the Christian life of the village. As one of England's rare surviving turf mazes, Julian's Bower remains a relic of a widespread but little-understood folk tradition, the winding path on the green hilltop above the Humber, walked by generations for reasons now half-lost.

Historical Context

Origins & Dating

Historical PeriodThe maze itself is thought to be medieval, possibly cut by monks of Spalding Priory (which held the church and land at Alkborough from before 1146 until at least 1220) as a penitential exercise, which if correct would predate even the Chartres Cathedral pavement maze
Earliest Written RecordAntiquarian Abraham de la Pryme's diary (entries 1671-1704) records the earliest known written reference to the maze, in 1697, speculating (probably wrongly) that it was of Roman origin

Folklore dates are often approximate, especially where a story predates any surviving written source.

Kelpie

A shape-shifting water horse lurking in Scottish lochs. It appears as a magnificent steed to lure riders onto its back before plunging into the depths. Its skin is adhesive: those who mount it cannot let go.

The Legend

The kelpie is Scotland's most notorious water spirit, a shape-shifting horse that haunts the country's lochs and rivers. It appears most often as a fine grey or black pony lingering at the water's edge, its coat dripping, inviting the weary or the curious to climb onto its back. The moment a rider mounts they find themselves stuck fast as the creature plunges into the deep to drown and devour them, only the liver or entrails, it was said, would later wash ashore.

The Horse at the Water's Edge

The kelpie could also take human form, usually a handsome stranger, betrayed by the water weed or sand tangled in its hair. Tales tell of those who escaped by seizing its enchanted bridle, which was said to grant mastery over the beast.

The word itself is old: 'kelpie' appears in place-names as early as 1674, recorded as 'Kelpie hoall' in the Burgh Records of Kirkcudbright, and Robert Burns alludes to kelpies in his 1786 poem 'Address to the Deil', showing the term was already well embedded in Lowland Scots by the late eighteenth century, long before Victorian collectors such as John Gregorson Campbell and Walter Scott set down the fuller Highland tradition. The kelpie's enduring hold on the Scottish imagination is now made monumental in The Kelpies, the colossal steel horse-heads that rise beside the canal at Falkirk.

Kilkenny Witch Trials

In 1324, wealthy Alice Kyteler fled the country rather than face a sorcery charge. Her maidservant Petronilla de Meath had no such escape. Tortured into confession, she was burned at the stake, the first person executed for heresy in Ireland.

The Legend

The Kilkenny proceedings of 1324 are among the earliest and most notorious witch trials in the British Isles, centred on the wealthy Hiberno-Norman noblewoman Alice Kyteler. Four times married and grown rich on each husband's estate, she was accused by her stepchildren, who watched her fortune grow at their expense through her fourth husband, Sir John le Poer, and concluded she was practising witchcraft against him.

Alice Kyteler escaped the trial, but Petronilla de Meath was left to face its terrible force.

The Charge in Kilkenny

Brought before Richard de Ledrede, the zealous Bishop of Ossory, Kyteler faced a formal list of charges: denying Christ and the Church, sacrificing animals at crossroads to a demon called the son of Art (better known as Robin Artisson), stealing the keys to the parish church for nightly meetings, brewing potions from a robber's skull and animal remains to harm and enchant her neighbours, and poisoning Sir John himself. Well connected and protected by powerful kin, Kyteler evaded the bishop's court and fled to Dublin and then abroad, never to be punished. Her maidservant Petronilla de Meath was not so fortunate. Whipped and tortured into confession, she was burned at the stake in Kilkenny on 3 November 1324, the first person recorded, by the contemporary chronicler John Clyn, to have been executed for heresy in Ireland.

Alice's son William Outlaw, himself named in the case, escaped more lightly: sentenced to hear three masses a day for a year and to re-roof St Canice's Cathedral in lead as penance. The lead roof he paid for held for eight years before collapsing in 1332, a small, very physical postscript to Ireland's earliest documented witch trial.

King Bladud

Exiled with leprosy, a prince turned swineherd watched his pigs cure themselves in Bath's hot mud. He founded the city on the spot, then, legend says, strapped on feathered wings and fell to his death trying to fly.

The Legend

According to Geoffrey of Monmouth's twelfth-century Historia Regum Britanniae, Bladud was the ninth king of the Britons, a prince who studied in Athens, contracted leprosy, and was driven from court and disinherited. Disguised and destitute, he found work as a swineherd near Swainswick, a couple of miles from the later site of Bath. He noticed his pigs would wallow in a warm, black mud fed by a hot spring in a nearby alder-moor, and that the ones that did so were free of the skin disease that plagued the rest of the herd. He tried the mud himself, was cured, and went on to found the city of Bath at the spring, later dedicated to the goddess Sulis.

The Story

Geoffrey was the first writer to name Bladud as Bath's founder, claiming to translate from "a very ancient book written in the British language," though he is now thought to have drawn on a much older Roman-era description of Aquae Sulis and grafted the Greek myth of Daedalus and Icarus onto Bladud's death: later in life the king took up magical experiments, fashioned wings, and leaped from a tall building, sometimes placed at a temple of Apollo in London, plunging to his death. The leprous-swineherd episode itself is a later medieval addition, absent from Geoffrey's original text, and some historians trace the figure's root to an earlier Welsh prince, Bleiddudd of Dyfed, praised in a ninth-century poem.

Bath still keeps Bladud close: a statue of him overlooks the King's Bath in the Pump Room precinct, and a trail of painted pig sculptures, King Bladud's Pigs, has run through the city's streets in his honour. In Geoffrey's line of legendary kings descended from Brutus of Troy, Bladud is the father of Leir, whose story of a divided kingdom and a favourite daughter later became the basis for Shakespeare's King Lear.

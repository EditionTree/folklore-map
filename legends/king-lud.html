King Lud

London's legendary pre-Roman king rebuilt the city and gave Ludgate its name. His own statue, beheaded during the Reformation and rebuilt twice, still stands in a Fleet Street churchyard.

The Legend

In Geoffrey of Monmouth's Historia Regum Britanniae, Lud was the son of King Heli and, by Geoffrey's legendary chronology, ruled Britain around 66 BC, late in the line of kings descended from Brutus of Troy. He is credited with rebuilding and fortifying London, renaming it Caer Lud, Lud's fortress, a name later writers connected to both "London" itself and to Ludgate, the westernmost of the City's ancient gates, where Lud was said to have been buried. When he died, his sons Androgeus and Tenvantius were judged too young to rule, so the throne passed to his brother Cassibelanus; both sons later distinguished themselves under their uncle, Androgeus as ruler of London and Tenvantius of Cornwall, in the defence of Britain against Julius Caesar.

The Story

The legend gained an unusually durable physical form. In 1260 Henry III commissioned stone figures of Lud and his two sons for Ludgate itself; the statues were beheaded during Edward VI's Reformation-era iconoclasm, repaired under Mary I, and rebuilt entirely in 1586 under Elizabeth I. When Ludgate was finally demolished in 1760, following damage in the Great Fire of London a century earlier, the statues survived: Sir Francis Gosling donated them to the City, the Marquess of Hertford later acquired them, and they now stand in the courtyard of St Dunstan-in-the-West on Fleet Street, close to their original site.

Even contemporaries were sceptical of the story; nineteenth-century antiquarians preferred a more prosaic origin for Ludgate, from an Old English term for "flood-gate" or "Fleet-gate," after the River Fleet that once ran past the spot. Geoffrey's Lud, like the rest of his Brutus-descended king-list, is treated by modern historians as literary invention rather than history. What makes the legend unusual within that tradition is the survival of a genuine monument: few of Geoffrey's early British kings can be visited in stone form on a London street corner today.

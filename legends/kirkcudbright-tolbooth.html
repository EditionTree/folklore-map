Kirkcudbright Tolbooth

The old tolbooth of Kirkcudbright in Galloway — a 16th-century civic building with a long history as a prison. It held John Paul Jones briefly after a dispute, and its tower and spire have become the emblem of the town that styles itself the Artists' Town of Scotland.

The Legend

The tolbooth of Kirkcudbright, raised between 1625 and 1629 as the Galloway burgh's council chamber, courtroom and gaol, is bound into the region's dark history of witch persecution. Its tower followed in 1644, and its cells, not properly fitted out as a prison until the 1750s, held those accused of witchcraft in south-west Scotland, among them Elspeth McEwen, who was imprisoned there for two years before being burned in 1698, by tradition the last woman put to death for witchcraft in Galloway.

The old stone prison still carries the memory of those held under accusation.

The Tolbooth Window

McEwen, of Balmaclellan, was accused of bewitching her neighbours' hens and stealing their milk and eggs by uncanny means, and a local minister's prosecution sealed her fate. She was tortured in her cell until, by her own account, she begged for death, and in March 1698 she confessed under duress to 'a contract and regular commerce with the Devil'; she was strangled and her body burned on the Whinny Hill.

The tolbooth, which briefly held the American naval officer John Paul Jones in 1770 after a sailor under his command died from a flogging, stands as a tangible relic of the witch-hunt, an ordinary town building whose stones witnessed the imprisonment and condemnation of the poor and friendless women who were the trials' usual victims. Restored as the Tolbooth Art Centre in 1993, it now houses a museum and gallery in the rooms that once held the accused.

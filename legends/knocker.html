Knocker

Mine spirits whose tapping warned Cornish miners of danger, rich seams, or unseen company in the dark. They could be helpful if respected and dangerous if mocked.

The Legend

The Knockers are the mine-spirits of Cornwall, small, wizened, grey-bearded fairy folk, about two feet tall, who dwell deep in the tin and copper workings and make themselves known by the knocking sounds heard in the dark underground. Cornish miners regarded them with wary affection: the knocking was widely believed to be the Knockers tapping at the richest lodes, and many a miner followed the sound to a fresh seam of ore.

The Otherworld at the Heart of the Tale

Robert Hunt, who gathered the fullest account of the belief in Popular Romances of the West of England (1865), described them as capricious rather than simply good or bad: helpful and generous to a miner who respected them, but quick to turn spiteful, bringing rockfalls or ruining a working, if slighted or cheated. Respect meant specific, small courtesies: it was reckoned unlucky to whistle or to swear below ground, to make the sign of the cross, or to fail to leave them a scrap of pasty-crust, the last piece of a miner's Cornish pasty set aside underground as their due.

Cornish miners carried the belief across the Atlantic to the mines of America, where the spirits lived on as the 'Tommyknockers'. The Knockers are a vivid expression of how a deadly trade peopled its darkness with spirits to be appeased.

Origins & Dating

Historical PeriodRecorded in the mid-Victorian Cornish folklore-collecting tradition (1865-1870); a possibly related belief in mine-spirits producing knocking sounds appears even earlier, in a 1656 letter from John Lewis in Ceredigion describing 'little sprites in the mines' — though this is Welsh rather than specifically Cornish evidence
Earliest Written RecordRobert Hunt's 'Popular Romances of the West of England' (1865) records a Knocker tale set at Ransom Mine; William Bottrell's 'Traditions and Hearthside Stories of West Cornwall' (serialised 1867-69, published as a book 1870) provides further early documentation

Folklore dates are often approximate, especially where a story predates any surviving written source.

Sacred Sites County Sligo, Ireland

Knocknarea

Queen Medb of Connacht is said to lie buried upright in the great cairn crowning Knocknarea above Sligo Bay, facing her eternal enemies in Ulster, still armed and waiting; every visitor who adds a stone to the pile strengthens the mound over her — and anyone who takes a stone away courts misfortune.

The legend

Knocknarea (Irish: Cnoc na Ríogh, 'Hill of the Kings') rises 327 metres above Sligo Bay, its summit dominated by the massive unexcavated cairn of Miosgán Médhbha — Queen Medb's Lump — approximately 55 metres in diameter and 10 metres high. The cairn is one of the largest in Ireland and is estimated to contain some 40,000 tonnes of stone. Despite being the most prominent prehistoric monument in the Sligo landscape, it has never been excavated, partly out of respect for local tradition and partly because survey evidence suggests multiple burial phases within the mound.

The story

Queen Medb (Maeve) of Connacht is the great sovereign queen of the Ulster Cycle, the instigator of the Táin Bó Cúailnge, and one of the most powerful female figures in all Irish mythology. Her connection to Knocknarea is ancient: tradition holds that she was buried standing upright within the cairn, spear in hand, fully armed and facing northeast towards Ulster — the province she spent her life fighting. The stance is one of enduring defiance rather than death. The Connacht provincial identity absorbed into her legend made Knocknarea a symbol of regional power for centuries.

Local custom demands that every visitor adds a stone to the cairn as they pass. The tradition preserves the mound against erosion but carries a symbolic weight: the stones are a tribute to Medb, an acknowledgement of her presence, and a form of protection for the visitor. To take a stone from the cairn is held to invite misfortune. W.B. Yeats, who lived in Sligo and drew heavily on its mythology, used Knocknarea as a powerful image in his poetry, most famously in The Hosting of the Sidhe and in references to Maeve throughout his work. The Sligo landscape, with Knocknarea to the west and Benbulben to the north, formed the essential mythological geography of his imagination.

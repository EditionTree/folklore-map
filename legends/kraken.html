Kraken

A vast many-armed creature rising from the deep. Norse sagas describe it as large enough to be mistaken for an island — only revealing itself when it submerged beneath the waves.

The Legend

The Kraken is the legendary monster of the northern seas, a colossal many-armed creature of the deep whose legend, rooted in Norse tradition, washed up on the shores of Orkney, Shetland and the Scottish coasts along with the Scandinavian heritage of those islands. In the old accounts it is a beast of almost unimaginable size — so vast that when it lay at rest on the surface, sailors mistook its back for an island, and might land upon it or anchor beside it, only for the 'island' to stir and sink, dragging ship and crew down into the whirlpool of its descent.

Norse sagas describe it as large enough to be mistaken for an island.

The Island That Moved

The Norse sagas and later Scandinavian writers described the Kraken rising from the sea-bed to feed, its great arms reaching up to seize whole ships and pull them under, and the disturbed water boiling and swirling as it submerged. Curiously, fishermen also held that the monster's presence brought a great abundance of fish, drawn to feed around it, so that a daring catch might be had above the lurking beast — at deadly risk. To see the sea heave and shoal strangely, or an unexpected reef appear and vanish, was to suspect the Kraken below.

Behind the legend almost certainly lies the giant squid, a real and enormous deep-sea creature, rarely seen alive, whose vast tentacled carcasses occasionally washed ashore or rose to the surface and gave terrifying substance to the tales. As the supreme sea-monster of the northern imagination, the Kraken has loomed ever larger in literature, from the old sagas to Tennyson's famous sonnet of the sleeping beast that will rise only once, at the end of the world, to die on the surface.

Historical Context

Origins & Dating

Approximate Origin1752-53
Historical Period18th-century Scandinavia; the name 'Kraken' itself never appears in the medieval Norse sagas, though related giant sea-creatures (the hafgufa and lyngbakr) are described under different names in the 13th-century Örvar-Odds saga
Earliest Written RecordErik Pontoppidan, Bishop of Bergen, gave the earliest substantial written account under the name 'Kraken' in his 'Natural History of Norway' (Danish original 1752-53, English translation 1755)

Folklore dates are often approximate, especially where a story predates any surviving written source.

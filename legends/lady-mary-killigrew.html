Lady Mary Killigrew

In January 1582, the grey-haired wife of Cornwall's Vice Admiral sent her servants to board a Spanish ship at anchor under royal safe conduct, seized its cargo, and was sentenced to death, only for the Queen to pardon her.

The Legend

Lady Mary Killigrew (née Mary Wolverston; c.1525, after 1582) was the daughter of a Suffolk privateer and wife of Sir John Killigrew IV, Vice Admiral of Cornwall and governor of Pendennis Castle, whose ancient seat of Arwenack House stood on the neck of land that would later become Falmouth. The Killigrews held Arwenack for some sixteen generations, with connections to the crown reaching back to the reign of Henry III in the thirteenth century, and their relationship with piracy was unusually frank even by the standards of the Cornish coast: Henry VIII built Pendennis Castle on Killigrew land in the 1540s and made a Killigrew its first hereditary captain, giving the family effective control of shipping through Falmouth harbour, and by 1567 Arwenack House itself had been fortified and was being used to store cargo stolen from raided ships. Mary was in her late fifties when she committed her most notorious act.

The Figure Behind the Legend

In January 1582, a Spanish vessel, the Marie of San Sebastian, was anchored at Penryn under a letter of safe conduct from Queen Elizabeth I. Having heard that it carried a rich cargo, Lady Killigrew directed her servants to row out at night, board the ship, overpower the crew, and strip its hold. Her men were later caught, tried, and condemned to death. Mary herself was arrested, tried, and sentenced to execution, a remarkable fate for a woman of her rank, wife of the royal governor of Pendennis Castle. Through the intercession of powerful connections the sentence was commuted and Elizabeth issued a pardon, but not before Lady Killigrew had spent a period imprisoned.

The incident left a lasting mark on Cornish coastal folklore, and even its central detail is unsettled: some later accounts name the culprit not Mary Wolverston but Elizabeth Trewinnard, a confusion that itself points to how thoroughly the story had passed into oral legend by the time it was written down. Mary appears in nineteenth-century Cornish antiquarian literature as a byword for high-born audacity, and her legend was kept alive in oral traditions associated with the Killigrew seat. Stories of a lantern hung in a tower window to lure ships onto the rocks of the Fal estuary cluster around the family; various women of the Killigrew line were accused of similar crimes across several generations, suggesting that 'Lady Killigrew, the pirate' became a composite figure in popular tradition, her exact identity finally less important to the story than the family's long, genuinely piratical reputation on this stretch of coast.

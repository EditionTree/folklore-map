Laidly Worm of Spindleston Heugh

A princess transformed by her jealous stepmother into a loathsome worm around Bamburgh. Her brother Childe Wynd broke the spell with three kisses, returning monster to maiden and justice to the hall.

The Legend

The Laidly Worm of Spindleston Heugh is a Northumbrian dragon-legend with a fairy-tale heart. The beautiful Princess Margaret of Bamburgh is transformed by her jealous new stepmother, a witch-queen, into a loathsome, venomous worm ('laidly' meaning loathly) that coils about Spindleston Heugh, a craggy outcrop near the castle, and ravages the country, demanding the milk of seven cows each day to keep its venom in check.

The Worm and the Spell

Her brother, the Childe Wynd, returns from over the sea to break the spell. Warned not to slay the worm but to kiss it three times, he does so, and the dragon shrinks away to reveal his sister restored; he then turns his vengeance on the stepmother, changing her into a toad said still to haunt the rocks below Bamburgh.

The ballad was first printed in 1778 in the antiquarian William Hutchinson's 'A View of Northumberland,' credited to the Reverend Robert Lambe of Norham. Lambe claimed to have translated it from a Latin manuscript by 'the old mountain-bard, Duncan Frasier, living on Cheviot, A.D. 1270,' but no such medieval manuscript has ever surfaced, and scholars now generally regard the ancient attribution as Lambe's own invention, dressing up a genuine local oral tale in a fabricated pedigree to give it greater antiquity.

The tale fuses the dragon-slaying tradition with the older motif of the disenchanting kiss, a distinctly tender variant among Britain's many worm legends, and one still tied to a real crag on the walk above Bamburgh Castle today.

Leprechaun

A solitary fairy shoemaker famous for hidden treasure and impossible bargains. Though often portrayed as cheerful, older folklore presents the leprechaun as cunning, elusive, and almost impossible to outwit.

The Legend

The leprechaun is the best-known of all Ireland's fairies, though the cheerful little figure of modern caricature is a thin shadow of the older tradition. In the folklore he is a solitary fairy, one of the lone, secretive sprites rather than the trooping fairy host, and his trade is shoemaking. He is most often found by the sound of his hammer, tapping away at a single shoe beneath a hedge or in a quiet hollow, dressed in his working clothes and forever mending footwear for the dancing fairy folk who wear theirs out.

The Shoemaker with Hidden Treasure

The earliest ancestor of the tale is a story called Echtra Fergusa maic Léti, composed by around the 8th century, in which King Fergus mac Léti falls asleep on a beach and wakes to find three lúchorpáin, 'small bodies', dragging him toward the sea. He seizes them and will not let go until they grant him three wishes, including the power to travel underwater, already carrying the same core shape of every leprechaun story since: catch the small creature, and it must bargain for its freedom. The word itself first survives in writing in the 12th-century manuscript Lebor na hUidre, the Book of the Dun Cow, where a scribe can be seen correcting his own spelling of it mid-page, from 'luprucan' to 'lucrupan', proof that even medieval copyists weren't quite sure how the name should be written down.

What every later story turns on is his treasure. The leprechaun is said to keep a hidden crock of gold, and if a mortal can catch him and keep him fast in their gaze, he must by the rules of the encounter reveal where it lies or buy his freedom with it. But the leprechaun is cunning beyond mortal wit, and the tales almost always end with him outwitting his captor, tricking them into glancing away for an instant, or marking the spot of the gold with a garter only to have the trick undone when the human returns to find every tuft in the field tied with an identical marker.

Older accounts make him a sharper, more ambiguous creature than the genial mascot of greeting cards: elusive, sardonic, and not to be trusted. He is sometimes confused or linked with related solitary fairies such as the clurichaun, a tippling cellar-haunting cousin, and the fear dearg. Above all the leprechaun embodies a recurring lesson of Irish fairy lore: that fairy gold and fairy bargains slip through human fingers, and that greed is rarely rewarded.

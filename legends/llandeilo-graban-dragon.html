Llandeilo Graban Dragon

A dragon nesting in the church belfry was destroyed by a blacksmith's iron decoy rigged with hidden spikes, said to be Wales's last dragon-slaying.

The Legend

St Teilo's Church in the small Powys village of Llandeilo Graban, near Painscastle and Builth Wells, is the setting for one of the most distinctive dragon legends of the Welsh borders. The village's name preserves 'graban', Welsh for corn marigold, a wildflower once common in the fields around the church; tradition holds all the same that a dragon took up residence in the church's belfry or tower, terrorising the surrounding parish whenever it left its lair to hunt.

A dragon nesting in the church belfry was destroyed by a blacksmith's iron decoy

The Iron Decoy

Unable to fight the creature directly, a local blacksmith devised a cunning trap: he forged a decoy dragon from iron and placed it in the nest while the real dragon was away. When the dragon returned and found the impostor, it attacked the iron figure in a rage, triggering a hidden mechanism that drove a set of sharp spikes into the beast and killed it on the spot.

The earliest identified print version traces to D. Edmondes Owen's 'Pre-Reformation Survivals in Radnorshire', read to the Honourable Society of Cymmrodorion in its 1910-11 session and published in 1912; even there the tale is framed as happening 'ages ago', with no date attached to the events themselves. The story is often cited in surveys of British dragon lore as one of the last dragons traditionally said to have been killed in Wales, fitting a pattern of 'dragon in the church tower' tales found at a handful of border churches. Llandeilo Graban's version is unusual for the ingenuity of the blacksmith's solution, and the 14th-century church itself still stands as the legend's setting.

Historical Context

Origins & Dating

Historical PeriodNo earliest written record of the dragon-in-the-belfry tale itself was located. St Teilo's Church, Llandeilo Graban is first documented in 1291 (Taxatio Ecclesiastica, as 'Ecclesia de Landeylar'); architecturally the nave is thought 14th-century with the chancel and tower added in the 15th century, per the Clwyd-Powys Archaeological Trust's Radnorshire Churches Survey, but this dates the building, not the legend attached to it
Earliest Written RecordD. Edmondes Owen, 'Pre-Reformation Survivals in Radnorshire,' Transactions of the Honourable Society of Cymmrodorion, session 1910-1911 (London: Issued by the Society, 1912), pp. 112-13

Folklore dates are often approximate, especially where a story predates any surviving written source.

Llangorse Lake

Gerald of Wales recorded in 1188 that a whole town once sank beneath Llangorse Lake in an earthquake — and locals still say its church bells can be heard tolling under the water on still days.

The legend

Llangorse Lake — Llyn Syfaddan in Welsh — is the largest natural lake in South Wales, lying beneath the Black Mountains in the old kingdom of Brycheiniog. When the Welsh churchman and chronicler Gerald of Wales passed by in 1188 while touring with Archbishop Baldwin to preach the Third Crusade, he recorded the lake as a place of wonders: in winter its ice was said to groan and roar like a pack of wolves, the water could turn green or red as if stained with blood, and on calm days the lake appeared to be 'covered with buildings'.

The story

Out of these reported marvels grew a more concrete local legend: that a town once stood where the lake now lies, and that it was destroyed in a single night when the ground split open and water poured out, drowning the settlement and everyone in it. Walkers and fishermen have long claimed that on still days the muffled sound of church bells can be heard ringing up from beneath the surface — the buried town's bells, tolling on even after the people who rang them were lost.

The legend gained an unexpected layer of truth in the 1980s and 90s, when archaeologists excavating a low island near the lake's eastern shore confirmed it to be a man-made crannog — a fortified timber platform built around AD 889-893 for the kings of Brycheiniog, generally identified with the royal stronghold the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle records as destroyed by Aethelflaed's Mercian forces in 916. The crannog is not the drowned town of legend, but its discovery shows that Gerald of Wales was, in his own way, reporting a real submerged settlement — just one far smaller than the lost city of folk memory.

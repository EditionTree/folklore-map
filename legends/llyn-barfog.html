Llyn Barfog

Llyn Barfog, the Bearded Lake, is a remote upland tarn in southern Snowdonia said to be the lair of the Afanc and the home of the Gwragedd Annwn, the fairy maidens of the lake. King Arthur is said to have dragged the monster from its depths here, leaving a hoofprint in the rock.

The Legend

Llyn Barfog, 'the Bearded Lake', sits high in the hills above Aberdovey in southern Snowdonia, its surface fringed with water plants that give it the name. It is one of the most storied lakes in Welsh mythology: the home of the Gwragedd Annwn, the fairy women of the lake, who tended milk-white cattle with reddish ears on the hillsides, and the lair of the Afanc, a monstrous water beast half crocodile and half demon.

Arthur was said to have dragged the Afanc from the lake, leaving a hoofprint in the rock.

The Hoofprint by the Water

One story tells of a poor farmer who found one of the fairy women's cattle grazing loose by the water, a milk-white cow with red ears, of the kind said to belong to the Gwragedd Annwn. He took her home, and her rich milk and her calves built him a fine herd over several generations. But when he tried to have the old cow slaughtered for meat, one of the fairy women rose from the lake in green and called every beast descended from her, down to the fourth generation, back into the water. Only a single black cow was left behind, and Welsh tradition holds her the ancestor of the native Welsh Black cattle breed. The farmer, ruined by the loss, is said to have drowned himself in the lake he had taken so much from.

The most celebrated legend places Arthur himself here. The monster had long terrorised the valley, and all attempts to destroy it had failed. Arthur came with his horse Llamrei, looped a chain around the sleeping Afanc, and dragged it bodily from the water. The beast thrashed and fought, but Llamrei strained until the creature was hauled onto dry land; accounts differ on where he sent it next, some saying Glaslyn far to the north, others the closer Llyn Cau, but all agree it never troubled Llyn Barfog again. The earliest print version of the tale traces to Elias Owen's Welsh Folk-Lore, written for the 1887 National Eisteddfod and published in 1896.

A rock on the hillside above the lake bears a hollow said to be the hoofprint Llamrei left in the effort, and is known to this day as Carn March Arthur, the Stone of Arthur's Horse.

Long Meg and Her Daughters

A stone circle said to be a coven of witches turned to stone for dancing on the Sabbath. Long Meg herself stands apart, taller and marked with strange carvings.

A visual interpretation of Long Meg and Her Daughters
The Legend

Long Meg and Her Daughters is one of the great prehistoric stone circles of Britain, a vast ring of some sixty stones near Penrith in Cumbria, with one tall outlying red sandstone monolith — Long Meg herself — standing apart from the circle of her 'daughters'. Long Meg is marked with ancient carvings, including a spiral and concentric rings, and the whole monument has gathered a rich body of folklore around its uncanny presence.

The circle was said to be a coven turned to stone for dancing on the Sabbath.

The Coven in Stone

The legend that names it tells that the stones are a coven of witches, turned to stone for profaning the Sabbath: Long Meg was a witch (in some tellings named Meg of Meldon) and her daughters her fellow witches, caught dancing wildly on the moor and petrified where they stood by a holy man — the great red pillar of Meg presiding over the frozen ring of her companions. Like many such circles, it carries the belief that the stones cannot be reliably counted — that anyone who tallies them twice and gets the same number will work a charm or break the spell — and that should the count ever come out true, the enchantment will be undone.

Long Meg herself is held to be charged with power: it is said that she will bleed if she is broken or chipped, and that ill luck or storm follows any attempt to disturb or move the stones. A tale tells of a landowner who tried to blast the stones apart and was driven off by a sudden violent tempest the stones called up. Between the petrified witches, the uncountable daughters and the bleeding monolith, Long Meg and Her Daughters is among the most legend-rich stone circles in the country.

Lough Derg (St Patrick's Purgatory)

St Patrick is said to have been shown a cave on this island leading straight into Purgatory — and for 800 years pilgrims have crossed to it to go barefoot, fast, and stay awake for three days and nights.

A visual interpretation of Lough Derg and St Patrick's Purgatory
The Legend

On Station Island in Lough Derg, County Donegal, tradition holds that Saint Patrick once fasted and prayed until Christ showed him a pit on the island leading directly down into Purgatory — proof of the torments awaiting sinners, given so that Patrick could convince doubters of the reality of the afterlife. The cave itself, later sealed up, became known as 'St Patrick's Purgatory' and made the island one of the most important pilgrimage sites in medieval Christendom.

A cave on the island was said to lead into Purgatory, drawing pilgrims for centuries.

The Island of Penance

Its fame spread across Europe through the Tractatus de Purgatorio Sancti Patricii, written around 1184 by an English monk known only as 'H.', recounting the experience of an Irish knight named Owein who entered the cave and was led through the torments of Purgatory before reaching a vision of Paradise. Copied and translated into most major European languages, Owein's vision became one of the most popular texts of the Middle Ages, and many scholars believe it influenced Dante's descriptions of Purgatory and Hell in the Divine Comedy two centuries later.

The pilgrimage tradition has never stopped. Modern pilgrims still travel to Station Island between June and August to take part in the 'three-day station': going barefoot over the island's stone 'beds', fasting on dry bread, black tea and coffee, and keeping an all-night vigil without sleep. It remains one of the most physically demanding pilgrimages in the Christian world, and one of the few places where a tradition first recorded in the twelfth century is still practised, largely unchanged, today.

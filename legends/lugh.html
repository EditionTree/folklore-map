Lugh

LOO

The shining god of skill and craftsmanship in Celtic tradition — master of every art. His festival Lughnasadh marks the first harvest. He slew the monstrous Balor of the Evil Eye with a slingshot to end a tyrannical age.

A visual interpretation of Lugh in Ireland & Northern Britain
The Legend

Lugh is the shining, many-skilled god of Irish myth, a leading figure of the Tuatha Dé Danann whose epithet Lámhfhada, 'of the long arm', marks him as a master of reach and of weapons. The most famous tale of his arrival has him come to the court of Nuada at Tara and ask to be admitted for his skills; turned away because each craft — wright, smith, champion, harper, poet, physician, sorcerer — is already filled, he asks whether they have any one man who is master of them all, and so wins entry as the Samildánach, the man skilled in every art together.

The Divine Presence

His defining deed comes at the Second Battle of Mag Tuired, where the Tuatha Dé Danann face the oppressive Fomorians. Their champion is Balor of the Evil Eye, Lugh's own grandfather, whose monstrous eye withers armies when its lid is heaved open. It was prophesied that Balor would fall to his grandson, and so he did: as the great eye was being raised, Lugh struck it through with a sling-stone, driving it back through Balor's skull to blast the Fomorian host behind him, and breaking their power for good.

Lugh's festival, Lughnasadh, falls at the start of August and opens the harvest — said to have been founded by Lugh himself as funeral games for his foster-mother Tailtiu, who died clearing the plains of Ireland for agriculture. Marked by fairs, contests and the first cutting of the corn, it survives in the modern Irish name for August, Lúnasa. A god of light, kingship and every human skill, Lugh also passes into Welsh tradition as Lleu Llaw Gyffes.

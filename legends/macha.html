Deities Navan Fort, County Armagh

Macha

MOKH-uh

Forced to race the King of Ulster's horses while nine months pregnant, the goddess Macha won — and in giving birth on the finish line cursed the men of Ulster to suffer the pains of labour in their greatest hour of need; the curse struck them all down at the very outset of the Táin Bó Cúailnge, leaving only Cú Chulainn to defend the province alone.

A visual interpretation of Macha's coerced race against the horses of Ulster
The Legend

Macha is one of three aspects of the Morrígan in Irish mythology, and in her own right a sovereignty goddess of considerable complexity, associated above all with Emain Macha (Navan Fort) in County Armagh. Three distinct figures called Macha appear in the mythological sources, but the most dramatically influential is the pregnant woman of the Ulster Cycle who becomes the origin of one of the great narrative engines of the Táin.

She begged for delay until the child was born; the king refused.

The Race That Cursed Ulster

The story is told in Noínden Ulad, the Debility of the Ulstermen. Macha's husband Crunniuc mac Agnomain boasted at a royal assembly that his wife could outrun the king's fastest horses. King Conchobar mac Nessa insisted on a race to prove it, despite Macha's advanced pregnancy. She begged for delay until the child was born; the king refused. Macha ran and won — but at the finish line she went into labour, and as she gave birth to twins she screamed out her curse on the men of Ulster: that whenever Ulster faced its greatest hour of need, the men of the province would be struck by the pains of childbirth and be prostrate for five days and four nights, as she had been forced to suffer. The place was named Emain Macha — the twins of Macha — in memory of the birth.

The curse — ces noinden Ulad, the debility of the Ulstermen — is the foundational dramatic premise of the Táin Bó Cúailnge. When Queen Medb of Connacht invades Ulster to seize Donn Cúailnge, every warrior in Ulster is struck down by Macha's curse at the moment of crisis, leaving only the seventeen-year-old Cú Chulainn — exempt because he is not a native Ulsterman — to defend the province alone through a series of single combats. Navan Fort (Scheduled Ancient Monument near Armagh city) was the royal site of Ulster for over a thousand years and the geographical anchor of Macha's legend; its name commemorates the birth that preceded the curse.

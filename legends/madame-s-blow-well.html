Madame's Blow-well

Circular ponds on the Lincolnshire coast were said to be bottomless, and one is still called Madame's Blow-well for the great lady whose coach and four vanished into it and were never seen again.

The Legend

Tetney, on the Lincolnshire coast near the Humber, sits above a cluster of natural artesian springs known as the Blow Wells, where fresh water forces itself up through the clay in circular pools. Local tradition long held these ponds to be bottomless: one 19th-century account records that a local landowner had some of them sounded and found no bottom, and that they were popularly said to run clean through to the Antipodes.

The Story

One well in particular carries its own name and its own story. Rev. J. Wild's 1901 account of Tetney parish records that a great lady, travelling with her coach and four horses, was swallowed bodily into one of the ponds and never seen again, and that the pool has been called Madame's Blow-well ever since. The tale was collected alongside other Lincolnshire ghost and water lore by Eliza Gutch and Mabel Peacock for the Folk-Lore Society's County Folk-Lore series in 1908, one of the more rigorous county-by-county folklore surveys of its era. Related accounts describe the wider blow-hole ponds in this corner of the county as haunted by a phantom coach and four and a grey lady, suggesting Madame's Blow-well anchors a broader local tradition of a vanished noblewoman still abroad on the marsh.

The wells themselves are real and still flowing: the site is now a Lincolnshire Wildlife Trust nature reserve southeast of Tetney church, its four spring-fed pools and surrounding wetland protected for the plant and bird life the constant flow of water supports, a quietly persistent landscape for a story about something that never stops disappearing into it.

Maeshowe Runes

A Neolithic chambered cairn broken into by Norse visitors who carved runes into the stone. Ancient tomb became Viking noticeboard, proving folklore sometimes arrives with a knife and poor manners.

The Legend

Maeshowe is one of the finest Neolithic chambered cairns in Europe, a great grass-covered mound on the Mainland of Orkney enclosing a magnificent stone-built central chamber, raised some five thousand years ago and aligned so that the light of the setting midwinter sun shines straight down the entrance passage to illuminate the back wall — a deliberate marriage of architecture and the turning year. But Maeshowe's most famous fame comes from a much later intrusion: the largest collection of runic inscriptions found anywhere outside Scandinavia.

The Story

In the 12th century, Norsemen — Orkney was then a Norse earldom — broke into the ancient tomb, probably crusaders or shelterers from a storm, and carved their runes into its stones. The graffiti they left is wonderfully, humanly ordinary: boasts that 'these runes were carved by the most skilled rune-carver in the western ocean'; claims that great treasure had been carried off from the howe; a note that 'Thorni bedded Helgi' and other ribald remarks; and the names of men recording simply that they had been there — a Viking-age noticeboard scratched into a tomb already three thousand years old when they entered it.

Norse tradition also peopled such mounds with their original inhabitants made monstrous — the howe-dwellers or 'haugbui', and the trows of Orkney lore were associated with just such ancient mounds. Maeshowe stands at a remarkable crossroads of belief and history: a Neolithic temple of the dead and the midwinter sun, reopened and inscribed by Norsemen whose runes turned the sacred cairn into a record of human curiosity, greed and bravado across the centuries.

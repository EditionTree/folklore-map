Maoveen, the Banshee of Shane’s Castle

Maoveen, 'little Mab', is the O'Neill family banshee, bound to wail at Shane's Castle on Lough Neagh before every clan death. Legend holds she burned the castle to the ground in 1816 after an earl dared to use the room kept forever reserved for her.

The Legend

Shane's Castle, an O'Neill stronghold on the northeastern shore of Lough Neagh in County Antrim, kept an unusual domestic arrangement: a room permanently vacant for the family's own banshee, Maoveen ('little Mab', sometimes Maeveen). The obligation traces to a legend from the days of the O'Neill chieftains. One of the lords, riding out to join a raid alongside the McQuillans, passed a thorn tree sacred to the fairy folk and found a cow tangled in its branches. Taking pity on the animal, he cut a branch to free her and angered the fairies, who seized his daughter Kathleen in revenge and dragged her down into Lough Neagh.

The Otherworld at the Heart of the Tale

The fairies relented enough to let Kathleen send word to her father that she was safe, but bound the family to two conditions: that she appear and wail whenever misfortune threatened the O'Neills, and that a room in the castle stand ready for her at all times. For generations the household kept the bargain, and Maoveen was regarded less as a threat than as a watchful kinswoman, a spirit that gave warning rather than caused harm. This places her within the much wider Irish tradition of the bean sí, the fairy woman attached to a particular family whose keening announces a coming death, a pattern recorded across dozens of Irish households besides the O'Neills.

Eighteenth-century accounts of the castle also mention a second, unrelated spirit: a silent White Lady who glides along the misty Lough Neagh shore just before heavy fog rolls in. The real test of Maoveen's bargain came when an Earl O'Neill held a party so large that every room in the castle was pressed into use, including hers. A fire broke out on the night of 10 October 1816 and destroyed most of the building; persistent tradition holds that Maoveen herself was seen hovering above the flames.

LibraryIreland's nineteenth-century account of Shane's Castle, drawing on earlier Belfast topographical writing, remains the fullest early record of Maoveen's name and origin story. The castle was originally called Eden-duff-carrick before being renamed for Shane O'Neill, and its fire-scarred ruins still stand on the Lough Neagh shore, a lasting trace of the night in 1816 when Maoveen's bargain was broken.

Margaret Finch

Margaret Finch, the 'Queen of the Norwood Gypsies', was a celebrated fortune-teller so accustomed to sitting in a crouched posture that she had to be buried in a specially made square box.

The Legend

Margaret Finch was the celebrated 'Queen of the Gypsies' of Norwood, a fortune-teller whose fame in the early 18th century made the Great North Wood of south London a place of pilgrimage for those seeking to know their future. Born around 1640, she spent much of her long life travelling the kingdom with the Romani people before settling at last at Norwood, then a wild, wooded district outside London, where her reputation for telling fortunes with great skill drew crowds of visitors, fashionable and humble alike, who came out from the city especially on Sundays to consult her.

Her fortune-telling and fierce reputation made her one of South London's best remembered folk figures.

The Queen of Norwood

Her appearance was as memorable as her reputation, and it survives in a print held today by the Royal Collection Trust, titled simply Margaret Finch, Queen of the Gypsies at Norwood. In her old age she fell into the habit of sitting always on the ground with her chin resting on her knees, a pipe in her mouth and her faithful dog beside her, until over the years her sinews contracted so completely that she could no longer straighten her body or rise from that crouched posture. She had become a living curiosity, and her fame only grew.

Margaret Finch died in October 1740, reputed to be about 109 years old. So contracted was her body that she could not be laid out flat, and she had to be buried in a specially made deep, square box rather than an ordinary coffin. The local publicans, who had profited handsomely from the visitors her fame attracted, paid for a grand funeral, and she was interred at St George's, Beckenham. The 'Norwood Gypsy' passed into legend, giving her name to Gypsy Hill and surviving in chapbooks, fortune-telling almanacs and London folklore for generations after.

Mari Lwyd

MAH-ree LOO-id

A winter wassailing custom in which a decorated horse skull is carried from door to door. The party trades verses with householders before being admitted for food, drink, and seasonal chaos.

The Legend

The Mari Lwyd is one of the strangest and most striking of Welsh folk customs, a midwinter wassailing tradition of south Wales in which a horse's skull, decked with ribbons, rosettes and bells and draped in a white sheet, is carried from house to house on a pole by a band of revellers during the Christmas and New Year season. The name is usually taken to mean 'the Grey Mare' or, in another reading, 'Holy Mary', and the snapping-jawed skull-horse is a weird and wonderful sight, its eyes set with bottle-glass and its jaw worked to clack at those it meets.

The party trades verses with householders before being admitted for food, drink, and seasonal chaos.

The Horse at the Door

The heart of the custom is the pwnco — a battle of wits in rhyme. Arriving at a door, the Mari Lwyd party sings verses requesting entry, and the householders within answer with verses of their own, refusing them and offering reasons or insults; the two sides trade improvised rhyming challenges back and forth, each trying to outdo the other, until the householders run out of replies and must admit the Mari and her followers, who are then rewarded with food, drink and merriment, the snapping horse bringing luck for the coming year.

The Mari Lwyd belongs to the wider British family of midwinter 'hooded animal' and luck-visiting customs, but is the most celebrated of them, and after a decline it has been vigorously revived in many Welsh communities, its grinning beribboned skull once again processing from door to door each midwinter.

Marion Pardone, the Witch of Scalloway

Accused of drowning four fishermen off Hillswick while disguised as a porpoise, Marion Pardone was strangled and burned on the hill above Scalloway in 1644.

The Legend

Marion Pardone was tried for witchcraft in Shetland in 1644, one of dozens of islanders swept up in the Scottish witch trials that followed the 1563 Witchcraft Act. Her surviving trial papers, held at Shetland Archives, run to some ten pages of accusations: cursing named individuals, spoiling brewing, contaminating cows' milk, consorting with the devil alongside another accused witch, and — most strikingly — drowning four fishermen in fair weather off the shore at Hillswick while disguised in the shape of a porpoise. She was also said never to have learned the Lord's Prayer in her life, a common mark of suspicion in Scottish witch trials.

The Story

As supposed proof of her guilt, Marion and her husband Swane were brought, days after the drowning, to touch the recovered bodies of the dead fishermen; one corpse was reported to bleed afresh at the collarbone, hands and fingers in her presence, taken at the time as a supernatural sign of her responsibility for the deaths. She was convicted, then strangled and burned on the hill above Scalloway, Shetland's former capital — the same fate met by several other accused Shetlanders of the period, including Andrew 'Luggie' Stephenson, executed at around the same time.

Marion's case is one of many documented in Shetland Museum & Archives' record of the islands' witch trials, and her story has since been revisited in a modern radio play examining how testimony and rumour could be twisted into a capital charge. In 2024 a memorial to the victims of Shetland's witch trials was unveiled on Scalloway's Gallow Hill, the same site where Marion is recorded to have died.

Sacred Sites Meehambee, County Roscommon

Meehambee Dolmen

A Neolithic portal tomb known locally as the Giant’s Bed, its twenty-four-tonne capstone now tilted at a dramatic angle after the rear support collapsed sometime in the medieval period.

A visual interpretation of Meehambee Dolmen, the Giant's Bed
The Legend

The Meehambee Dolmen rises from the fields of Mihanboy near Athlone, its massive sandstone capstone — estimated at twenty-four tonnes — resting at a forty-five degree angle where it slid when the rear support stone collapsed sometime after the medieval period. In its original form around 3500 BC, the tomb would have presented a very different aspect: a great flat stone held level on tall portal stones over two metres high, a monument visible for miles across the Roscommon landscape and intended to impress.

The great capstone rests at a dramatic tilt, giving the portal tomb its giant-sized name.

The Giant's Bed

The dolmen carries the weight of local naming traditions that hint at older beliefs. It was known as the Giant’s Bed, a designation common to Irish portal tombs where the sheer scale of the stones invited stories of superhuman builders. In the 1930s, the Dúchas Schools’ Folklore Collection — an extraordinary project in which over fifty thousand schoolchildren recorded oral traditions from their communities — documented the tomb under the name Leabaidh Éirn, ‘Éirn’s Bed,’ preserving a personal name otherwise lost to history. The same collection noted a nearby earthwork known simply as ‘the Fort,’ which carried fairy associations in local belief.

Two stone axes discovered near the tomb in the 1960s by local schoolchildren confirmed sustained Neolithic activity in the surrounding landscape. The dolmen is one of over 1,200 portal tombs identified across Ireland, each believed to mark either a high-status individual burial or a communal tomb for a local kinship group. At Meehambee, the collapse that tilted the capstone has paradoxically given the monument its most distinctive and dramatic quality — the stone appears frozen in the act of falling, caught between its ancient purpose and the slow geological patience of gravity.

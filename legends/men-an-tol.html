Mên-an-Tol

MEHN an TOLL

The Mên-an-Tol is a small formation of standing stones in Cornwall, United Kingdom. It is about three miles northwest of Madron. It is also known locally as the "Crick Stone".

A visual interpretation of Mên-an-Tol, the Crick Stone
The Legend

The Mên-an-Tol — Cornish for 'the holed stone' — is a small but famous prehistoric monument near Madron in the far west of Cornwall, consisting of a round granite stone pierced by a large central hole, set upright between two standing stones. Its true age and original purpose are uncertain, but for centuries it has been the focus of powerful folk-healing belief, and is known locally as the 'Crick Stone'.

The holed stone was remembered for healing rites and the strange power of passing through.

The Stone with the Hole

The central hole gave rise to a widespread curative ritual: to be healed, a person passed through the opening. Children, especially, were passed naked through the hole a set number of times — usually three, or nine — and often against the sun, as a cure for rickets, scrofula, tuberculosis of the spine and other childhood ailments; adults suffering from 'the crick' (back and joint pain) would crawl or climb through to ease their affliction. The passing-through enacted a symbolic rebirth, leaving the sickness behind on the far side of the stone.

The Mên-an-Tol was credited with other powers too — that crawling through it could restore fertility, and that pins or other objects laid on the stone would answer questions by their movements, watched over (in local belief) by a guardian piskie or fairy. As one of the best-known holed stones in Britain and a site of living folk-magic well into modern times, the Mên-an-Tol is a remarkable survival of the ancient belief in the healing virtue of standing stones and the magic of passing through a gap in the rock.

Mên Scryfa

MEHN SKRIV-a

Mên Scryfa is an inscribed standing stone in Cornwall, England, United Kingdom. The inscription, dating to the early medieval period, commemorates "Rialobranus son of Cunovalus."

A visual interpretation of Mên Scryfa
The Legend

Mên Scryfa — Cornish for 'the stone of writing' or 'inscribed stone' — is a tall, slender standing stone in the moorland of West Penwith in Cornwall, not far from the Mên-an-Tol. Unlike the prehistoric monuments around it, the stone bears an early medieval inscription, carved in the post-Roman centuries, reading 'RIALOBRANI CUNOVALI FILI' — 'Rialobran, son of Cunoval' — commemorating a man whose name means something like 'royal raven', son of 'famous chief'.

The early medieval inscription names Rialobranus, leaving a human trace on the moorland stone.

The Written Stone

Local legend grew up to explain the stone and its vanished hero. Rialobran was remembered as a chieftain or prince who fell in a battle fought on the moor, defending his land — and the standing stone was said to have been raised to mark the very spot where he died, set up to his own height, so that the length of the pillar gave the stature of the warrior buried or commemorated beneath it. The lonely stone thus became a memorial to a Dark Age leader slain in a forgotten fight.

A tale attaches to the stone's disturbance: that a greedy man, believing treasure or gold lay buried with the warrior at its foot, dug beneath Mên Scryfa and toppled it — and found nothing but ill luck for his pains, the stone later being re-erected. As a rare inscribed memorial standing among the prehistoric stones of Penwith, Mên Scryfa is a haunting survival, a named man of the sixth century kept in memory by a pillar on the Cornish moor and the legend of the battle in which he fell.

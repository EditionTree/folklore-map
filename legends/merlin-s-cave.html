Merlin's Cave

A sea cave beneath Tintagel Castle that floods twice daily with the tide — and whose Arthurian name turns out to be a Victorian invention, dreamed up for 19th-century guidebooks.

A visual interpretation of Merlin's Cave beneath the Tintagel headland
The Legend

Merlin's Cave is a natural tunnel roughly 100 metres long, cut through the headland beneath Tintagel Castle by centuries of wave action along a seam between slate and volcanic rock. At low tide it can be walked from end to end across a sandy floor, linking Tintagel Haven to West Cove; at high tide it fills completely with seawater and becomes impassable, a tidal rhythm that has long added to its otherworldly reputation.

Despite the name, no medieval source places Merlin inside — or anywhere near — this cave.

The Cave Below Tintagel

Despite the name, no medieval source places Merlin inside — or anywhere near — this cave. Geoffrey of Monmouth's Historia Regum Britanniae, which first linked Tintagel to Arthur's conception, says nothing of a sea cave, and neither do the later French romances of Robert de Boron or Chrétien de Troyes. The connection is a 19th-century literary graft: Alfred, Lord Tennyson's Idylls of the King (1859–1885) describes the infant Arthur being carried from the waves by Merlin, and Victorian guidebook writers, eager to give Tintagel's dramatic sea cave a story, attached Tennyson's image to this spot.

The name stuck. By the early 20th century 'Merlin's Cave' was fixed on maps, signposts, and in the popular imagination, despite having no basis earlier than the previous generation's poetry. It is now one of the most visited features of Tintagel, a striking case of a brand-new legend taking root so quickly in an already-legendary landscape that few visitors realise how recent it really is.

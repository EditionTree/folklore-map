Moel Tŷ Uchaf

MOIL tee IKH-av

This Berwyn hilltop circle above Llandrillo stands in country said to hide Gwyn ap Nudd's palace and his howling Cŵn Annwn, close to the site of 1974's still-disputed 'Welsh Roswell' fireball.

The Legend

Moel Tŷ Uchaf is a Bronze Age ring cairn dressed as a stone circle, forty-three kerbstones set in a ring about ten metres across on a hilltop above Llandrillo in the Berwyn range of Denbighshire, raised some 4,000 years ago by people who chose to bury their dead 1,400 feet in the sky, overlooking the valley below.

The small circle keeps its place on the high Welsh slope beneath the Berwyn hills.

The Circle on the Hill

The Berwyns carry a heavier weight of folklore than most Welsh hill country. Local tradition holds that the range hides the palace of Gwyn ap Nudd, king of the Otherworld and huntsman of the Cŵn Annwn, the white, red-eared hounds whose howling was said to mark a soul being carried off to the dead. Further south, at Llanrhaeadr-ym-Mochnant, villagers once told of being troubled by flying dragons, part of the same tradition of a mountain range too wild and too old to be entirely of this world.

On 23 January 1974, that reputation gained a modern chapter. Villagers around Llandrillo reported streaking lights, a tremor felt across the district and a loud explosion on the mountain. The official explanation blamed a meteor coinciding with a minor earthquake, but witness accounts and police records describing a hovering object before the blast kept the story alive as the "Welsh Roswell." The circle's forty-three stones still stand on the hillside above Llandrillo today, predating both the hounds and the fireball by thousands of years.

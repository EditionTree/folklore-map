Pirates & Smugglers Wallasey, Wirral, Merseyside

Mother Redcap's

A low house stood on the Wallasey shore behind walls nearly three feet thick, with a five inch oak door and a trapdoor inside the entrance that dropped an unwelcome caller some nine feet into a cellar. It was built in 1595 and kept, by tradition, by a red hooded woman who sheltered smugglers, held privateers' money for them and hid sailors from the press gang. Tunnels were said to run inland towards Bidston and out to the caves at the Red Noses.

The Legend

The house went by several names over the years, among them the Halfway House, the White House and Seabank Nook, but the one that stuck came from the red hood or cap its keeper was said to wear. She is remembered as a Cheshire woman of pleasant manner and formidable competence, trusted with the earnings of men who had nowhere safe to put them. One strand of the tradition gives her the name Poll Jones, though it traces only to a 1927 newspaper article with no earlier corroboration, and no person of that name appears in the Cheshire records of the period.

The Story

The hiding places were not invented. When the building was worked on in 1888 the owner found a large cellar under the yard, reached through flagstones and screened by heaps of coal and manure, along with smaller cavities in the walls, one of them just big enough to take a small man. The defences were built into the fabric by someone who expected to need them.

The press gang side of it is grounded too. Men rowed ashore near the Red Noses and lay up in the sandhills until their ships had sailed, and the parish register records the ones who drowned trying, among them William Evans in 1762.

The tunnels are the part that belongs to legend. They are always said to reach somewhere satisfying and have never been traced. The building closed as a nightclub in 1960, fell into ruin, and was demolished in October 1974, when a bulldozer broke through the ground and exposed a well with a doorway partway down its shaft, exactly the kind of hiding place the smuggling stories had always promised. The foreman had it filled in on the spot and threatened to sack anyone who told the museum. A nursing home carrying the Mother Redcap's name stands on the site today.

Origins & Dating

Historical PeriodThe building dates from 1595 and the smuggling and press gang traditions belong to the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries. The hiding places are documented from 1888. The tunnels are not
Earliest Written RecordThe smugglers' caves and tunnels of the Red and White Noses are described from firsthand visits by the Liverpool antiquarian James Stonehouse in the mid nineteenth century, well before the 1927 newspaper article that supplies the innkeeper's name

Folklore dates are often approximate, especially where a story predates any surviving written source.

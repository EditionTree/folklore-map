Mount Killaraus and the Giant's Dance

Merlin is said to have torn a giant-built stone circle called the Giant's Dance from an Irish hilltop and shipped it to Britain — where it was raised again as Stonehenge.

The Legend

The earliest known written legend of Stonehenge's origin comes not from Britain but from Ireland. In Geoffrey of Monmouth's twelfth-century Historia Regum Britanniae, King Aurelius Ambrosius wants to raise a permanent memorial over the graves of British nobles treacherously slain by the Saxons, but no mason of the day can build anything worthy of the dead. The wizard Merlin tells him of a stone circle called the Giant's Ring, or Giant's Dance, standing on Mount Killaraus in Ireland — stones with healing and magical properties, said to have originally been carried from Africa by giants and set up on the mountain by their own strength, beyond the power of any living man to move.

The Story

Ambrosius sends his brother Uther Pendragon (father of the future King Arthur) to Ireland with fifteen thousand men to seize the stones. The Irish king Gillomanius raises an army to stop them, mocking the Britons for thinking they need Irish stones when they have plenty of their own, but is defeated in battle. When the Britons try to shift the huge stones by main force and fail, Merlin steps in with his own devices and 'engines', dismantling the circle and loading the stones onto ships. Carried back across the sea, the stones are re-erected on Salisbury Plain in exactly the pattern they had stood on Killaraus, becoming Stonehenge.

The Latin name mons Killaraus is generally read by scholars as 'the hill of Killare', pointing to the Hill of Uisneach in County Westmeath, a real hill long regarded as the ceremonial and mythological centre of Ireland and covered in genuine prehistoric monuments, including its own large stone, the Cat Stone (Ail na Míreann). While Geoffrey's history is treated by modern scholars as legendary rather than factual, the tale gained an unexpected real-world echo in 2021 when archaeologist Mike Parker Pearson's team found evidence that part of Stonehenge's bluestones had first stood as a stone circle at Waun Mawn in Wales's Preseli Hills before being dismantled and moved — a genuine prehistoric stone-circle relocation eerily close to the shape of the medieval legend, even if the specific Irish origin recorded by Geoffrey has never been archaeologically confirmed.

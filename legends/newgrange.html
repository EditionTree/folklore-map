Sacred Sites County Meath, Ireland

Newgrange

A passage tomb older than Stonehenge, built to align with the rising sun on the winter solstice — light flooding the inner chamber for seventeen minutes at dawn. In Irish mythology it is the home of Aengus, who tricked his father the Dagda out of it for eternity.

The Legend

Newgrange was built around 3200 BC, making it five centuries older than the Great Pyramid of Giza and a thousand years older than Stonehenge. Its passage and chamber were aligned with such precision that once a year, at the winter solstice, the rising sun shines directly down the 19-metre passage and illuminates the inner chamber for approximately seventeen minutes, flooding with light a space that is otherwise in complete darkness. The builders achieved this without instruments, without metal tools, and without the wheel.

At midwinter dawn, light enters the passage and reaches the heart of the tomb.

The Chamber of Sunrise

In Irish mythology, Newgrange, Brú na Bóinne, the Palace of the Boyne, is the home of the Dagda, the father-god, and later of his son Aengus Óg, the god of love and youth. The myth of how Aengus came to possess it is one of the most elegant in the cycle: he asked his father for the brú for a day and a night. When the Dagda tried to take it back, Aengus pointed out that all time is composed of a day and a night, and so he holds it still.

The mound lay overgrown and largely forgotten until 1699, when the landowner Charles Campbell had labourers quarry stone from it and they broke into the passage entrance instead. The Welsh antiquarian Edward Lhwyd visited soon after and recorded what he called the 'barbarous sculpture' carved into the stones, the first scholarly notice of the megalithic art that covers much of the site, including the great triple spiral, or triskele, carved into the three-tonne entrance stone. The tomb was not properly excavated until 1962 to 1975, when the archaeologist Michael J. O'Kelly led the first thorough investigation, uncovering the white quartz facade that now fronts the mound and confirming the solstice alignment by identifying the roofbox above the entrance through which the sunlight actually enters. His findings, published in 1982, placed Newgrange within the wider Brú na Bóinne complex alongside the neighbouring passage tombs of Knowth and Dowth, three related monuments O'Kelly read as expressions of the same Neolithic cult of the dead. The complex was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1993.

Newgrange continued to be a sacred site through the Bronze Age, Iron Age, and into the early Christian period, known to every people who came after its builders and interpreted according to their own cosmologies. The winter solstice sunrise it was built to capture is still observed today; the chamber is open to those who win the annual lottery draw, and thousands more watch the event streamed live.

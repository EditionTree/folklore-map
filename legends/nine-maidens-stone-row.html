Nine Maidens Stone Row

Nine Maidens stone row is an ancient monument in the parish of St Columb Major, Cornwall, England. The Nine Maidens are also known in Cornish as Naw-voz, or Naw-whoors meaning "the nine sisters". This late neolithic stone row is 2 miles (3.2 km) north of St Columb Major.

The Legend

The Nine Maidens is a late Neolithic or Bronze Age stone row near St Columb Major in Cornwall — an unusual alignment of standing stones, rather than a circle — and its name carries the familiar Cornish petrifaction legend: that the stones are young women turned to rock as a punishment for dancing on the Sabbath. The Cornish name, Naw-voz or Naw-whoors, 'the nine sisters', preserves the same tradition.

The Story

In the legend the nine maidens went out and fell to dancing on a Sunday, breaking the holy day, and for their sin were struck and frozen into the line of standing stones where they had been caught in their revelry. The motif is one repeated across Cornwall and beyond — told of the Merry Maidens, the Hurlers and other prehistoric monuments — in which the medieval Church recast the standing stones of the old religion as a stern warning against profaning the Sabbath with dance and play. A nearby single stone, the Old Man or the Fiddler, is sometimes named as the piper who played for their dance and shared their fate.

Several different Cornish monuments bear the 'Nine Maidens' name, a sign of how widespread the petrified-dancers tradition was across the county. As a stone row rather than a ring, the St Columb Major Nine Maidens is a distinctive example, its straggling line of weathered stones long explained to the country folk as a row of sisters caught mid-dance and turned to stone, keeping the old moral legend alive on the Cornish moor.

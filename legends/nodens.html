Nodens

A Romano-British god of healing, hunting, dogs, and the sea, worshipped at Lydney Park. His temple complex suggests a cult of cure, dream, water, and sacred hounds.

The Legend

Nodens was a Romano-British god of healing, hunting and the sea, best known from the great temple complex raised in his honour at Lydney Park, on a wooded hill above the River Severn in Gloucestershire. Built in the late Roman period, the sanctuary was no mere shrine but a healing centre: alongside the temple stood a range of rooms including what appears to be a guest-house for pilgrims and a long structure interpreted as a place for ritual sleep, where the sick would lie hoping for a curative dream or vision from the god — a practice of dream-incubation known from healing cults across the classical world.

A Romano-British god of healing, hunting, dogs, and the sea, worshipped at Lydney Park

The Divine Presence

A striking feature of his cult is the dog. Numerous figurines of dogs were offered at Lydney, and the hound — an animal long associated in antiquity with healing, its licking thought to cure wounds — seems to have been sacred to Nodens. The site also yielded a famous defixio, a curse tablet, in which a man named Silvianus asks the god to withhold health from whoever has stolen his ring until it is returned to the temple of Nodens. His association with the Severn estuary, whose great tidal bore surges past below the temple, marks him also as a god of water and the sea.

Nodens has deep roots and a long afterlife. His name connects him to the Irish Nuada of the Silver Hand and the Welsh Lludd Llaw Ereint, divine kings of the older mythologies, suggesting a once-major Celtic deity worshipped under a Roman guise. The Lydney site, excavated in the 1920s with J.R.R. Tolkien among those involved in studying its inscriptions, is thought to have helped inspire elements of his legendarium — giving this obscure healing god an unexpected thread into modern myth.

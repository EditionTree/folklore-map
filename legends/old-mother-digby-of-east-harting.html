Old Mother Digby of East Harting

A Sussex hare-witch could be caught out only by her own wounds — when a hound tore the hare she had become, the injury turned up on her body at home.

The Legend

In a cottage on Hog's Lane in East Harting, at the foot of the South Downs, lived a woman remembered as Old Mother Digby (born Mollen), reputed in the 18th century to have the witch's classic gift of turning herself into a hare. Like the goddess Hecate she was said to keep the company of dogs, and the local squire, Squire Russell of Tye Oak, made a habit of hunting a hare that always escaped him at the same spot — a drain by a sink-hole close to the old woman's house.

The Story

One day his hounds finally caught the hare by its hindquarters as it fled for the drain, tearing at it before it vanished down the hole. Squire Russell, suspecting what everyone already whispered, went straight to Mother Digby's door and let himself in — to find the old woman nursing a fresh wound on exactly the part of her body where the dog had seized the hare, the classic proof-by-injury that closes so many British witch-hare tales.

The story survives because it was collected and printed by the essayist E. V. Lucas in Highways and Byways in Sussex (1904), one of the standard Edwardian topographical guides to the county, which helped fix a number of otherwise oral South Downs traditions in print before they were lost. It sits alongside other Sussex hare-witch tales — an unnamed witch of Ditchling Common, a witch of Plumpton who could stop carts in the road — as part of a distinctly Wealden and Downland vein of the shapeshifting-witch legend that runs the length of southern England.

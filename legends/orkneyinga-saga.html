Orkneyinga Saga

The Old Norse saga of the earls of Orkney, binding the Northern Isles to Norway, Scotland, feud, conversion, sea-kings, and bloodline memory.

A visual interpretation of Orkneyinga Saga memory on the northern coast
The Legend

The Orkneyinga Saga — the 'Saga of the Orkney Islanders' — is the great medieval prose chronicle of the Norse earls of Orkney, composed in Iceland around the year 1200 and drawing on older tradition and verse. It is the single most important literary source for the history and legend of the Northern Isles in the Viking age, narrating the deeds of the Earls of Orkney across some three centuries, from the islands' mythic beginnings down to the late 12th century, and binding Orkney and Shetland into the wider world of Norway, Scotland and the Norse Atlantic.

The Saga by the Shore

The saga opens in legend, tracing the earls' line back to a fabulous ancestry among the kings of the far north, before moving into its sweeping history of feud, conquest, piracy, pilgrimage and politics. Its pages are crowded with vivid figures: Sigurd the Stout, who carried the fatal raven banner woven by his sorceress mother; the great Earl Thorfinn the Mighty; the warring nephews Earl Hákon and the pious Earl Magnus, whose murder on Egilsay made him Orkney's martyr-saint and led to the building of the magnificent cathedral in Kirkwall that bears his name; and the cultured, crusading Earl Rögnvald who raised it.

More than a history, the Orkneyinga Saga is a work of memory and identity, preserving the bloodlines, the blood-feuds and the conversion of a Norse society at the edge of the Scandinavian world. It remains the foundational text for understanding Viking-age and medieval Orkney and Shetland, the literary monument that fixes the Northern Isles' Norse heritage and keeps the sea-kings of the north alive in story.

Osmington White Horse

The Osmington White Horse is a hill figure cut into the limestone of Osmington Hill just north of Weymouth in Dorset in 1808. It is in the South Dorset Downs in the parish of Osmington.

A visual interpretation of the Osmington White Horse being cut into the Dorset hillside
The Legend

The Osmington White Horse is a large chalk hill-figure cut into the steep slope of the South Dorset Downs above Weymouth, depicting — unusually among Britain's white horses — a horse with a rider: King George III, mounted and in cocked hat. It was cut in 1808, in honour of the king who had made the seaside town of Weymouth fashionable by his repeated visits, taking the new fashion of sea-bathing there for his health, to the great profit of the town.

The mounted figure has looked out across Weymouth Bay since 1808.

The King Riding from Weymouth

A well-known local legend attaches to the figure and its supposed effect on the king. The horse is carved striding away from Weymouth rather than towards it — and tradition holds that George III, when he saw himself depicted riding out of the town he had so favoured, took it as a snub, an unwelcome hint that he should leave and not return, and was so offended that he never came back to Weymouth again. In a grimmer version of the tale, the man who designed or cut the figure was so mortified at having unintentionally insulted the king by facing the horse the wrong way that he was driven to despair and took his own life.

Whether or not the king truly took umbrage — and his visits did indeed cease around that time — the story has clung to the hillside figure ever since, a piece of Dorset folklore reading royal displeasure into the direction of a chalk horse. As a monument to the Georgian craze for Weymouth and a hill-figure wrapped in a tale of accidental insult, the Osmington White Horse keeps a curious local legend grazing on the downs above the bay.

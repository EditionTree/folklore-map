Owlman of Mawnan

A huge owl-like creature with red eyes and black claws, first sighted hovering over Mawnan church tower in 1976. Multiple witnesses reported encounters over the following years.

The Legend

The Owlman of Mawnan is a modern Cornish cryptid centred on the village of Mawnan near Falmouth. The first reported sighting came on 17 April 1976, when two young girls, June and Vicky Melling, said they saw a huge feathered creature like a man-sized owl hovering over the tower of St Mawnan and St Stephen's Church, set in dense woodland above the Helford estuary. Their father was so disturbed he cut the family holiday short. Further reports followed in early July 1976, from witnesses including Sally Chapman, Barbara Perry and Jane Greenwood, describing much the same thing: a grey or silvery winged figure the size of a person, with pointed ears, glowing red eyes and pincer-like black claws.

Witnesses described a huge owl-like humanoid with red eyes and black pincer-like claws.

The Figure Above the Church

The witnesses, often young women, described the creature as terrifying rather than aggressive; it would rise into the trees and vanish when approached. The sightings clustered around the churchyard and the woods, and recurred sporadically into the 1980s and 1990s, keeping the story alive.

The story first reached print in a pamphlet titled 'Morgawr: The Monster of Falmouth Bay', circulated in Cornwall in 1976 under the pseudonym 'Anthony Mawnan-Peller', alongside contemporary coverage in the Falmouth Packet newspaper the same year. The name behind the pseudonym was the writer Tony 'Doc' Shiels, a showman and self-described wizard whose promotion of the case, and its clear links to his own contemporaneous 'Morgawr' sea-serpent claims, led many to suspect the whole affair was at least partly a hoax or a piece of folkloric theatre.

Whether misidentified bird, escaped exotic, invention or genuine mystery, the Owlman has earned a settled place in Cornish folklore as a contemporary legend, proof that the old impulse to people the wild, wooded corners of the land with uncanny watchers did not end with the modern age, but simply took new shape around a lonely church above the Helford.

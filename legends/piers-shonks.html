Piers Shonks

A giant lord of Brent Pelham slew a dragon under a yew, then outwitted the vengeful Devil by being buried inside the church wall, neither in the church nor out of it, beyond the Devil's claim.

The Legend

Sir Piers Shonks is the dragon-slayer of Brent Pelham in Hertfordshire, remembered as a benevolent giant, twenty feet tall, who was lord of the manor and rid the district of a monstrous serpent lairing in a cave beneath an ancient yew. Riding out from his moated house with his hounds, he drove his lance down the dragon's throat and killed it.

outwitted the vengeful Devil by being buried inside the church wall

Neither in nor Out

No sooner was the beast dead than the Devil appeared, furious at the loss of his favourite, and swore to have Shonks's soul whether he were buried inside the church or outside it. Dying, Shonks called for his bow and loosed a last arrow, vowing to be buried wherever it fell. It flew across the village and struck the north wall of St Mary's church, lodging in the stone, and there he was entombed, neither within the church nor without, so cheating the Devil of his claim.

His monument still sits in a recess of that north wall, a black marble slab carved with the symbols of the four Evangelists and a cross thrust into the jaws of a dragon. Above it hangs a later tablet, believed to have been added by a seventeenth-century vicar, bearing a Latin and English verse that pointedly ranks Shonks above the great dragon-slayers of legend and myth: 'Nothing of Cadmus nor St George, those names of great renown, survives them but their fames; time was so sharp set as to make no bones of theirs, nor of their monumental stones. But Shonks, one serpent kills, t'other defies, and in this wall, as in a fortress, lies.' The verse, and the tomb it accompanies, have kept the story wedged as firmly into local memory as Shonks himself is wedged into the wall.

Historical Context

Origins & Dating

Historical Period17th century (inscription, per the Salmon attribution) to 1700 (first known written narrative account); the underlying tomb slab is 13th century, and the '1086' death date carved on it is a later, unsourced antiquarian tradition of unknown origin
Earliest Written RecordEarliest known written account of the legend is by antiquarian Henry Chauncy in The Historical Antiquities of Hertfordshire (1700). The tomb's Latin verse inscription is attributed, per historian Nathanael Salmon writing in 1728, to a vicar of Brent Pelham (Rev. Raphael Keen, who died in 1614 after nearly 76 years as vicar) -- which, if accurate, would put the inscription's composition before 1614, though that attribution is itself secondhand (recorded 1728) and unverified.

Folklore dates are often approximate, especially where a story predates any surviving written source.

Piskie

Small West Country fae known for leading travellers astray, especially on moors and lanes. To be 'piskie-led' is to wander in circles while the familiar world quietly rearranges itself.

A visual interpretation of a traveller being piskie-led on the West Country moors
The Legend

The Piskie (or Pixie) is the characteristic small fairy of Cornwall and Devon, a merry, mischievous, often kindly being of the moors, lanes and old places of the far South-West. Piskies are typically described as little, with pointed ears and pointed caps, sometimes red-haired and squint-eyed, dressed in rags or green, and given to dancing in rings on the moor by moonlight, riding the farmers' colts in the night, and helping — or hindering — about the farm. Like the brownie, a piskie might thresh corn or do chores for a household it favoured, and a bowl of cream left out was the proper thanks.

To be piskie-led is to wander in circles while the familiar world quietly rearranges itself.

The Path That Circles Back

Their most famous trick gives the West Country its enduring phrase: to be 'piskie-led'. A traveller crossing the moor or following a familiar lane at dusk might suddenly find themselves hopelessly lost, wandering in circles though they knew the way perfectly, while the piskies led them astray for sport — the landscape itself seeming to shift and rearrange. The traditional remedy was to turn a coat or a pocket inside out, which broke the enchantment and let the bewildered wanderer find the road again.

Piskies were also linked in some traditions to the spirits of the unbaptised dead, or of the old pagan folk of the land, lingering on in a diminished, fading state. Whether helpful sprite, moorland trickster or wistful remnant of an older people, the piskie is inseparable from the identity of Cornwall and Devon, where 'piskie-led' travellers and piskie-haunted moors remain a living part of local lore.

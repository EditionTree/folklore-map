Witches Pittenweem, Fife

Pittenweem Witch Trials

In 1705 accusations by a local teenager led to the persecution of several people in Pittenweem. The case belongs on the map as a documented local witch-trial tradition, while its summary should keep the focus on the victims rather than repeat the accusations as fact.

A visual interpretation of the Pittenweem witch trials
The Legend

The Pittenweem witch trials of 1704–05 were among the last and most shameful of Scotland's witch persecutions, in the little Fife fishing burgh on the East Neuk. They began when a local teenager, Patrick Morton, fell into fits and accused several townsfolk — chief among them Beatrix Laing — of bewitching him; the accused were imprisoned, deprived of sleep, and tortured into confession.

In a fishing burgh at the edge of the sea, accusation spread through neighbours and families.

Fear on the Harbour Street

The darkest chapter was the fate of Janet Cornfoot. Having escaped and been recaptured, she was seized in January 1705 not by the courts but by a mob, who beat her, swung her by the heels from a rope between a ship and the shore, and finally crushed her to death under a door piled with stones — then drove a horse and sledge over her body to be sure. No one was ever punished for the lynching. The case stands on the map as a stark memorial to the cruelty of the witch-hunt at its very end, when official scepticism had already begun to grow.

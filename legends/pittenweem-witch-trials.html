Pittenweem Witch Trials

A teenager's fits of bewitchment tipped Pittenweem into Scotland's last witch panic, ending when a mob beat Janet Cornfoot, swung her from a rope, and crushed her to death under a door of stones.

The Legend

The Pittenweem witch trials of 1704–1705 were among the last and most shameful of Scotland's witch persecutions, playing out in the small Fife fishing burgh on the East Neuk. They began when a teenage boy, Patrick Morton, fell into fits and accused several townsfolk, chief among them Beatrix Laing, of bewitching him. The accused were imprisoned, deprived of sleep, and tortured into confession; one of them, Thomas Brown, died in custody before the case reached its most infamous victim.

In a fishing burgh at the edge of the sea, accusation spread through neighbours and families.

Fear on the Harbour Street

The darkest chapter was the fate of Janet Cornfoot. Having escaped and been recaptured, she was seized on 30 January 1705 not by the courts but by a mob, who beat her, swung her by the heels from a rope between a ship and the shore, and finally crushed her to death under a door piled with stones — then drove a horse and sledge over her body to be sure. The Privy Council investigated the killing afterwards, but no one was ever punished for it. It happened at the very end of Scotland's witch-hunting era, when official scepticism about such prosecutions was already spreading elsewhere in the country — too late to save her. The sequence survives in contemporary court and kirk-session records, catalogued today in the University of Edinburgh's Survey of Scottish Witchcraft database, which names the Pittenweem accused directly.

Pixie

Small, mischievous fae of the West Country with pointed ears and a gift for leading travellers astray. Being pixie-led means walking in circles until you turn your coat inside out to break the spell, and the pixies were also said to ride farm horses through the night, returning them exhausted and sweat-soaked by dawn.

The Legend

The pixie, or piskie, is the small fairy of the West Country, Cornwall and Devon especially, distinct in name and temper from the fairies of other regions. Often described as little, ragged, red-haired and pointed of ear, pixies are above all mischievous: they dance in rings on the moor by night, ride and tangle the manes of farm horses, and delight in leading travellers astray.

They steal horses to ride through the night, returning them exhausted and sweated by dawn.

The Riders in the Stable

To be 'pixie-led' was a real and feared misfortune, to find oneself walking in circles on familiar ground, unable to reach home, the paths confused by laughing unseen sprites. The classic remedy was to turn one's coat or a pocket inside out, which broke the enchantment. Further along the southern coast, in Hampshire and Dorset, the same family of belief produced the colt-pixy, a pixie that took the shape of a shaggy grey pony to lure farm horses off the paths and into bogs, leaving them found sweated and exhausted, their manes tangled into knots, by morning. Yet pixies could also be kindly, threshing corn or doing housework for the deserving in the manner of a brownie, provided they were neither watched nor thanked with gifts of clothing.

Cornwall gave its pixies a queen of sorts in Joan the Wad, whose name means 'torch' in old dialect. A cottage industry in her image grew up in the twentieth century: small brass lucky charms bearing her name, registered by F. T. Nettleinghame in 1932 and still collected today, with examples held at the Museum of Witchcraft and Magic in Boscastle. On Dartmoor and the Cornish moors the piskie remained a living part of country belief well into modern times.

Origins & Dating

Earliest Written RecordFirst substantially recorded in print by Anna Eliza Bray in her letters to Robert Southey, published as Traditions, Legends, Superstitions, and Sketches of Devonshire, on the Borders of the Tamar and the Tavy (1836); belief in pixies is understood to be considerably older and was transmitted orally before this.

Folklore dates are often approximate, especially where a story predates any surviving written source.

Plynlimon

PIM-lih-mon

A great giant of Welsh legend whose three daughters, Hafren, Gwy, and Rheidol, raced each other from his summit to the sea. Hafren reached the sea first, winning greatest fame, and her name became the River Severn. Plynlimon watches still from the highest ground in mid-Wales, father of rivers.

The Legend

Plynlimon (Pumlumon), the high boggy massif of the Cambrian Mountains, was in Welsh tradition the resting place of a great giant whose three daughters, water nymphs named Hafren, Gwy and Rheidol, gave their names to the three rivers that rise from its slopes: the Severn, the Wye and the Rheidol.

Folklore made the rivers his three daughters: Hafren (Severn), Gwy (Wye) and Rheidol.

The Father of Rivers

Their father set each a single day to reach the sea, promising each whatever land she crossed before nightfall as her dowry. Hafren rose first and took the longest, easiest road east, winning the most fertile country and, in time, the greatest fame as the River Severn; Gwy chose to wander, seeking what she called beauty and tranquillity, and became the meandering Wye. Rheidol, the youngest and, by her father's own complaint, an incorrigible lie-abed, was roused only at midday and had to sprint due west down the steepest ground, bounding over rock to reach Aberystwyth and the sea first of the three.

The tale is a poetic geography, explaining in the shape of a giant's daughters why three rivers of such different lengths and characters should all spring from one bleak mountain, the watershed of Wales made into a family story.

Origins & Dating

Earliest Written RecordFirst recorded in print by Sir John Rhys in Celtic Folklore: Welsh and Manx (1901), who states the legend of the three sisters was told to him in his youth in 1840s Ceredigion; no earlier manuscript source has been identified, though earlier antiquarians (Geoffrey of Monmouth, Edward Lhuyd, Thomas Pennant) discussed related Hafren/Sabrina material that may draw on older, otherwise-unrecorded manuscript or oral sources.

Folklore dates are often approximate, especially where a story predates any surviving written source.

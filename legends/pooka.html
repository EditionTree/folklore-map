Pooka

A shape-shifting spirit of Irish folklore, most often a dark horse with burning eyes that offers rides to unwary travellers before galloping at terrifying speed through bogs and rivers until dawn, when it vanishes and leaves its rider far from home and shaking. It can also take the form of a goat, hare, or goblin.

The Legend

The Pooka, púca in Irish, is a shape-shifting spirit of Irish folklore, appearing as a sleek black horse, a goat, a hare, a great dog, or an eagle, often with burning golden eyes. In its horse form it was notorious for offering, or forcing, a wild night ride across the country, sweeping up the unwary and setting them down again at dawn, shaken and far from home. Yet the Pooka was not purely malevolent: it could give sound counsel and warnings, and was treated with wary respect.

Sweeping up the unwary and setting them down again, shaken and far from home, by dawn.

The Ride Before Dawn

It was closely bound to the turning of the year, and especially to the days after Samhain, when by tradition the Pooka spoiled the wild fruits still left on the bushes. Blackberries picked after 1 November were said to have been spat on, or worse, by the creature, and were no longer fit to gather. Its name survives in the Irish landscape too, most famously at Poulaphouca (Poll a' Phúca, 'the pooka's hole') on the River Liffey between counties Wicklow and Kildare, where a dramatic waterfall was long held to be one of the creature's haunts, a spot later given a second life in James Joyce's Ulysses.

Its name echoes across the Celtic and Germanic worlds, the Welsh pwca, the English Puck, marking it as a very old figure indeed. The Pooka remains among the best-loved and least predictable of all Ireland's fairy beings.

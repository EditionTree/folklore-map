Pooka

A shape-shifting spirit of Irish folklore, most often a dark horse with burning eyes that offers rides to unwary travellers before galloping at terrifying speed through bogs and rivers until dawn, when it vanishes and leaves its rider far from home and shaking. It can also take the form of a goat, hare, or goblin.

The Legend

The Pooka (púca in Irish) is a shape-shifting spirit of Irish folklore, appearing as a sleek black horse, a goat, a hare, a great dog or an eagle, often with burning golden eyes. In its horse form it was notorious for offering, or forcing, a wild night-ride across the country, sweeping up the unwary and setting them down again at dawn, shaken and far from home. Yet the Pooka was not purely malevolent: it could give sound counsel and warnings, and was treated with wary respect.

The Ride Before Dawn

The National Folklore Collection, gathered from Irish schoolchildren in the 1930s, preserves a plainer, more physical kind of encounter. In an account from Boher, County Westmeath, a local man named Paddy Ward, known around the parish as a bit of a "tricester" and sportsman, took a dare one Halloween night after a wedding to walk through Killan graveyard at two o'clock in the morning. He got about halfway round before something knocked him flat, an encounter neighbours put down to the Pooka ever after.

The timing was no accident. The Pooka was closely bound to the turning of the year, and especially to the days after Samhain, when by tradition it spoiled the wild fruits still left on the bushes, which were no longer to be gathered. Its name echoes across the Celtic and Germanic worlds, in the Welsh pwca and the English Puck, marking it as a very old figure indeed. The Pooka remains among the best-loved and least predictable of all Ireland's fairy beings.

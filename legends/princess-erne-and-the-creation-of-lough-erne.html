Princess Erne and the Creation of Lough Erne

Queen Medb's lady-in-waiting Erne fled in terror from the monster of Oweynagat. She and her maidens drowned in the river, their bodies dissolving into the great lake that bears her name.

The Legend

Lough Erne, the great chain of island-strewn lakes spreading through County Fermanagh, takes its name in Irish legend from a woman called Erne, chief among the maidens attending the warrior-queen Medb at her royal seat of Cruachan in Connacht, keeper, tradition says, of her combs and caskets. The story of how the lake came to be is one of the etiological myths so common in Ireland, in which a feature of the land is explained as the drowned, dissolved body of a goddess or noblewoman.

The Water Legend at the Heart of the Tale

The tale tells that Erne and her fellow maidens were at Cruachan when terror came upon them: a fearful monster, in the tradition a being that emerged from the cave of Oweynagat, the 'Cave of the Cats', the famous Otherworld entrance at Cruachan that breathed out destructive creatures at Samhain. Fleeing the horror in panic, Erne and her maidens ran northward, away from Connacht, until they came to a river, where they were overwhelmed and drowned. Their bodies, the legend says, dissolved into the water and spread out across the low country, and from them formed the broad waters of Lough Erne, which has carried her name ever since.

The story survives because it was written down as part of the dindshenchas, the medieval Irish 'lore of places', a genre of poems and prose explaining how features of the landscape got their names. Erne's tale is told as Poem 85 of the Metrical Dindshenchas, transmitted through the Book of Leinster recension of around 1160 and later manuscripts including the Rennes Dindshenchas and the Book of Ballymote; the scholar Edward Gwynn first edited and translated the full cycle for the Royal Irish Academy in 1906, the edition through which most readers still meet the tale.

Scholars connect the name more deeply still to an ancient goddess Érann, eponymous deity of the Érainn, one of the early peoples of Ireland, so that Loch Éirne may mean 'the lake of the goddess Érann', the drowning legend read as a folk memory of a divine figure whose power dissolved into the water to give life to the land. Either way, the great lake of Fermanagh keeps in its very name the memory of a woman of myth who fled a monster and became the water itself.

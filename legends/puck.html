Puck

The oldest Old Thing in England — an ancient spirit of place tied to the Sussex Weald, known since before the Normans came. Rudyard Kipling immortalised him as the guardian of England's deep memory.

A visual interpretation of Puck as an ancient spirit of the Sussex Weald
The Legend

Puck is one of the oldest and most deep-rooted of English fairy spirits, a being whose name and nature reach back well before the Norman Conquest. In the older tradition he is a hobgoblin or household sprite, also known as Robin Goodfellow — a shape-shifting, prank-playing spirit who might help with the chores like a brownie or lead night-travellers astray like a will-o'-the-wisp, blow out candles, sour the ale, and laugh his famous laugh at the confusion he caused. The word 'puck' itself (Old English pūca) is an ancient term for a goblin or mischievous nature-spirit, with cousins across the Germanic and Celtic worlds.

The Oldest Old Thing

Shakespeare fixed his fame for all time in A Midsummer Night's Dream, where Puck — Robin Goodfellow — is the merry, blundering servant of Oberon, king of the fairies, who anoints the wrong lovers' eyes and sets the whole comedy of errors in motion, and who speaks the play's gentle closing apology. This gave the mischievous English hobgoblin his most enduring literary shape.

Centuries later Rudyard Kipling drew on the older, deeper Puck in Puck of Pook's Hill, setting him in the Sussex Weald and naming him 'the oldest Old Thing in England' — the last of the People of the Hills, a spirit of place and memory who conjures up figures from the country's long past for two Sussex children. In this guise Puck becomes the guardian of England's deep history, a fittingly ancient role for a spirit who was already old when the fairies of romance were young.

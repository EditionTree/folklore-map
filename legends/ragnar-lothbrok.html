Ragnar Lothbrok

Legendary Norse king who raided England, was captured by King Ælla of Northumbria and thrown into a pit of snakes at York, dying there, singing. His sons launched the Great Heathen Army to avenge him and conquered half of England.

The Legend

Ragnar Lothbrok occupies the border between history and legend so completely that separating them may be impossible and is probably beside the point. The sagas describe a great Viking king and raider who terrorised both England and France across the ninth century; the historical record is equivocal, with no contemporary source naming him clearly. But his legend accumulated detail and force long before the sagas fixed it in writing.

He sang as he died and warned that the little pigs would grunt if they knew what the old boar suffered.

The Old Boar in the Snake Pit

The most vivid moment in his tradition is his death. Having raided Northumbria, he was captured by King Ælla and thrown into a pit of snakes at York. The sagas record that he sang as he died, a death-song called the Krákumál, in which he declared that he was laughing and would soon drink ale with the gods, and that his last words were a warning: 'The little pigs would grunt now if they knew what the old boar suffers.' His sons heard the news.

The response was the Great Heathen Army of 865, the most significant Scandinavian invasion of England: led by Ivar the Boneless, Halfdan, and Ubbe, it conquered Northumbria, East Anglia, and Mercia. Ælla was killed, according to the sagas by the blood eagle. Ragnar's sons effectively remade the map of England.

Modern scholarship is sceptical that any single man stands behind all of this. The most thorough study, Rory McTurk's 1991 Oxford analysis of the sagas and their sources, concludes that Ragnar is a composite figure, several historical and legendary Vikings fused together over centuries of retelling into one great and satisfyingly named king. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle itself, the closest thing to a contemporary record of the Great Heathen Army, names only Ivar, Halfdan and Ubba as its leaders and says nothing of Ragnar or a revenge motive at all; that connection belongs entirely to the later Norse sagas. Composite or not, the pit of snakes at York, the death-song, and the revenge army have made him one of the most resonant figures in the Norse tradition's collision with Britain.

Historical Context

Origins & Dating

Historical PeriodLegendary/semi-historical figure notionally placed in the 9th century; the surviving textual sources are 9th century (Ragnarsdrapa, if it does refer to him) through 13th century (the sagas)
Earliest Written RecordPossibly first alluded to in Ragnarsdrapa, a 9th-century skaldic poem by Bragi Boddason (though scholar Vesteinn Olason argues the poem's dedicatee may be a different, later Ragnar, not Lothbrok); the first source to explicitly name Ragnar Lodbrok as a Danish king and narrate his death in a snake pit at the hands of King AElla is Book IX of Saxo Grammaticus's Gesta Danorum, c. 1200 (sometimes cited as c. 1185); the Icelandic Ragnars saga lodbrokar and Ragnarssona thattr, which supply the fullest saga narrative, both date to the 13th century

Folklore dates are often approximate, especially where a story predates any surviving written source.

Renwick Cockatrice

Workmen demolishing the old church at Renwick are said to have loosed a great winged creature from its foundations, a cockatrice bred out of the ancient stonework. John Tallantire beat it to death with the only wood that could pierce its deadly gaze, a branch of rowan, and the parish kept the branch as proof.

The Legend

The Cumbrian village of Renwick, in the Eden valley below the Pennines, keeps a curious tale tied to the rebuilding of its parish church. As workmen pulled down the old building, they are said to have disturbed a monstrous winged creature, a cockatrice, or in some tellings a great bat, that flew up out of the foundations and terrified the parish until a brave villager, named in tradition as John Tallantire, killed it with a branch of rowan, the only wood accounted proof against a cockatrice's deadly gaze.

A great winged creature flew out from the foundations of the old church at Renwick.

The Thing Beneath the Church

The date usually given for the demolition, 1733, does not actually appear in the earliest surviving account. That account is a set of agricultural footnotes contributed by the surveyor John Housman to William Hutchinson's History of the County of Cumberland, published in 1794, which places the event 'about 200 years ago', pointing instead to the 1590s or 1600s. Housman also mentions a document, supposedly dated to the seventh regnal year of James I (1609 or 1610), that recorded Tallantire's tithe exemption, though he admitted he was never shown it himself.

For his deed Tallantire and his lands were said to have been freed from tithes, a claim tested and not upheld when the Tithe Commutation Schedule was drawn up for the parish in 1842. The story also gave the villagers their nickname, the 'Renwick Bats', and stands among the small clutch of English cockatrice legends, alongside Wherwell in Hampshire and others, in which a serpent-fowl hatched from a hidden egg must be put down by cunning or a charmed weapon. That the creature was already a familiar figure in the region is suggested by a fifteenth-century misericord carved with a cockatrice in Carlisle Cathedral, decades before Renwick's own story was ever written down.

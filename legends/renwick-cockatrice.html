Renwick Cockatrice

When workmen demolished the old church at Renwick in 1733, a great winged creature flew out from the foundations — a cockatrice hatched from the ancient stonework. John Tallantire beat it to death with a branch of rowan, the only wood proof against its deadly gaze. The church was rebuilt, but the rowan was kept.

The legend

The Cumbrian village of Renwick, in the Eden valley below the Pennines, keeps a curious tale tied to the rebuilding of its church in 1733. As workmen pulled down the old building, they are said to have disturbed a monstrous winged creature — a cockatrice, or in some tellings a great bat — that flew up out of the foundations and terrified the parish until a brave villager, named in tradition as John Tallantire, killed it with a branch of rowan, the wood proof against evil.

The story

For his deed Tallantire and his lands were said to have been freed from tithes, a detail that hints at a real local dispute dressed in legendary clothing. The story also gave the villagers their nickname, the 'Renwick Bats', and stands among the small clutch of English cockatrice legends — with Wherwell in Hampshire and others — in which a serpent-fowl hatched from a hidden egg must be put down by cunning or by a charmed weapon.

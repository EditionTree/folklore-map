Deities Ceredigion, Wales

Rheidol

HRAY-dol

The wildest of Plynlimon's three daughters, she scorned the long roads her sisters took and tore straight down the mountain to Cardigan Bay, arriving first and leaving a valley of cataracts behind her.

The Legend

In the folklore of mid Wales, the Afon Rheidol is the third and youngest daughter of the mountain giant Plynlimon, sister to Hafren (the Severn) and Gwy (the Wye). When their father challenged the three river spirits to race to the sea, promising each the territory she could cover in a single day as her dowry, Rheidol chose the boldest and most direct strategy. She flung herself straight west down the mountainside, carving a steep and narrow valley through rock and forest, and reached Cardigan Bay at the place now called Aberystwyth before either of her sisters had even cleared the foothills.

The Race Down Plynlimon

Her victory came at a cost: her territory was the smallest of the three, a mere twenty-four miles of mountain stream compared to Hafren's 220-mile empire or Gwy's graceful 134-mile border journey. But what Rheidol lacked in length she made up in ferocity. The valley she carved is one of the steepest river gorges in Wales, dropping from the boggy plateau of Plynlimon at over 600 metres to sea level in a succession of falls and rapids that have made the Rheidol a byword for wild water. The Vale of Rheidol Railway, built in 1902 to serve the lead mines of Devil's Bridge, follows her course and gives passengers a visceral sense of the gradient the legend describes.

The three-sisters legend was first recorded by the Celtic scholar John Rhŷs from oral tradition in 1840s Ceredigion, and later set down fully in his 1901 study Celtic Folklore: Welsh and Manx. Some versions substitute the Ystwyth for Rheidol as the third sister (both rivers rise on Plynlimon and reach the sea near Aberystwyth), but the Rheidol variant is the more widely told, perhaps because her short, dramatic descent makes the better story. The legend gives each river a personality, Hafren the ambitious, Gwy the contemplative, Rheidol the impetuous, and in doing so transforms the hydrology of central Wales into a family drama.

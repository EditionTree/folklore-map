Legendary Figures Rathlin Island, County Antrim

Robert the Bruce

Legend says that Robert the Bruce sheltered in a cave after defeat and watched a spider repeatedly attempt to build its web. When the spider finally succeeded, its persistence inspired Bruce to continue his own struggle. Several caves claim the tale; Rathlin Island preserves a strong local association.

The Legend

Robert the Bruce was born in 1274 into a family with a real claim to the Scottish throne, and seized it by force. In February 1306 he stabbed his rival John Comyn before the high altar of Greyfriars Church in Dumfries, an act that saw him excommunicated by the Pope even as his own bishop absolved him. Six weeks later he was crowned King of Scots at Scone. The crown brought him no immediate safety: defeated by the English at the Battle of Methven that same year, Bruce fled into hiding, his fortunes at their lowest ebb.

When the spider finally succeeded, its persistence inspired Bruce to continue his own struggle.

The Lesson in the Cave

The most enduring legend of these hunted months belongs not to the battlefield but to a cave, where Bruce is said to have sheltered. Tradition most often sets the cave on Rathlin Island off the Antrim coast, where Bruce had fled in 1306. Sheltering there, despairing, he watched a spider try again and again to swing a thread across a gap in the rock to anchor its web.

Six times the spider failed, and six times it tried once more, until at last the thread held and the web was begun. Bruce, who had himself been defeated six times, took the spider's persistence as a sign and resolved to fight on. Walter Scott gave the story its widest audience in Tales of a Grandfather (1828), and by his telling it was already a composite: some earlier versions attach the spider to Bruce's ally Sir James Douglas rather than to Bruce himself, and Arran's King's Cave presses a rival claim alongside Rathlin's. Whichever cave and whichever man, the tale gave the English-speaking world its proverb of 'if at first you don't succeed, try, try again.'

The persistence the legend celebrates was, at least, real. Bruce fought his way back from that low point through years of guerrilla campaigning, recapturing castle after castle, until his heavily outnumbered army broke a far larger English force at the Battle of Bannockburn on 23 to 24 June 1314. The Declaration of Arbroath in 1320 won papal recognition of Scotland's independence, and by the Treaty of Edinburgh-Northampton in 1328, England formally renounced its claim to overlordship, months before Bruce's death.

Bruce died in 1329 asking that his heart be carried to the Holy Land, since illness had stopped him fulfilling his own crusading vow. It was the same Sir James Douglas, sometimes credited with the spider itself, who set out early in 1330 with the heart in a silver casket around his neck. Douglas never reached Jerusalem: diverted to Spain to fight alongside King Alfonso XI against the Moors, he was killed at the Battle of Teba. A story added to the record two centuries later has him hurl the casket into the enemy line and charge after it, but what's certain is that his body and the heart were both recovered and brought home. Douglas was buried in Scotland, and Bruce's heart was laid beneath the high altar of Melrose Abbey, where a marker still shows where it rests.

Historical Context

Origins & Dating

Earliest Written RecordStory of a persistent spider inspiring perseverance first appears in print attached to Sir James Douglas (not Bruce) in David Hume of Godscroft's History of the House and Race of Douglas and Angus, published posthumously in 1643/1644. The version specifically starring Robert the Bruce was popularized by Sir Walter Scott in Tales of a Grandfather (1828).
Historical PeriodHistorical setting: Bruce's real flight and hiding after his 1306 defeats at Methven and Dail Righ, including overwintering on Rathlin Island in 1306-1307. The spider legend itself is a literary accretion first recorded in the 1640s (re: Douglas) and reattached to Bruce in 1828.

Folklore dates are often approximate, especially where a story predates any surviving written source.

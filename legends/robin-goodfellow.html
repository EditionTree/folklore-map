Robin Goodfellow

A puckish household and woodland spirit, prankster, shape-shifter, and night-wanderer. Robin Goodfellow links older English fairy belief to the literary Puck of Shakespeare and Kipling.

The Legend

Robin Goodfellow, better known as Puck, is the archetypal hobgoblin of English folklore, a mischievous household and woodland sprite, neither wholly good nor truly wicked. By night he might thresh the corn, churn the butter and sweep the hearth in return for a bowl of cream left out in thanks; cross or neglect him, and he would sour the milk, tangle the horses' manes and lead benighted travellers astray with his laughter, crying 'Ho, ho, ho!'

The Otherworld at the Heart of the Tale

A seventeenth-century ballad, possibly by Ben Jonson, has him list his own tricks: taking the shape of an ox, a hound or a horse to gallop wild-eyed riders through hedge and marsh, blowing out the candles at a gathering to steal a kiss from a shrieking maid, and carding wool or spinning flax by night for households too poor or too idle to do it themselves. He pinches lazy servants and strips their bedclothes, exposes women who gossip and scheme against their neighbours, and leads travellers off the path with mimicked voices, laughing all the while.

He was the son, in some tellings, of the fairy king Oberon, and a shape-shifter who could also take the form of a hare or a will-o'-the-wisp. William Shakespeare fixed his character forever in 'A Midsummer Night's Dream' (c.1595), where Puck serves Oberon and works the play's enchantments, while the chapbook 'Robin Good-Fellow, His Mad Pranks and Merry Jests' (1628) gathered his older lore. He remains the very pattern of the English trickster-fairy.

Historical Context

Origins & Dating

Historical PeriodLate medieval/early modern England, with the name in circulation by the 1590s at latest and the surviving chapbook dated 1628
Earliest Written RecordThe character appears in oral/household tradition prior to print; the earliest known printed collection specifically under this name is the chapbook Robin Good-fellow: His Mad Prankes, and Merry Jests, printed in London for F. Grove, 1628 (two parts, each with its own woodcut title page). Shakespeare's A Midsummer Night's Dream (c.1595-96) already refers to Puck explicitly as 'Robin Goodfellow', predating the surviving chapbook by roughly three decades and implying the character/name was already established in popular tradition by the 1590s.

Folklore dates are often approximate, especially where a story predates any surviving written source.

Robin Hood

Outlaw of Sherwood, robber of the rich and friend of the poor, the archetypal English rebel against unjust authority, told since the 13th century. The Major Oak is said to have sheltered him between raids.

The Legend

Robin Hood has haunted the English imagination for at least seven centuries, appearing in ballads as early as the late thirteenth century, predating the printing press, the Renaissance, and almost every institution of the England we recognise. Whether he was a specific historical figure, a composite of forgotten outlaws, or a literary invention who then acquired geographical memory, he belongs to the land as much as any named king.

Robin Hood is the outlaw of Sherwood Forest, the archetypal English rebel against unjust authority.

The Outlaw Under the Greenwood

The core of the legend is simple and endlessly renewable: a skilled archer, living under the greenwood of Sherwood Forest, who robs from those who have too much and gives to those who have nothing, and who cannot be taken by the corrupt sheriff who hunts him. Maid Marian, Friar Tuck, Little John, and Will Scarlet are later additions; the early ballads show a harder, less romantic figure, sometimes violent, sometimes devotional, always elusive.

Historians hunting for a real Robin Hood behind the legend keep returning to one especially good candidate: a Yorkshire outlaw named Robert Hod, recorded as a debtor to St Peter's, York, in the 1226 assize rolls, and traceable under variant spellings, Hood, Hod, Hobbehod, through nine successive Yorkshire Pipe Rolls between 1226 and 1234. He is the earliest known outlaw actually called Robin Hood, though nothing in the record shows he was ever a bandit rather than simply a man who owed money and fled the law, and at least half a dozen other candidates have been proposed over the centuries with just as little proof. The name may equally have been a stock alias, attached again and again to whichever outlaw the teller had in mind.

The Major Oak, a vast pollarded oak in the heart of Sherwood Forest near Edwinstowe, is traditionally associated with Robin's camp, its hollow trunk large enough to have sheltered men. It is almost certainly far too young to have been there in any historical Robin's time, but tradition does not require botanical accuracy. The tree still stands, propped and protected, and visitors still come.

Historical Context

Origins & Dating

Historical PeriodMid-to-late 14th century oral tradition (attested 1377) through 15th-century ballad tradition (manuscript c.1450, print c.1500)
Earliest Written RecordFirst recorded in William Langland's Piers Plowman (B-text, commonly dated to 1377), in which the character Sloth says 'I can noughte perfitly my pater-noster as the prest it syngeth, / But I can rymes of Robyn Hood and Randolf erle of Chestre.' The earliest surviving narrative ballad is 'Robin Hood and the Monk', preserved in Cambridge University manuscript Ff.5.48, written shortly after 1450. The earliest printed ballad collection is 'A Gest of Robyn Hode' ('A Lytell Geste of Robyn Hode'), with surviving printed editions from around 1500 (composition thought to date from the latter half of the 1400s).

Folklore dates are often approximate, especially where a story predates any surviving written source.

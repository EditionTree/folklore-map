Rollright Stones curse

A Bronze Age stone circle said to be a king and his knights turned to stone by a witch. The stones are impossible to count twice and reach the same number. The King Stone bleeds if cut.

The Legend

The Rollright Stones, on the border of Oxfordshire and Warwickshire, are a remarkable group of prehistoric monuments, a stone circle called the King's Men, a single tall stone called the King Stone, and a cluster called the Whispering Knights, and they carry one of the richest bodies of folklore attached to any megalithic site in England. The legend explains them all: they were once a would-be conqueror and his army, turned to stone by a witch.

The King in Stone

In the tale, an ambitious king marched to conquer all England and met a witch (in some versions Mother Shipton) on the high ground at Rollright. She challenged him: 'Seven long strides shalt thou take, and if Long Compton thou canst see, King of England thou shalt be.' He strode forward confidently, but a long mound of earth rose up to block his view of the village of Long Compton just as he took his seventh stride, and the witch cried out her triumph, turning him to the King Stone, his knights plotting treachery apart into the Whispering Knights, and his soldiers into the ring of the King's Men. The witch herself became an elder tree standing by.

A wealth of further belief clusters about the stones: that they cannot be counted twice to the same number (a baker who tried, laying a loaf on each, found a loaf always missing); that the King Stone will bleed if cut; that at midnight, or on certain days, the King's Men come alive and join hands to dance, or go down to a spring to drink; and that no one can carry a Rollright stone away without disaster following until it is returned. Between the petrified king, the uncountable stones and the dancing at midnight, the Rollright Stones are among the most legend-haunted monuments in Britain.

Historical Context

Origins & Dating

Historical PeriodMonument: Late Neolithic (King's Men stone circle, c.2500 BC), with the wider complex spanning Early Neolithic to Bronze Age (Whispering Knights dolmen c.3800-3500 BC; King Stone, Early-to-Middle Bronze Age, c.1500 BC). Legend: first attested in print in 1586, though likely older as local oral tradition.
Earliest Written RecordThe petrification belief is first recorded in William Camden's Britannia (Latin, 1586), which notes local people called them 'Rolle-rick stones' and believed they were once men transformed into stone. The fullest version of the King-and-witch narrative was collected from local informants by Arthur Evans and published in the journal Folklore in 1895.

Folklore dates are often approximate, especially where a story predates any surviving written source.

Rose Cullender and Amy Duny, the Lowestoft Witches

A refused herring sale and children vomiting pins hanged two Lowestoft widows in 1662; Sir Thomas Browne's testimony helped convict them, and Salem's judges later cited the trial as precedent.

The Legend

In March 1662 the Suffolk fishing town of Lowestoft accused two elderly widows, Amy Duny and Rose Cullender, of witchcraft after the merchant Samuel Pacy refused to sell them herring and his daughters Elizabeth and Deborah began suffering violent fits, vomiting pins and complaining of biting and pinching by invisible hands. Neighbours added further charges: that a toad had fallen hissing from a child's blanket and vanished into the fire, that a cart had jammed at Duny's gate, and that livestock, chimneys and other household mishaps across the town could be traced back to the two women.

The Story

The case went to trial at the Bury St Edmunds assizes on 10 March 1662 before Sir Matthew Hale, later Lord Chief Baron of the Exchequer, who told the jury they need only decide whether the children were genuinely bewitched and whether the two women were responsible. Crucially, the physician and author Sir Thomas Browne, then living in Norwich, was called as an expert witness and testified that the children's fits were consistent with witchcraft, drawing a comparison to reported cases in Denmark. The jury convicted Duny and Cullender within half an hour; they were hanged at Bury St Edmunds on 17 March 1662.

The trial's afterlife proved longer than the women's lives. An anonymous account, A Tryal of Witches (1682), circulated in New England and was read closely by Cotton Mather, who cited it in The Wonders of the Invisible World (1692) as a case 'much considered by the judges of New England,' while Increase Mather referenced it that same autumn. Because Sir Matthew Hale was among the most respected jurists in England, his willingness to accept spectral evidence at Lowestoft gave the Salem magistrates a precedent to lean on three decades later, tying a Suffolk fishing town directly into the most notorious witch trials in American history.

Rudston Monolith

The tallest standing stone in Britain, rising from a churchyard like a leftover sentence from an older religion. Its original purpose is unknown, which leaves folklore plenty of room to breathe.

A visual interpretation of the Rudston Monolith
The Legend

The Rudston Monolith, in the churchyard of All Saints' Church at Rudston in the East Riding of Yorkshire, is the tallest standing stone in Britain — a vast gritstone pillar nearly eight metres high, raised in the Neolithic or Bronze Age and brought from some miles away. Its sheer scale, and the strange fact that a medieval Christian church came to be built right beside this towering pagan stone, have made it a focus of folklore and speculation.

Britain's tallest standing stone rises in the churchyard like a remnant of an older sacred order.

The Tallest Standing Stone

The most common legend, of the familiar 'Devil threw it' type, holds that the Devil, enraged at the building of the church, hurled the great stone from a distance as a missile to destroy it — but missed, the monolith burying itself in the churchyard where it stands. The very name of the village, Rudston, is thought to derive from 'rood-stone', the stone of the cross or crucifix, hinting at how the early Church may have tried to Christianise or claim the pagan pillar by placing its church beside it and reconsecrating the spot.

The siting is itself the heart of the story: rather than destroy the monolith, the Christian builders raised their church in its shadow, an act often read as the new faith deliberately planting itself upon a place already sacred for thousands of years. With its unknown original purpose, its towering presence over the graves, and its Devil-hurled legend, the Rudston Monolith is a vivid emblem of the long overlay of pagan and Christian belief on a single hallowed spot.

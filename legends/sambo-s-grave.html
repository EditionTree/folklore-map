Sacred Sites Sunderland Point, Lancashire

Sambo's Grave

In 1736, an African boy travelling as an enslaved servant to a ship's captain died at Sunderland Point after being left ashore, and was buried in unconsecrated ground on the foreshore because he had not been baptised. Sixty years later, a retired schoolmaster who heard the story from a local innkeeper raised money from summer visitors to mark the grave with a stone and an epitaph, and it has been tended and visited ever since. The name recorded on that memorial, and used locally and in heritage sources since, is a term imposed on him by others and is now recognised as a racial slur, not a name of his own choosing.

The Legend

Sunderland Point, at the mouth of the River Lune near Lancaster, was a working port through the first half of the eighteenth century, handling trade with the West Indies and North America at a time when Lancaster itself was Britain's fourth largest slaving port. The earliest surviving printed account, from The Lonsdale Magazine in 1822, describes a boy left ashore at the Ship Inn by a departing ship's captain who fell into despair believing himself abandoned, refused food, and died within days. He was buried without ceremony in a field outside the churchyard, in unconsecrated ground, because he had not been baptised into the Christian faith, the same reason no contemporary record of his burial, real name, or even his existence as a documented individual has ever been found. Researchers who have examined the case in detail are clear that the story survives as a well established local tradition rather than a verified historical record.

The Story

In 1796, some sixty years after the events the story describes, the retired schoolmaster and former Lancaster Grammar School headmaster James Watson heard the tale from George Jackson, landlord of the Ship Inn, and was moved to write a memorial poem. He collected contributions from visitors to pay for a simple stone and a brass plaque, inscribed with an epitaph ending in the words that the dead should be judged not on their colour but on the worth of their heart, a sentiment later noted for its resemblance to language used more than a century afterward in the American civil rights movement. That plaque, since replaced by a replica after the original was damaged, still stands, and the grave remains a place local children visit to leave flowers and painted stones, and which has since drawn poets, historians and broadcasters examining Britain's connection to the transatlantic slave trade.

The name recorded on Watson's plaque and used in almost every account since was not the boy's own. It is a term that was commonly applied to enslaved and colonised people of African descent in this period, used here by his captors and later commemorators rather than chosen by him, and it is now widely recognised as a racial slur. Historic England and other heritage bodies record the site under this name because it is the term fixed in the historical record and on the memorial itself, not because it is considered acceptable in ordinary use, and the site's real significance lies in the burial of an unnamed and unbaptised child, forcibly displaced by the slave trade, and in the long local tradition of care and commemoration that followed rather than in the term itself.

Origins & Dating

Historical PeriodThe burial is traditionally dated to 1736, a figure derived only from an 1822 magazine account working back from the memorial epitaph's claim of 'sixty years' since the death. No contemporary record confirms the year, or the boy's existence as a documented individual. The memorial itself is firmly dated to 1796.

Folklore dates are often approximate, especially where a story predates any surviving written source.

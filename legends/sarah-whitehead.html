Sarah Whitehead

Sarah Whitehead is the reported name of a woman whose ghost is said to haunt the Bank of England; her ghost became known as The Black Nun.

The Legend

Sarah Whitehead, remembered as the 'Black Nun of Threadneedle Street', is the ghost said to haunt the precincts of the Bank of England in the City of London, not a nun at all, but a grief-maddened sister whose mourning dress gave her the name. Her tragedy began in 1811, when her beloved brother Philip Whitehead, a clerk who had worked at the Bank, was arrested and charged with forgery; in 1812 he was tried, convicted and hanged, forgery being a capital crime in that harsh age.

The Haunting at the Heart of the Tale

The shock undid her. According to the legend, Sarah was not told of her brother's execution, and when she came to the Bank asking for him, an unthinking or careless clerk blurted out the truth of his shameful death. The blow shattered her reason. For some twenty-five years afterward, dressed always in deep black mourning with a heavy veil, Sarah returned again and again to the Bank and the surrounding streets, approaching clerks and passers-by to ask, over and over, whether they had seen her brother Philip, a familiar, pitiable figure in the City. In 1818 the Bank's directors began paying her a small annuity in the hope of persuading her to stay away, though she kept returning regardless.

After her death Sarah was reputedly buried in the old churchyard that was absorbed into the Bank's garden courtyard, and it is there and along Threadneedle Street that her veiled black figure has been reported ever since, still seeking the brother she would not believe was dead. The 'Black Nun' has become one of the most enduring ghost stories of the City of London, a haunting of madness and grief at the very heart of the nation's financial centre.

Historical Context

Origins & Dating

Earliest Written RecordThe ghost legend first appears in print in 1837, in the pamphlet 'Memoirs, Extraordinary Life, and Singular Adventures of that Eccentric Character, Miss Whitehead, the Bank Nun' (J. Thompson, 1837) and the sketch collection 'Streetology of London, or the Metropolitan Papers of the Itinerant Club' (1837); the story was well established as a Bank of England urban legend by the 1880s.
Historical PeriodHistorical figure active early 19th century; the ghost legend crystallised in Victorian print culture from 1837 onward

Sarey Bowers of Kirtlington

Local huntsmen blamed Sarey Bowers of Foxtownsend for bewitching foxes to escape and hounds to lose the scent — one tale has an uncatchable vixen vanish into her cottage, leaving only Sarey panting by the fire.

The Legend

Sarey Bowers was the reputed witch of Kirtlington in Oxfordshire, remembered in local folklore from a cottage at Foxtownsend on the Heyford Road, and her legend belongs to the widespread and ancient British belief that a witch could change her shape into that of a hare — or, in her case, a fox. Her story is bound up with the local hunt, for Sarey was said to have a particular sympathy with the foxes the huntsmen pursued, and to use her powers to thwart them.

The hunt could lose the scent, but local memory always found its way back to Sarey Bowers.

The Fox by Foxtownsend

In the best-remembered tale, the local hunt was repeatedly frustrated by a remarkably fine vixen that always outran the hounds and could never be caught, however close they came. On one occasion the huntsmen pressed the fox so hard that they saw it dart, exhausted, straight into the cottage of Sarey Bowers, the known witch. Bursting in after it, they found no fox anywhere in the house — only the old woman herself, sitting innocently by her fire, but suspiciously out of breath, as though she had been running for her life. The implication was plain: the vixen and the witch were one and the same.

Sarey was further blamed for laying her spells on the hunt itself — bewitching the foxes to give them speed and cunning to escape, and confounding the hounds so that they lost the scent and could not follow the trail. Her legend is a classic local example of the shape-shifting witch-hare (or witch-fox) tradition found across England, in which an old woman's affinity with a hunted animal, and her breathlessness after its narrow escape, betray her as a witch able to take its form. The tale survives only in twentieth-century folklore compilations (Christine Bloxham's Folklore of Oxfordshire and Jennifer Westwood and Jacqueline Simpson's The Lore of the Land), with no earlier or contemporary record behind it, reading more as local hunting-country memory than a documented case.

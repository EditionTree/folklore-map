Selkies of Orkney

Seal-folk who shed their skins to walk as humans on land. If a fisherman steals a selkie's skin, she cannot return to the sea and must live as his wife, yearning always for the ocean.

The Legend

By day the selkies (seal-folk) swim the cold seas of Orkney and the wider Northern Isles in the shape of seals, but they can shed their sleek skins to step ashore as men and women of unearthly beauty, dancing on the rocks and strands in the moonlight. Few figures in Northern Isles folklore carry the same ache: to return to the sea they need only draw their skin back on, but their whole tragedy lies in that skin, for whoever holds it holds the selkie.

If a fisherman steals a selkie's skin, she cannot return to the sea and must live as his wife, yearning always for the ocean.

The Skin Beside the Sea

The classic tale tells of a fisherman or crofter who comes upon selkie women dancing on the shore, their skins laid aside, and steals one so that its owner cannot flee back beneath the waves. Trapped in human form, she becomes his wife and bears his children, and may live so for many years, a good wife to all appearances, yet forever drawn to the sea, gazing out across the water with a longing she cannot name. The story almost always ends the same way: one day a child finds the hidden skin in the thatch or a locked chest, and innocently shows it to the mother. The moment it is in her hands she is gone, back into the sea, leaving husband and children behind, though she is sometimes seen afterwards swimming offshore to watch over the little ones she could not stay for.

Male selkies appear too, coming ashore as handsome strangers to seek out lonely or discontented women of the land, and were sometimes blamed for unexplained pregnancies. The selkie legends carry a deep, melancholy theme of divided nature and impossible belonging, of a creature caught between two worlds and never wholly at home in either, and they remain one of the most beloved strands of Orcadian and Hebridean folklore.

Origins & Dating

Historical PeriodOrkney-specific version first collected in print by Walter Traill Dennison (1825-1894), 'Orkney Folk-Lore', The Scottish Antiquary, or Northern Notes and Queries, vol. 7 (1893), pp. 171-177, including the tale of 'The Goodman o' Wastness'
Earliest Written RecordSamuel Hibbert, A Description of the Shetland Islands, Comprising an Account of Their Scenery, Antiquities, and Superstitions (Edinburgh: Archibald Constable and Co., 1822) -- contains an early literary account of the stolen seal-skin/forced-wife tale, localized to Unst.

Seonaidh

SHAW-nee

Seonaidh, also called Shony or Shoney, is a water spirit associated with Lewis. Martin Martin recorded an island ritual in which ale was poured into the sea near St Mulvay's Church as an offering for an abundant crop of seaweed to fertilise the fields.

The Legend

Seonaidh, anglicised as Shony or Shoney, was a sea-spirit or sea-god of the Isle of Lewis in the Outer Hebrides, propitiated by the islanders to bring a good harvest from the shore. Our knowledge of him rests above all on the account of the traveller Martin Martin, a Lewisman himself, who recorded the ritual offering made to Seonaidh in his own lifetime, before the Reformed Church suppressed it. Martin witnessed the custom during his tour of Lewis in 1699, part of a wider survey of the Western Isles he undertook between 1695 and 1699 under the patronage of the naturalist Hans Sloane, and he published his account in A Description of the Western Islands of Scotland in 1703.

The Spirit in the Sea

By Martin's account the people of the parish gathered at Hallowtide at the old church of St Mulvay, Eoropie, in the far north of Lewis. Each family brought malt, which was brewed into ale, and one man was sent to wade out into the sea up to his waist carrying a cup of it. There he poured the ale into the waves and called aloud to Seonaidh, asking the spirit to send a plentiful crop of seaware, the seaweed cast up by winter storms that the islanders spread on their thin fields as fertiliser. The company then returned to the church, where after a moment's silence by a candle on the altar they put out the light and spent the night drinking, dancing and singing in the fields.

The rite is a remarkable survival of pre-Christian sea-worship clinging on within the forms of the old church calendar, and it scandalised the ministers, who stamped it out not long after Martin recorded it. Seonaidh endures as one of the clearest documented examples of a Hebridean offering to the sea itself, a bargain struck with the ocean for the gift of the seaweed on which the islanders' crops, and so their survival, depended.

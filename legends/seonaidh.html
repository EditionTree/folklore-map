Seonaidh

SHAW-nee

Seonaidh, also called Shony or Shoney, is a water spirit associated with Lewis. Martin Martin recorded an island ritual in which ale was poured into the sea near St Mulvay's Church as an offering for an abundant crop of seaweed to fertilise the fields.

The Legend

Seonaidh — anglicised as Shony or Shoney — was a sea-spirit or sea-god of the Isle of Lewis in the Outer Hebrides, propitiated by the islanders to bring a good harvest from the shore. Our knowledge of him rests above all on the account of the traveller Martin Martin, a Lewisman himself, who in his late-17th-century Description of the Western Islands of Scotland recorded the ritual offering made to Seonaidh in his own lifetime, before the Reformed Church suppressed it.

The Spirit in the Sea

By Martin's account the people of the parish gathered at Hallowtide at the old church of St Mulvay (Eoropie, in the far north of Lewis). Each family brought malt, which was brewed into ale, and one man was sent to wade out into the sea up to his waist carrying a cup of it. There he poured the ale into the waves and called aloud to Seonaidh, asking the spirit to send a plentiful crop of seaware — the seaweed cast up by winter storms, which the islanders spread on their thin fields as fertiliser. The company then returned to the church, where after a moment's silence by a candle on the altar they put out the light and spent the night drinking, dancing and singing in the fields.

The rite is a remarkable survival of pre-Christian sea-worship clinging on within the forms of the old church calendar, and it scandalised the ministers, who stamped it out. Seonaidh endures as one of the clearest recorded examples of a Hebridean offering to the sea itself — a bargain with the ocean for the gift of the seaweed on which the islanders' crops, and so their survival, depended.

Legendary Figures Cappagh and Altmore, County Tyrone, Northern Ireland

Shane Bernagh

A dispossessed Tyrone landowner turned mountain outlaw, robbing coaches on the Dublin road and sharing his takings with the poor like an Ulster Robin Hood, from a hideout still called his Chair.

The Legend

Shane Bernagh Donnelly, according to local tradition, was dispossessed of his family's land at Ballydonnelly, now Castlecaulfield, and retreated into the Altmore mountains above Cappagh, County Tyrone, where he lived in a cave and turned highwayman, holding up coaches on the main Dublin-to-Derry road. He is remembered as targeting the Protestant Ascendancy in particular and sharing his takings with poorer neighbours, earning him a reputation, still current in the area, as a local Robin Hood.

The Story

The authorities took him seriously enough to build Altmore Barracks in 1700 specifically to counter his activities, garrisoning soldiers within sight of the cave still known locally as Shane Bearnach's Sentry Box. Nearby Bernish Glen is said to mark a horseback leap he made to escape pursuing soldiers, and a rock outcrop on the edge of Cappagh and Altmore, shaped like a chair, is still known as Shane Bernagh's Chair. He was eventually captured and executed by the Dublin Castle administration, his body reportedly cast into a lough at the summit of Slieve Beagh, the mountain where the counties of Tyrone, Fermanagh and Monaghan meet.

A related but distinct tradition survives on the Monaghan side of the same mountain: fieldwork gathered for the Irish Folklore Commission's Schools' Collection in the Tydavnet area recorded a "Shane Bearna," active there "about 1840," who drove stolen cattle across Slieve Beagh to hidden stables reached by swimming the animals across a lake, and who reputedly had his horses shod backwards to confuse pursuers. Whether this is the same figure remembered with a different, later date, or a separate cattle-raider whose story merged with the earlier rapparee's, the Schools' Collection account does not resolve, and it is presented here as a second, place-anchored strand of the same regional legend. Bernagh's memory was later carried into print by George Sigerson's ballad "The Mountains of Pomeroy" and John Montague's poem "A Lost Tradition."

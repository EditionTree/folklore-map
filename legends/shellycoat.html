Shellycoat

A Scottish bogle draped in shells that rattle with every movement. It haunts streams and rivers, delighting in leading travellers astray with mischievous cries.

The Legend

The Shellycoat is a bogle of Lowland Scottish and Border folklore, a mischievous water-spirit that haunts streams, rivers and pools, and takes its name from the coat of shells it is said to wear, a covering of shells and seaweed that rattles and clatters with its every movement, announcing its approach. Unlike the deadly water-horses and drowning-hags of darker tradition, the Shellycoat is generally more of a prankster than a true menace, given to bewildering and harassing travellers rather than killing them.

The Otherworld at the Heart of the Tale

Its favourite sport was to lead wayfarers astray, especially at night and near water. A famous tale tells how the Shellycoat would imitate the cries of a drowning person, calling out in distress from the river, luring kindly travellers to stumble up and down the banks in the dark searching for the victim, while the spirit's mocking laughter rang out as it watched them flounder. Two men so deceived are said to have chased the cries half the night, only to hear the Shellycoat hooting with glee at their gullibility as dawn broke.

The rattling shell-coat marks it as a creature of the water-margin, kin to the brags, bogles and other shape-shifting tricksters of the Scottish Borders. Its name first appears in print in 1720, in Allan Ramsay's line about a 'shelly-coated kow', and it is Sir Walter Scott's Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border, eighty years later, that gives the fullest early account, placing its hauntings at Gorinberry on the Hermitage Water and near Leith and remaining the basis for most modern descriptions. It stands as a characteristic figure of the older Lowland imagination, the uncanny, dripping thing in the stream that meant a traveller no fatal harm, but could not resist leading him a merry and miserable dance.

Origins & Dating

Approximate Origincirca 1720
Historical Periodearly 18th century (first literary mention); early 19th century (fullest narrative account)
Earliest Written RecordAllan Ramsay, Poems (1720), contains the line 'shelly-coated kow,' the earliest known literary attestation of the name. An explanatory note was added in the 1733 edition of Ramsay's Poems, describing shellycoat as 'one of those frightful spectres the ignorant people are terrified at... clothed with a coat of shells, which make a horrid rattling.' This wording was later reproduced in Brand's Observations on Popular Antiquities (1813), where it is misprinted as 'Spelly coat.' Sir Walter Scott's Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border (1802-03) is the earliest detailed narrative source and remains the basis for most modern descriptions (haunts at Gorinberry on the Hermitage Water, and near Leith).

Folklore dates are often approximate, especially where a story predates any surviving written source.

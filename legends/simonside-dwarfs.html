Simonside Dwarfs

Dwarf-like beings said to lure night travellers across the Simonside Hills with false lights. Follow them too far and the moor becomes a trap with no ordinary road home.

The Legend

The Simonside Dwarfs — known locally as the Duergar — are malevolent dwarf-like fairies of Northumberland, said to haunt the Simonside Hills near Rothbury, a range of brooding crags and moorland above Coquetdale. Small, ugly and ill-disposed toward humankind, they were greatly feared by travellers crossing the hills after dark, for their chief delight was to lure the unwary to their deaths among the bogs and crags by means of false lights.

The Otherworld at the Heart of the Tale

Like the will-o'-the-wisp, the dwarfs would show flickering lights across the moor at night, which a lost or benighted traveller might take for the candle of a cottage or the lantern of a fellow wayfarer and follow in hope of safety. But the lights led only deeper into the wilderness — toward the edge of a precipice, into a treacherous mire, or round and round until the victim was hopelessly lost and exhausted in the dark, far from any road home. The dwarfs were said to carry clubs or to delight in the traveller's terror.

A classic tale, recorded in the 19th century, tells of a wanderer who sheltered by a fire on the hills only to find a malignant duergar sharing it with him; warned never to accept the creature's hospitality or follow its fire, he kept still until daybreak, when the dwarf and its fire vanished and he found himself perched at the very brink of a deadly drop. The Simonside Dwarfs remain among the most sharply localised and genuinely sinister of England's fairy beings, bound to one stretch of haunted Northumbrian upland.

Historical Context

Origins & Dating

Earliest Written RecordThe local newspaper the Morpeth Gazette carried a mention of the Simonside dwarfs in 1889 -- the earliest documented written reference found. A fuller literary treatment appears in Tyndale's Legends and Folklore of Northumbria (1930), and a narrative retelling titled 'The Duergar' appears in Frederick Grice's Folk Tales of the North Country (Thomas Nelson & Sons, London & Edinburgh, 1944), pp. 130-133.
Historical Periodlate 19th century (earliest documented written mention, 1889); legend itself believed considerably older as part of Northumbrian oral tradition

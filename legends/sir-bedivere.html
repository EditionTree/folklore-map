Sir Bedivere

Last knight at Camlann, charged by the dying Arthur with casting Excalibur into the lake. He faltered twice; on the third attempt the sword was thrown and a hand rose to receive it. Then he carried the king to the barge and outlived every knight of the Round Table.

The Legend

In the oldest Welsh sources Bedivere appears not as a servant but as one of Arthur's fiercest warriors, known as Bedwyr Bedrydant, "Bedwyr of the Perfect Sinew," a name that praised his strength despite having only one hand. The early poem Pa Gur yv y Porthaur? has him and Cei killing by the hundred on the shores of Tryfrwyd, fighting a warrior called Garwlwyd. In the tale of Culhwch and Olwen he stands second only to Cei at the head of Arthur's warband, wielding a magic spear whose head could detach from the shaft to strike nine wounds at once, and he helps Culhwch slay the giant Wrnach, rescue the imprisoned Mabon ap Modron, and hunt the monstrous boar Twrch Trwyth. The Stanzas of the Graves, preserved in the Black Book of Carmarthen, name his burial place as Tryfan, treating him as a hero worth remembering by name.

on the third attempt the sword was thrown and a hand rose to receive it

The Final Cast of Excalibur

Centuries later, Malory's Morte d'Arthur gave Bedivere the role he is best remembered for today: witness of Arthur's ending, the one knight who was there when it was over and who had to bear both the doing of it and the telling. After the final battle at Camlann, where Arthur is mortally wounded, the king commands Bedivere to take the sword Excalibur to the nearest water and throw it in, then report what he sees.

Twice Bedivere cannot bring himself to cast the sword away. It is too precious, too storied, too much the king himself. Twice he hides it and returns to say he saw only wind and water. Twice Arthur knows he is lying. On the third try Bedivere hurls the sword as far as he can, and a hand and arm rise from the water, catch it, brandish it three times, and pull it under. He carries Arthur to the shore, where three queens receive him onto a barge bound for Avalon.

Bedivere then does something remarkable: he lives on. The other knights are dead, dispersed, or vanished into hermitage. Bedivere goes to a hermitage himself, but he is there when Lancelot arrives years later, and he tells the story. Between the one-handed warrior of Tryfrwyd and the grieving witness at Avalon lies centuries of retelling, but in both versions Bedivere is the one who was there, and the one who stayed to say so.

Historical Context

Origins & Dating

Historical Period9th-10th century (earliest Welsh poetic attestation, Pa Gur and Englynion y Beddau); c.1136 (earliest Latin chronicle attestation, Geoffrey of Monmouth)
Earliest Written RecordBedwyr (Bedivere) appears in Pa Gur (Pa Wr yw'r Porthor?, 'What Man is the Gatekeeper?'), an Old Welsh poem preserved in the Black Book of Carmarthen (manuscript c.1250) but believed composed in the 9th or 10th century, possibly as early as the 8th; he is called 'Bedwyr Perfect-Sinew' and described fighting on the shores of Tryfrwyd. He also appears in Englynion y Beddau (Stanzas of the Graves), likewise preserved in the Black Book of Carmarthen and thought composed in the 9th-10th century. He is a major character in the prose tale Culhwch and Olwen (linguistically dated to perhaps as early as the 11th century, surviving in the White Book of Rhydderch c.1325 and Red Book of Hergest c.1400). His first appearance in Latin chronicle literature, as Beduerus, Arthur's royal butler, is in Geoffrey of Monmouth's Historia Regum Britanniae, c.1136.

Folklore dates are often approximate, especially where a story predates any surviving written source.

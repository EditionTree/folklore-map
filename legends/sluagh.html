Sluagh

SLOO-agh

The Sluagh are airborne hosts of the unforgiven dead in Scottish Gaelic folklore. Hebridean tradition warns that they sweep in from the west seeking souls, so west-facing doors and windows were kept shut when death was near.

The Legend

The Sluagh, the 'host', are the most dreaded of the spirits in Scottish and Irish Gaelic belief: the airborne multitude of the restless, unforgiven dead. Neither the blessed dead nor quite the fairy folk, they were imagined as a dark flock sweeping through the night sky, often from the west, with a sound like a great rushing of wings, gathering up souls and bent on mischief and harm.

The Haunting at the Heart of the Tale

The earliest written trace of the belief comes from the minister and folklorist Robert Kirk, whose 1691 manuscript The Secret Commonwealth of Elves, Fauns and Fairies distinguishes the 'Sluagh Saoghalta', the earthly or secular host, from the 'Sluagh Sith', the fairy host proper, and records the Highland belief that the souls of the dead join this fairy host when they die. Two centuries later, fieldworkers collecting directly from Hebridean informants, Alexander Carmichael in his Carmina Gadelica and John Gregorson Campbell in his Superstitions of the Highlands and Islands of Scotland, both independently recorded the specific custom of keeping a house's west-facing doors and windows shut when death was near.

Hebridean tradition held that the Sluagh flew in from the west, the direction of the dead, and so people were careful to keep those windows shut lest the host swoop in to seize the departing soul. The Sluagh were said to snatch up unwary travellers caught abroad after dark and carry them for miles through the air, forcing them to take part in their malice, even compelling them to fire elf-shot at cattle and people below, before dropping them, dazed and far from home. They are the night-flying, soul-stealing host at the darkest edge of the Gaelic otherworld.

Origins & Dating

Historical PeriodEarliest written record: late 17th century (Kirk, c.1691). Fullest documentation of the Hebridean tradition (including the west-door custom): late 19th-century fieldwork, published 1900 (Carmichael, Campbell)
Earliest Written RecordThe earliest known written use of 'sluagh' in a Highland fairy/spirit-belief context is Rev. Robert Kirk's manuscript treatise The Secret Commonwealth of Elves, Fauns and Fairies, written c.1691 (not published until 1815, edited by Walter Scott -- Kirk himself already researched in a prior run of this pipeline), which distinguishes 'Sluagh Saoghalta' (the earthly/secular host) from 'Sluagh Sith' (the fairy host), noting Highlanders' belief that souls join the Sluagh Sith when they die. The specific Hebridean custom described in the entry -- closing west-facing doors and windows to keep the Sluagh out when someone is dying -- is documented in Alexander Carmichael's Carmina Gadelica (Volume II, first published 1900; based on oral fieldwork conducted primarily across the Highlands and Islands from the 1860s to 1890s), and independently in John Gregorson Campbell's Superstitions of the Highlands and Islands of Scotland (published posthumously in 1900).

Folklore dates are often approximate, especially where a story predates any surviving written source.

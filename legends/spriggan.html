Spriggan

Ugly, fierce Cornish fae said to guard buried treasure, ruins, and ancient places. Though small at first glance, spriggans can swell into giant forms when angered.

The Legend

Spriggans are among the more dangerous and uncanny of the Cornish fairies, the grim guardians of the old places: standing stones, barrows, cairns, ruined castles and buried hoards. In their ordinary aspect they are small, withered and ugly, but their defining power is that they can swell. At need, or in anger, a spriggan can grow from a dwarfish figure into a towering giant, which folk tradition explains by naming them the shrunken ghosts of Cornwall's old giants, still able to recover something of their former bulk.

The Otherworld at the Heart of the Tale

The word itself is older than the great Victorian folklore collections that made spriggans famous. The Oxford English Dictionary traces 'spriggan' back to 1754, in the writing of the Cornish antiquary William Borlase, more than a century before Robert Hunt's Popular Romances of the West of England (1865) and William Bottrell's Traditions and Hearthside Stories of West Cornwall (1870-1880) set down the fullest tales of them.

Their chief duty is to guard treasure, and many stories warn of the spriggans' vengeance on those who dig for buried gold in the cromlechs and hill-forts they protect: sudden storms, a spriggan swelling to monstrous size, or misfortune following the greedy home. They were also blamed for a wide range of country mischief, stealing away human babies and leaving their own ugly changelings in the cradle, blighting crops, whipping up whirlwinds, and leading travellers astray on the moors. One well-known tale tells of an old woman who outwitted a band of spriggans and made off with some of their fairy gold, only for it to bring trouble in its train.

Closely tied to the bleak, stone-strewn landscape of west Cornwall, particularly the cairns and barrows of West Penwith, the spriggans embody the unease of the ancient monuments, the sense that something watches over the barrows and standing stones and does not welcome disturbance. They remain one of the most distinctive figures of Cornish folklore, fierce little wardens of the buried past.

St Botolph and the Devil

The Devil, cornered by St Botolph at his new seventh-century chapel, could only huff and puff in fury, and the whirlwind around "Boston Stump" is said never to have entirely subsided since.

The Legend

According to an account first printed in Roger Quaint's 1841 Traditions of Lincolnshire, St Botolph, who tradition holds founded a chapel at Boston in 654, though historians now consider Iken in Suffolk the more likely site of his actual monastery, grew briefly proud of his achievement, a weakness the Devil moved to exploit. Botolph recognised the temptation for what it was and confronted Satan directly; the ensuing struggle was so one-sided that "the poor devil puffed and blowed so violently as to raise a whirlwind, which has never since entirely subsided."

The Story

The legend survives today as a piece of living folk-explanation: the exposed footpath beside St Botolph's parish church tower, universally known as "Boston Stump," is one of the windiest spots in the churchyard, and locals still attribute the gusts to the Devil's furious, unending exhalation. The Paranormal Database independently records the same tradition under its Lincolnshire devil-lore entries, describing the winds as "the Devil's breath." Quaint himself was already fending off rival explanations in 1841, noting drily that "some vain-glorious philosophers have pretended to explain this phenomenon on what they call scientific principles," a line that shows the supernatural account was competing with weather science even in his own day.

The tale sits alongside a small cluster of Lincolnshire saint-versus-devil stories, including the Lincoln Imp and the curse laid on Crowland Abbey, and follows the same pattern as other English tales in which the Devil is thwarted by a local holy man, such as the story told of Dunstan.

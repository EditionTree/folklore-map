St Trinian's Church

A roofless chapel at the foot of Greeba Mountain where a monstrous Buggane tore off every roof ever built — until a young tailor sewed a pair of breeches inside the church at midnight and outran the creature to consecrated ground.

The Legend

St Trinian’s is the roofless ruin of a medieval chapel at the foot of Greeba Mountain in the parish of Marown, Isle of Man, dedicated to the fourth-century Pictish saint Ninian. Known in Manx as a keeil brisht — a broken church — it owes its permanent rootlessness to one of the island’s most celebrated folk tales. A Buggane, a huge shape-shifting ogre that lived inside Greeba Mountain, had sworn that the church would never be completed. Every time builders finished the roof, the creature tore it off overnight, flinging timbers across the countryside and bellowing with fury.

The Story

After several failed attempts at roofing, a young tailor named Timothy Clucas wagered he could break the curse. He sat alone inside the church at nightfall with needle and thread, determined to complete a pair of breeches before the Buggane came. As the last stitches went in, the ground shook and the creature burst through the floor — vast, tusked, and furious. Timothy snatched up the breeches and sprinted for Marown Church, whose consecrated ground the Buggane could not enter. The ogre, maddened at losing its prey, tore off its own head and hurled it over the churchyard wall, where it burst at the tailor’s feet in a shower of sparks. The Buggane was never seen again, but the church at St Trinian’s was never re-roofed either.

The tale appears in Sophia Morrison’s Manx Fairy Tales (1911), the foundational collection of Manx folklore, and is retold by Culture Vannin, the Isle of Man’s official cultural body. The historical reason for the church’s unfinished state is probably more prosaic — construction likely halted around 1343 when William Montagu conquered the island and expelled the Scottish monks of Whithorn Priory who had been building it — but the Buggane legend has long since displaced the political explanation in popular memory. The ruin stands beside the A1 Douglas-to-Peel road and remains one of the most visited folklore sites on the island.

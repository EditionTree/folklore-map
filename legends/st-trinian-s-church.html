St Trinian's Church

A roofless chapel at the foot of Greeba Mountain where a monstrous Buggane tore off every roof ever built, until a young tailor outsewed and outran him to consecrated ground.

The Legend

St Trinian's is the roofless ruin of a medieval chapel at the foot of Greeba Mountain in the parish of Marown, Isle of Man, dedicated to the fourth-century Pictish saint Ninian. Known in Manx as a keeil brisht, a broken church, it owes its permanent roofless state to one of the island's most celebrated folk tales. A Buggane, a huge shape-shifting ogre that lived inside Greeba Mountain, could not bear the thought of church bells ringing over his territory, reasoning, in Sophia Morrison's telling, that he would have no peace night or day with their jingling if he let the building stand finished. He swore the church would never be completed, and every time builders finished the roof, he tore it off overnight, flinging timbers across the countryside and bellowing with fury.

Every roof was said to be torn away until the chapel's midnight test was won.

The Church Without a Roof

After several failed attempts at roofing, a young tailor named Timothy Clucas wagered he could break the curse. He sat alone inside the church at nightfall with needle and thread, determined to complete a pair of breeches before the Buggane came. As the last stitches went in, the ground shook and the creature burst through the floor, vast, tusked, and furious. Timothy snatched up the breeches and sprinted for Marown Church, whose consecrated ground the Buggane could not enter. The ogre, maddened at losing its prey, tore off its own head and hurled it over the churchyard wall, where it burst at the tailor's feet in a shower of sparks. The Buggane was never seen again, but the church at St Trinian's was never re-roofed either.

The tale appears in Sophia Morrison's Manx Fairy Tales (1911), the foundational collection of Manx folklore, and is retold by Culture Vannin, the Isle of Man's official cultural body. The historical reason for the church's unfinished state is probably more prosaic, construction likely halted around 1343 when William Montagu conquered the island and expelled the Scottish monks of Whithorn Priory who had been building it, but the Buggane legend has long since displaced the political explanation in popular memory. The ruin stands beside the A1 Douglas-to-Peel road and remains one of the most visited folklore sites on the island.

Beyond this one tale, the Buggane is a recurring figure across Manx folklore generally, a huge, hairy, shape-shifting ogre-spirit of enormous strength and foul temper, blamed island-wide for mischief, ruin and ill luck. St Trinian's is simply its most famous haunt, and the byword the Island still uses for any fearsome supernatural bogey traces back to this same creature.

Historical Context

Origins & Dating

Historical PeriodChapel fabric shows Romanesque mouldings suggesting a 12th-century phase, with standing walls generally dated 13th-14th century; a 7th-century inscribed cross and lintel graves point to an earlier (6th-10th century) burial ground on the site. The buggane/tailor folk tale was first fixed in print in 1890-1891, though oral tradition likely predates this by at least a century.
Earliest Written RecordThe earliest identified written appearance of the buggane/tailor legend is a report in the Isle of Man Times, 29 October 1890, naming the tailor as Timothy Clucas. It was then included in A.W. Moore's Folk-Lore of the Isle of Man (1891) and later in Sophia Morrison's Manx Fairy Tales (1911). A separate, earlier tradition records that Rev. Philip Moore (chaplain of Douglas, born 1705) once staged a prank at the ruins in the 18th century specifically to debunk locals' belief in the buggane, implying the tale was already established by then.

Folklore dates are often approximate, especially where a story predates any surviving written source.

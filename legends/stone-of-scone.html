Stone of Scone

Stolen from Scone by Edward I in 1296 as a trophy of conquest, Scotland's coronation stone carries legends of Jacob's pillow and the screaming Irish Lia Fail, and possibly the real stone was never taken at all.

The Legend

Long before the Wars of Scottish Independence, the Stone of Scone already carried a legend reaching back to the Bible. Medieval chroniclers identified it as the very stone the patriarch Jacob used as a pillow at Bethel when he dreamed of a ladder to heaven, a relic said to have travelled by way of Egypt, Spain and Ireland into the hands of the Dalriada kings and to have become, in Ireland, the Lia Fail, the Stone of Destiny kept at the Hill of Tara, which was said to cry out when the rightful King of Ireland stood upon it. Tradition holds that the fifth-century Irish prince Fergus Mor mac Eirc carried it across to Dalriada in Scotland around 500 AD, and that Kenneth MacAlpin, first king of a united Scotland, placed it at Scone Abbey in 843 when he brought the Pictish and Gaelic kingdoms together. Scottish kings were inaugurated on it at Scone from at least the ninth century by tradition, and with documented certainty from Alexander III's inauguration there in 1249.

The stone carries stories of conquest, coronation, and whether the true stone ever left Scotland.

The Coronation Stone

Edward I seized the Stone in 1296 during his conquest of Scotland and had it built into a new throne at Westminster Abbey, a permanent, deliberate symbol that England now held the source of Scottish kings' legitimacy. It stayed there for seven hundred years, used at every English and British coronation that followed, with one notable interruption. On Christmas Day 1950, four Scottish nationalist students broke into the abbey and made off with the Stone, cracking it in the process; it turned up again at Arbroath Abbey the following April, to enormous public interest. The Stone was formally returned to Scotland in 1996 and now sits in the Crown Room of Edinburgh Castle.

The most persistent legend of all, though, is that Edward never got the real stone. What sits beneath the English throne, and now in Edinburgh, is a plain block of Old Red Sandstone almost certainly quarried near Scone itself, nothing like the ancient Middle Eastern relic the earlier legends describe. The medieval historian John of Fordun and later Scottish tradition both hold that the monks of Scone Abbey hid the true coronation stone and fobbed Edward off with a substitute. No hidden stone has ever surfaced, if one exists, but modern geological survey confirms the object in question really is local sandstone from the Scone Sandstone Formation, and Historic Environment Scotland's own catalogue dates the physical stone no earlier than the thirteenth century, a description that sits awkwardly against the ninth-century and older traditions. Scotland's old boast, that it outwitted its conqueror in the very act of surrender, has never quite let go.

Origins & Dating

Historical PeriodUsed as the Scottish coronation/inauguration stone at Scone from at least 1249 (documented) and by tradition from the 9th century, until removed by Edward I in 1296. Returned to Scotland in November 1996. Briefly stolen from Westminster Abbey by four Scottish nationalist students on Christmas Day 1950 and recovered at Arbroath Abbey by April 1951.
Earliest Written RecordThe stone's earliest confirmed documented coronation use is Alexander III's inauguration at Scone in 1249. Popular tradition holds Kenneth MacAlpin brought the stone to Scone around 841-847 AD for royal inauguration use, but this is a later attributed tradition rather than a contemporary 9th-century record. Historic Environment Scotland's own object catalogue dates the surviving physical stone itself to '13th century; Medieval.'

Folklore dates are often approximate, especially where a story predates any surviving written source.

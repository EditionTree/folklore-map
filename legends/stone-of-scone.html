Stone of Scone

Stolen from Scone by Edward I in 1296 as a trophy of conquest, Scotland's coronation stone carries legends of Jacob's pillow and the screaming Irish Lia Fáil — and possibly the real stone was never taken at all.

A visual interpretation of the Stone of Scone
The Legend

The legend of the Stone of Scone reaches back long before the Wars of Scottish Independence. One tradition, carried in medieval chronicles and Christian origin-legend, identifies it as the stone that the patriarch Jacob used as a pillow at Bethel when he dreamed of a ladder to heaven (Genesis 28). This stone was said to have passed via Egypt, Spain, and Ireland into the possession of the Dalriada kings, becoming the Lia Fáil — the Stone of Destiny kept at the Hill of Tara — which would cry out when the rightful King of Ireland stepped upon it. The 5th-century Irish prince Fergus Mór mac Eirc supposedly carried it to Dalriada in Scotland around 500 AD, and the Scottish kings used it for coronations at Scone from at least the 9th century. Kenneth MacAlpin, first King of Scotland, placed it at Scone Abbey in 843 when he consolidated the Pictish and Gaelic kingdoms.

The stone carries stories of conquest, coronation, and whether the true stone ever left Scotland.

The Coronation Stone

Edward I of England seized the Stone in 1296 during his conquest of Scotland, placing it beneath the throne in Westminster Abbey as a permanent symbol of English dominance. The gesture was deliberate: controlling the stone was controlling the legitimacy of Scottish kingship. The stone remained at Westminster for seven hundred years — used in every English and British coronation — with one notable interruption: on Christmas Day 1950, four Scottish nationalist students broke into Westminster Abbey and removed the stone, cracking it in the process. It was recovered at Arbroath Abbey in April 1951, to considerable public attention. The Stone was formally repatriated to Scotland in 1996, and resides today in the Crown Room of Edinburgh Castle.

The most persistent legend, however, is that Edward never got the real stone at all. The Stone of Scone is a plain block of Old Red Sandstone, likely quarried near Scone itself — a description wholly inconsistent with an ancient Middle Eastern relic brought from Tara. The medieval historian John of Fordun and later Scottish tradition hold that the monks of Scone Abbey hid the genuine coronation stone and presented Edward with a substitute. No one has produced the hidden stone, if it exists; but the geological evidence confirms that whatever rests beneath the English throne is local Scottish sandstone, and the legend that Scotland outsmarted its conqueror in the very act of submission has never quite gone away.

Stonehenge

Raised by giants according to Geoffrey of Monmouth, or conjured by Merlin from Ireland — Stonehenge defies rational explanation. For five thousand years it has been Britain’s supreme monument, and for nearly a thousand its legends have made it Merlin’s stage.

A visual interpretation of Stonehenge at midsummer dawn
The Legend

The folklore of Stonehenge is dominated by Geoffrey of Monmouth’s account in his twelfth-century Historia Regum Britanniae. According to Geoffrey, the stones originally formed a circle called the Giants’ Dance (Chorea Gigantum) on Mount Killaraus in Ireland, where they had been raised by giants who brought them from the farthest shores of Africa for their healing properties — water poured over the stones and collected in baths below could cure any ailment. When the fifth-century British king Aurelius Ambrosius wished to build a fitting memorial to the Celtic nobles massacred by the Saxons at Salisbury, the wizard Merlin advised him to fetch the Giants’ Dance from Ireland. An army of fifteen thousand men under Uther Pendragon sailed to Ireland but could not shift the stones by force; Merlin dismantled them by his arts, transported them across the sea, and reassembled them on Salisbury Plain in a single night.

Giants, Merlin and the Solstice

Geoffrey’s narrative was not idle invention — or at least, modern archaeology has given it an uncanny resonance. The bluestones at Stonehenge’s inner ring were quarried in the Preseli Hills of Pembrokeshire, over 150 miles away, and recent excavations at Waun Mawn have identified a dismantled stone circle whose empty sockets match the Stonehenge bluestones almost exactly. Whether the legend preserves a genuine folk memory of long-distance stone transport or whether Geoffrey simply guessed well remains one of British archaeology’s most provocative open questions.

Beyond Geoffrey, Stonehenge has accumulated layers of folklore. The Heel Stone — the outlier that marks the midsummer sunrise — is said to bear the imprint of the Devil’s heel after he threw it at a friar who had recognised him in disguise. The stones are popularly believed to be uncountable: anyone who counts them twice will get a different number each time. Defoe recorded this tradition in the 1720s, and it persists in local belief. The site is managed by English Heritage and draws over a million visitors a year, making it the most visited prehistoric monument in Europe.

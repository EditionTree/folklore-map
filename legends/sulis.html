Sulis

SOO-lis

A Celtic goddess of healing waters whose sacred spring at Bath was so powerful the Romans built a great temple around it, naming her Sulis Minerva. Thousands of curse tablets were cast into her waters: prayers, pleas and vengeance.

The Legend

Sulis was the presiding goddess of the hot springs at Bath, in Somerset, where steaming mineral water wells up from deep in the earth at a constant heat — a phenomenon that to the Britons could only be the work of a powerful deity. She was a goddess of healing and of sacred water, and her shrine at the spring was a place of pilgrimage long before the Romans arrived. When they came, rather than displace her they fused her with their own Minerva, so that the great temple and bathing complex they raised over the spring was dedicated to Sulis Minerva — a striking survival of a native British name at the heart of a Roman religious centre.

The Divine Presence

The most vivid testimony to her cult is the hoard of curse tablets, known as defixiones, recovered from the spring. Hundreds of small sheets of lead and pewter, inscribed and folded, were cast into her waters by ordinary people seeking justice: the victims of theft above all, who named suspected culprits and asked the goddess to afflict them — to deny them sleep, blood and health — until the stolen goods, often something as humble as a bath-house cloak or a few coins, were returned. They are among the most personal voices to survive from Roman Britain.

Sulis thus held two faces: the gentle healer whose warm waters eased the sick, and the implacable power to whom the wronged appealed for vengeance. Her spring still rises at Bath, and the gilded bronze head of her cult statue, recovered from the site, remains one of the finest images of a deity to come down from Roman Britain.

Origins & Dating

Approximate Originc. 60-70 AD (temple construction)

Earliest Written RecordTemple to Sulis Minerva built c.60-70 AD at Aquae Sulis, within the first few decades of Roman Britain; the associated curse tablets (defixiones) recovered from the Sacred Spring in a 1979-1980 excavation (c.130 tablets found under the King's Bath) date from the 2nd century to the late 4th century AD, per the full corpus published by Roger Tomlin as Tabellae Sulis: Roman Inscribed Tablets of Tin and Lead from the Sacred Spring at Bath (Oxford University Committee for Archaeology, monograph 16, 1988)

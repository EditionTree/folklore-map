Sutton Hoo

In 1938 archaeologists opened an undisturbed Anglo-Saxon ship-burial at Sutton Hoo, a king buried with his ship, his gold and the now-iconic helmet, in the same funeral rite that opens Beowulf.

The Legend

On a bluff above the River Deben near Woodbridge in Suffolk, a group of Anglo-Saxon burial mounds was excavated in 1938 and yielded the breathtaking treasure of an undisturbed royal ship-burial: a great rowing ship dragged up from the river and buried whole, filled with gold, garnets, silver and the now-iconic helmet, almost certainly the grave of an early 7th-century king of the East Angles, very possibly Rædwald. It is one of the most important archaeological sites in Britain, but long before the archaeologists came the mounds had their own folklore.

The great burial mounds keep the shape of an Anglo-Saxon world beneath the turf.

The Ship Beneath the Mound

The grassy barrows had stood, conspicuous and mysterious, for thirteen centuries, and local tradition had woven tales around them, stories of buried treasure and of figures seen about the mounds at night, the common folklore of ancient burial-grounds where something is felt to lie hidden and watchful. An earlier attempt to dig into one mound is said to have been thwarted, and the landowner of the 1930s, Edith Pretty, was by repute interested in spiritualism and had reportedly been told of, or sensed, ghostly figures and a sense of presence on the mounds, a circumstance often credited with prompting the excavation that uncovered the treasure.

In the great ship-burial itself there is a powerful echo of legend: the ship-funeral of a king, laden with arms and gold and sent into the earth, is the very scene with which the Old English epic Beowulf opens and closes, so that Sutton Hoo gives solid, golden reality to the world of Anglo-Saxon heroic poetry. Where folklore once told of treasure under the haunted mounds, the spade proved it true, and found the burial of a king out of the age of legend.

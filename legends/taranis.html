Taranis

tuh-RUN-is

The Celtic god of thunder, his wheel-symbol found carved across Roman Britain from Chester to Hadrian's Wall. Like Jupiter but wilder — his rumbling marked the turning of fate. Worshipped with fire and offering.

The Legend

Taranis was the Celtic god of thunder, his very name derived from the Celtic word for thunder (compare Welsh taran, 'thunder'). He is named by the Roman poet Lucan as one of three great Celtic gods honoured with dread sacrifices, alongside Esus and Teutates, and the Romans equated him with their own sky-father Jupiter — the thunderer and wielder of the lightning-bolt. Across the Celtic provinces, including Britain, he is identified by his distinctive emblem: the wheel.

The Divine Presence

The solar or thunder-wheel is Taranis's defining symbol, found carved on altars and cast as small votive wheels offered to him, from sites across Roman Britain. Statues and reliefs of a 'Jupiter with a wheel' represent this fusion of the native thunder-god with the Roman sky-king, a powerful deity of storm, sky and the turning heavens whose rumbling overhead was the sound of divine power and, perhaps, of fate. His worship is recorded as having involved fire, and the classical sources darkly associate the three named gods with human sacrifice, though such accounts must be read with caution as the testimony of conquerors.

In Britain, Taranis belongs to the company of great Celtic sky and weather deities honoured under Roman forms, his wheel-symbol scattered from the south to Hadrian's Wall. As the god in the thunder, he embodies the awe of the storm — the same primal reverence for the power above that the incoming Anglo-Saxons would later bring with their own thunder-god, Thunor.

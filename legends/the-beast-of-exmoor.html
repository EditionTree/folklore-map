The Beast of Exmoor

Since the 1970s, and especially after a South Molton farmer reported the loss of more than a hundred sheep to unexplained throat injuries over three months in 1983, Exmoor has had a persistent tradition of a large phantom cat roaming its moorland. A Royal Marines tracking operation in 1988 found no trace of any animal, and no large cat has ever been captured or conclusively identified.

The Legend

Reports of a large, cat-like predator on Exmoor date back to the 1970s, but the tradition became a national story in 1983 when farmer Eric Ley, near South Molton, reported that more than a hundred of his sheep had been killed in a few months, many with wounds described as clean throat injuries inconsistent with dog attacks. The scale of the losses and the press attention that followed prompted an official response, including a Ministry of Agriculture investigation and, in 1988, a tracking operation by Royal Marines marksmen, none of which produced a carcass, clear photograph, or other physical evidence of a large cat.

A Ministry of Agriculture investigation and a 1988 Royal Marines tracking operation produced no carcass, clear photograph, or other physical evidence of a large cat.

The Cat on the Heather Ridge

The leading explanation offered by researchers is that one or more large cats, such as pumas or black leopards kept privately, were released into the wild after the Dangerous Wild Animals Act 1976 made private ownership more difficult, and that a small, elusive population (or a succession of individual escaped or released animals over the decades) has persisted since. Sightings and livestock losses attributed to the Beast have continued intermittently since the 1980s, but no scientific study has confirmed a breeding population, and some incidents have subsequently been attributed to feral dogs or natural causes.

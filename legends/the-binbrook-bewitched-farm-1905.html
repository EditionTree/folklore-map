The Binbrook Bewitched Farm (1905)

Flying sausages, a dead rabbit that kept coming back to its hook, and a wagon six horses couldn't shift — the 1905 "witching" of Walk Farm made national news before the trail went cold.

The Legend

In the winter of 1904-1905, foreman Henry White and his wife Sarah Jane, tenants of Walk Farm near Binbrook on the Lincolnshire Wolds, reported an escalating run of inexplicable events: potted plants tilting and righting themselves on the windowsill, a dead rabbit that detached itself from a hook, ran about, and returned to the hook, and brooms that came "dancing" across the floor at dinnertime. The disturbances were widely reported in the local and national press through January and February 1905, and grew steadily more sensational — strings of home-made sausages breaking apart after being re-hung three times, a dozen dinner plates found relocated unbroken from a high shelf, and eventually a loaded wagon that six horses together could not move, while the foreman was reportedly wheeled about the yard at speed in a wheelbarrow by unseen hands.

The Story

The household's servant girl, Mabel Sheldrick, was seriously burned when her clothing caught fire while she stood well clear of any hearth, and spent nearly a month recovering in Louth Hospital — an injury that hardened suspicions of witchcraft against her specifically. Offers to "lay the witch" poured in from around the country, and the Whites eventually accepted help from a professional witch-doctor, John Dunn of Grimsby. Mrs White later disputed many of the more fantastical newspaper embellishments, and by March 1905 press interest had faded and the case went cold.

The episode is documented independently in period-newspaper reprints and in local folklore history, and is notable for being investigated as recently as 1905 — remarkably late for an English witchcraft scare.

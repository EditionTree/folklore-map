The Bute Witches

Four named women, Margaret McLevin, Margaret McWilliam, Janet Morrison and Isobell McNicoll, were tried at Rothesay in 1661-62 during the Great Scottish Witch Hunt; one was said to keep a fairy of her own.

The Legend

The Rothesay case belongs to the Great Scottish Witch Hunt of 1661-62, the second and largest of Scotland's national witch panics, which swept dozens of parishes across the Lowlands and the western isles in little more than a year. During it, six women from the parish of Rothesay on the Isle of Bute were interrogated for witchcraft; historians identify four of them, Margaret McLevin, Margaret McWilliam, Janet Morrison and Isobell McNicoll, as having been executed in 1662. The case opened on 15 January 1662, when Janet Morrison gave the first recorded statement against her neighbours at her own home.

she 'soon brought home a little fairy of her own to nurse.'

The Trial on the Island

A sixth accused, Janet McNicol, escaped that fate only to meet it later. Named by the four executed women and imprisoned in Rothesay's Tolbooth in 1662, she fled to Kilmarnock before she could be tried. She was not forgotten: rearrested eleven years later, she was tried again at Rothesay on 15 October 1673, found guilty, and executed by strangulation and burning on 24 October 1673, more than a decade after the case that first named her.

Margaret McWilliam was the most notorious of the group tried in 1662. She had been regarded by her neighbours as a witch for more than thirty years before the trial, and her reputation was entangled with an earlier Bute tragedy. In 1630, a group of women starved to death while imprisoned in Rothesay Castle, and before dying they accused McWilliam of being a fairy herself, claiming she 'soon brought home a little fairy of her own to nurse.'

The Bute case is unusually well documented for an island trial of this period and illustrates how, in the Scottish islands, accusations of witchcraft were frequently inseparable from older beliefs about fairies, changelings and the 'good neighbours'. The accused were as likely to be feared as intermediaries with the fairy world as they were as servants of the Devil.

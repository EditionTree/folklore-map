The Cauld Lad of Hylton

The spirit of a stable boy killed by a lord of Hylton Castle. He haunted the kitchens, tidying if work was left undone, until given a cloak and hood, whereupon he vanished forever.

The Legend

The Cauld Lad of Hylton (the 'Cold Lad') is the famous spirit of Hylton Castle near Sunderland, in the old county of Durham, and one of the best-known household hauntings of north-east England. Local tradition names him as a stable-boy, killed by the Baron of Hylton and never decently laid to rest, though the story's grip on a real death is looser than it first appears. The killing most often attached to the legend is that of Roger Skelton, a stable boy accidentally struck by a scythe on 3 July 1609 while Sir Robert Hylton, 13th Baron of Hylton, was mowing; an inquest ruled it 'accident of misfortune', and Hylton received a formal pardon from Bishop James that September. Robert Surtees, who first set the tale down in print in his History and Antiquities of the County Palatine of Durham (1820), treated the Cauld Lad primarily as an older brownie or household-spirit tradition in its own right, noting that some tellings had already folded the Skelton death into it as 'an admixture of English superstition'.

The Haunting at the Heart of the Tale

Whatever its origin, the ghost haunted the castle kitchens in the manner of a brownie as much as a vengeful spirit. The servants would hear him at night: if they had left the room tidy, the Cauld Lad would mischievously throw everything into disorder, scattering the pewter and the ashes; but if they had left it in a mess, he would set it all to rights before morning, working away unseen. He was sometimes heard, shivering, to lament his own cold and naked state, 'Wae's me, wae's me...', a wail that gave him his name.

As with the classic brownie, the way to be rid of him was to give him a gift of clothing. The servants, pitying his cold, left out a green cloak and hood for him by the hearth. At midnight the Cauld Lad came, put them on, and was heard to rejoice that he was now properly dressed, declaring that, having received his reward, he must now depart, and with that he vanished from Hylton Castle and was never seen or heard there again. The tale fuses a household brownie tradition with the memory of a real accidental death, two strands of local history and folklore knotted together so tightly that even Surtees, writing two centuries closer to events, could only partly pull them apart.

The Dagda

DAHG-duh

The Good God of the Tuatha Dé Danann, not good in the moral sense but good at everything: a club that kills and revives, an inexhaustible cauldron, and a harp whose music controls the seasons.

The Legend

The Dagda (Dagda Mór, the Great Good God) is the father-god of the Tuatha Dé Danann, but he is not the remote divine patriarch of later religious traditions. He is enormous, pot-bellied, often dressed in a short tunic that does not quite cover him, dragging a vast club behind him on a wheel. He eats cauldrons of porridge stirred with a ladle the size of a man. He is, in other words, deliberately earthly and comic, and also the most powerful being in his world.

These are the tools of a god who controls death, abundance, and time.

The Three Treasures

His three treasures define him. His club has two ends: strike a man with one end and he dies; touch him with the other and he revives. His cauldron, the Undry, is inexhaustible, no company ever left it unsatisfied. His harp, called Uaithne or the Four-Angled Music, plays the three strains of Irish music by itself: the strain that makes men weep, the strain that makes them laugh, and the strain that puts them to sleep, and only the Dagda himself can ever coax a note from it. When the Fomorians stole the harp during their war with the Tuatha Dé Danann, they found they could get no sound from it at all; the Dagda simply called it by name, and it tore itself off the wall and flew back into his hands, killing nine Fomorians on its way across the hall.

Before the second, decisive battle at Mag Tuired, the Dagda met the war-goddess the Morrígan washing herself at the river Unius; the two became lovers there, and in return she promised him her power against the Fomorian host the next day. The myths treat him throughout with a mixture of reverence and satirical affection that is characteristic of Irish mythological writing. He negotiates badly at treaties, is tricked out of his home at Brú na Bóinne by his son Aengus, and has a series of improbable lovers. But when it matters, at the battles of Mag Tuired, the Dagda's power is absolute. He is the ground under everything, the force that will not be denied.

Historical Context

Origins & Dating

Historical PeriodMedieval Irish literature; text composed/redacted c. 9th-12th century, surviving manuscripts 14th-16th century
Earliest Written RecordCath Maige Tuired (The Second Battle of Mag Tuired) is the principal early narrative source for the Dagda. The fullest surviving text is in Harleian MS 5280 (British Library), dated to the first half of the 16th century, with a fragmentary version also preserved in the Yellow Book of Lecan (compiled c. 1391-1401). Philologist Gerard Murphy characterised the surviving redaction as the product of an 11th-12th century editor working mainly from 9th-century material. The Dagda also appears in the Metrical Dindshenchas and in Lebor Gabala Erenn (The Book of Invasions), an 11th-12th century compilation extant in manuscripts including the Book of Leinster (c. 1160).

Folklore dates are often approximate, especially where a story predates any surviving written source.

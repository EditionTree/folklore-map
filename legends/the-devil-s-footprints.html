Sacred Sites Exe Estuary, Devon

The Devil's Footprints

On the night of 8 to 9 February 1855, after heavy snowfall, single lines of small hoof-like marks appeared overnight across a great stretch of east and south Devon, reportedly totalling forty to a hundred miles across more than thirty locations. The tracks crossed rooftops, walls and rivers as though the obstacles were not there, and were widely blamed on the Devil at the time.

The Legend

The marks measured roughly four inches long by three inches across, spaced eight to sixteen inches apart, mostly in a single line as though made by a two legged, cloven hoofed creature. They were reported from towns and villages along and near the Exe estuary, including Exmouth, Topsham, Dawlish and Teignmouth, and a couple of locations across the county border in Dorset. Contemporary press, notably The Times and the Illustrated London News, carried accounts within days, and the story quickly became a national sensation with the Devil named as the obvious culprit given the tracks' apparent disregard for physical barriers.

The Story

No single explanation has ever accounted for every reported track. Proposals over the years have included wandering badgers, rats, cats, or other animals leaving overlapping trails made confusing by melting and refreezing snow, an escaped kangaroo (a theory later shown to be a fabrication), and hopping rodents whose paired prints could resemble a single cloven hoof when the animal's stride matched the snow conditions. A 1950 search of Devon archives by folklorist and researcher Mike Dash and others recovered letters and tracings connected to the local vicar Henry Thomas Ellacombe of Bitton, but produced no decisive resolution. What can be dated with confidence is the event itself and its wide contemporary press coverage in February and March 1855.

The Dullahan

A headless horseman carrying his own severed head, which glows like a lantern. Where the Dullahan stops and calls your name, you die. No lock or gate can bar his passage.

The Legend

The Dullahan rides the night roads of Ireland as a harbinger of death, a headless horseman carrying his own severed head, which he holds aloft or sets upon his saddle — a head with a hideous grinning face the colour and texture of mouldy cheese, and eyes that dart about ceaselessly, glowing with an unearthly light by which the rider sees his way, sometimes said to be used as a lantern. Few figures in Irish folklore are as feared. In many tellings he drives a black coach, the cóiste bodhar or 'death coach', drawn by black horses, its trappings made from grisly materials — a spine for a whip, human bones, a shroud-cloth.

The Rider on the Road

Where the Dullahan halts his ride and calls out a name, the person named will die; for the calling of the name is itself the summons of death, and no door, lock or gate can bar the Dullahan's way — all fly open before him. He is implacable and not to be watched: those who spy on his passage may be lashed blind by his bone whip or have a basin of blood flung over them, marking them as the next to die. Only one thing is said to give him pause: gold. A piece of gold, even a small one, will frighten the Dullahan away, the single weakness in an otherwise unstoppable terror.

The Dullahan belongs to the same family of death-omens as the banshee in Irish tradition, an embodiment of death come riding rather than wailing. Popularised in the 19th-century folklore collections, he has ridden on into modern imagination, and stands behind many a later headless-horseman tale.

Origins & Dating

Historical PeriodAttested in print from the early-to-mid 19th century; believed to reflect older Irish oral tradition
Earliest Written RecordThe earliest substantial written documentation is in Thomas Crofton Croker's Fairy Legends and Traditions of the South of Ireland, Part II (1828; Part I published 1825), which devotes a chapter to the Dullahan containing four stories and a ballad, with Croker's own commentary on the name's etymology (from Irish dubh, 'black/dark').

Folklore dates are often approximate, especially where a story predates any surviving written source.

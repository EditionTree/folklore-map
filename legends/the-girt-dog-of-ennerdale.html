The Girt Dog of Ennerdale

Through the summer of 1810 a mysterious dog killed hundreds of sheep across the Cumberland fells, draining their blood and eating only the choicest parts, eluding huge hunts before it was finally shot.

The Legend

The Girt Dog of Ennerdale was a real terror of the Cumberland fells in 1810, but one that passed swiftly into legend. From May to September a single elusive animal killed between three and four hundred sheep across the high ground around Ennerdale, and what unnerved the dalesmen was its manner: it drained its victims of blood, ate only the choicest parts, and slew far more than it could eat, sometimes seven or eight sheep in a night.

The Fell Predator of 1810

A reward was posted and great hunts were mounted, one so eagerly anticipated that the men of Ennerdale abandoned a Sunday church service to join it. On a single July day two hundred men and their hounds combed the Kinniside fells without success, and the creature evaded every pursuit, seeming to vanish and reappear at will. Witnesses who glimpsed it compared it variously to a lion and to a cross between a mastiff and a greyhound, though no one could agree on quite what they had seen.

It was finally shot on 12 September 1810 by a man named John Steel; the carcass weighed some eight stone, roughly a hundredweight, and its skin was stuffed and displayed at Hutton's Museum in Keswick, where it remained until it was discarded, decayed beyond keeping, later in the century. Its strange habits, the blood-draining, the taste for soft organs, have led some since to wonder, fancifully, whether it was an escaped thylacine; in the dales at the time it was simply the 'Girt Dog', a flesh-and-blood beast that hunted like a phantom.

Historical Context

Origins & Dating

Approximate Origin1810
Historical PeriodEarly 19th century (Georgian era)
Earliest Written RecordEvents dated to May-September 1810 in Ennerdale and the surrounding Cumberland fells: first sighting attributed to a Mr Mossop of Thornholme on 10 May 1810; the animal was run to ground and shot near the River Ehen on 12 September 1810, having reportedly killed 300+ sheep. A reward of 10 pounds for the dog's capture was offered by John Russell, a Whitehaven brewery owner and Ennerdale sheep farmer. The most-cited historical document is a 19th-century reprint pamphlet, The Story of the Wild Dog of Ennerdale: Reprinted from the Whitehaven Herald (Whitehaven: Callander & Dixon, 1864).

Folklore dates are often approximate, especially where a story predates any surviving written source.

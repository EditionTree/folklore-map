The Green Knight

A huge green challenger who enters Arthur's court carrying a holly branch and an axe. His beheading game with Gawain tests courage, courtesy, truth, and the strangeness of winter.

The Legend

The Green Knight is the eerie supernatural challenger of the great 14th-century English poem Sir Gawain and the Green Knight, one of the masterpieces of medieval literature. He bursts into King Arthur's Christmas feast at Camelot: an enormous man, entirely green from his skin to his clothes to his horse, bearing a holly branch in one hand and a great axe in the other. He issues a strange and terrible 'beheading game' — he will allow any knight to strike him a single blow with the axe, on condition that the same knight seek him out a year and a day later to receive a blow in return.

His beheading game with Gawain tests courage, courtesy, truth, and the strangeness of winter.

The Beheading Game

Gawain takes up the challenge and strikes the Green Knight's head clean from his shoulders — whereupon the giant simply picks up his own severed head, which speaks, reminding Gawain of his bargain and naming the Green Chapel as the trysting-place, before riding away. The rest of the poem follows Gawain's honour-bound winter journey to keep his appointment and meet what seems certain death, and the subtler test of his virtue that awaits him at the castle of a hospitable lord along the way.

At the climax the Green Knight is revealed to be that same lord, Bertilak de Hautdesert, transformed by the magic of Morgan le Fay, who devised the whole game to test the renown of the Round Table and frighten Guinevere. He spares Gawain with only a nicking blow for his single small failing — concealing the magic green girdle — and sends him home wiser and humbled. As a figure the Green Knight blends the menace of a beheading-game demon, the vitality of a green man of the woods, and the courtesy of a great lord, embodying the strangeness, the testing and the deep midwinter mystery at the heart of the poem.

Beasts Liverpool, Merseyside

The Liver Birds

Two copper birds stand on the clock towers of the Royal Liver Building, one turned to the city and one to the sea, and pointedly not to each other. Local tradition holds that they are chained in place, because if they ever broke free and flew off the Mersey would burst its banks and drown Liverpool. They are kept facing apart for a second reason. Were the pair to mate and leave together, the city would cease to exist.

The Legend

The birds were designed by the German sculptor Carl Bernard Bartels and went up when the Royal Liver Building opened in 1911. Each is eighteen feet tall with a wingspan of twenty four, and each is fixed down hard against the weather coming up the estuary, which is the mundane origin of the chains the legend makes so much of.

The Story

The liver bird itself is far older than the building and has served as the town's emblem since the medieval seal, but the superstition belongs to the twentieth century. It grew up around the statues once they were tall enough to be seen from every part of the waterfront, and it works the way a city's protective myths usually do, by making the survival of the place depend on something anyone can look up and check.

The two versions sit oddly together and are usually told as though they were one. In the first the birds are shackled against flight and the danger is flood. In the second they are turned away from one another and the danger is courtship. Both agree that Liverpool stands only as long as the birds stay where they are.

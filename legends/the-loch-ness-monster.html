The Loch Ness Monster

Britain's most famous monster lurks in Loch Ness's peat-dark depths — first recorded in 565 AD when St Columba banished a river-beast, and hunted by camera and sonar ever since.

The Legend

The earliest recorded brush with something unusual in the waters around Loch Ness comes from Adomnán's seventh-century Life of St Columba, which describes the saint encountering an "aquatic monster" in the River Ness in 565 AD. When the beast lunged at one of his companions, Columba is said to have made the sign of the cross and commanded it to go no further, and it obeyed, retreating as if dragged back by ropes. For centuries afterwards, the loch and its surrounding glens remained part of a Highland landscape rich in tales of water-horses and other lake-dwelling spirits.

Modern Nessie has been hunted by cameras, submarines and sonar for over a century, always elusive.

The Beast in the Water

The modern legend began in earnest in 1933, after a new road was cut along the loch's northern shore, giving travellers an unbroken view of the water for the first time. A wave of sightings followed, including a much-publicised report of a "dragon or prehistoric animal" crossing the road. The following year brought the so-called Surgeon's Photograph, showing a long-necked head and neck rising from the water, an image that shaped the popular idea of Nessie as a surviving plesiosaur, and which was only revealed decades later to have been staged with a toy submarine and a sculpted head.

Despite repeated sonar sweeps, underwater cameras, and large-scale searches such as Operation Deepscan in 1987, no conclusive evidence of a large unknown animal has ever been found. The most rigorous recent attempt came in 2018, when geneticist Neil Gemmell of the University of Otago led an international team that took some 250 water samples from around the loch and sequenced the environmental DNA, the traces of skin, scale and waste that every living thing sheds into water, comparing it against global genetic databases. They found no trace of anything resembling a large reptile, but did find an unexpectedly large quantity of eel DNA at almost every sampling point, enough that Gemmell suggested the loch could hold eels of an unusual size, and that a sufficiently large eel might explain at least some sightings, though the theory remains unproven.

Yet the loch remains one of the most surveyed bodies of water on earth, and Nessie has become inseparable from Scotland's image abroad, a rare case of a folkloric water-beast outliving its origins to become a modern cultural icon in its own right.

Historical Context

Origins & Dating

Approximate Origin565 AD (claimed date of the recorded encounter between St Columba and a river-beast in the Ness)
Earliest Written RecordAdomnan of Iona (ninth abbot of Iona), Vita Sanctae Columbae (Life of St Columba), Book II, Chapter 27, composed c. 697 AD (before Adomnan's death in 704) -- roughly 130 years after the event it describes. The earliest surviving manuscript copy is the Schaffhausen Codex (Schaffhausen, Stadtbibliothek, MS Generalia 1), written at Iona and dated to c. 700-713 AD, making it the earliest extant text of the episode.

Folklore dates are often approximate, especially where a story predates any surviving written source.

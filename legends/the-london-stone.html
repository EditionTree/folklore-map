Sacred Sites Cannon Street, London

The London Stone

A weathered block of limestone, now displayed behind glass at 111 Cannon Street, has stood on or near the same spot in the City of London since at least the twelfth century and possibly since Roman times. A Victorian tradition holds that it is the remnant of a palladium brought from Troy by the legendary founder Brutus, and that London's fortunes are tied to the stone's safety, though this specific claim was only put forward in 1857.

The Legend

The London Stone is first recorded by name around the year 1100, and references through the medieval and early modern periods treat it as a notable City landmark, most famously in Shakespeare's Henry VI Part 2, where the rebel Jack Cade strikes it with his sword while claiming the city in 1450. Its original purpose and precise age are unknown, though its material and worked surface have led some to suggest a Roman origin, possibly as part of a larger structure such as a milestone or building foundation, without firm proof either way.

The Story

The now-popular idea that the stone is connected to Brutus of Troy, the legendary founder of Britain from Geoffrey of Monmouth's twelfth century pseudo-history, and that it once supported a palladium protecting London, was not attached to the stone until the Reverend Richard Williams Morgan's book The British Kymry, published in 1857. Morgan quoted a supposed ancient proverb that so long as the Stone of Brutus is safe, so long shall London flourish, but no version of this proverb or the Brutus connection has been traced earlier than his own writing. Geoffrey of Monmouth's original Brutus legend makes no mention of the stone at all. The object itself has been moved and re-housed several times over the centuries, most recently returning to a purpose-built case at 111 Cannon Street, opposite its traditional site, in 2018.

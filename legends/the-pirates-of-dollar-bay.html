The Pirates of Dollar Bay

Four mutineers slaughtered Captain Glass, his wife and daughter aboard the Earl of Sandwich in 1765 and buried 250 sacks of Spanish silver on a lonely Wexford beach, giving Dollar Bay its name.

The Legend

On the night of 30 November 1765, four crewmen of the merchant ship Earl of Sandwich turned on their captain and passengers as she ran through the English Channel bound for London from Tenerife. Peter McKinley, an Irish boatswain, George Gidley, a Yorkshire-born cook, Richard St Quentin, an Englishman, and Andreas Zekerman, a Dutch seaman, beat Captain John Cochrane to death with a marlinspike and threw him overboard. Among the passengers was Captain George Glass of the Royal Navy, an adventurer returning from the Canary Islands with a fortune in Spanish silver and gold dust; Glass fought back and wounded two of his attackers before he was overpowered, and his wife and young daughter Kathleen were thrown into the sea after him. Only two cabin boys were spared.

The Figure Behind the Legend

The mutineers scuttled the Earl of Sandwich once they were within reach of the Irish coast and rowed inshore in the ship's boat, so heavily loaded with coin that it nearly swamped; they tipped handfuls of silver dollars overboard to keep it afloat. About two miles south of Duncannon Fort they waded ashore on a lonely stretch of the Hook Peninsula and buried some 250 sacks of coin in the dunes, fearing the fort's garrison would search them if they carried it further. Flush with cash they had not troubled to hide, the four spent their way toward New Ross, paying for ale and giving away the drowned family's jewellery to innkeepers along the road; the spending drew notice within the week.

A magistrate's party backed by soldiers from Duncannon Fort recovered the buried treasure from the sand and shipped it under guard to the Custom House. All four mutineers were tried in Dublin and hanged at St Stephen's Green on 3 March 1766; their bodies were caged in chains and displayed as a warning, McKinley and Gidley's on the small rock islet of the Muglins in Dublin Bay.

The beach where the silver was dug from the sand has been called Dollar Bay ever since, and not every coin was recovered. Local tradition holds that some of the Earl of Sandwich's treasure still lies in the dunes, and metal detectorists still search the shore for it. Some tellers of the tale credit it with helping to shape Robert Louis Stevenson's Treasure Island.

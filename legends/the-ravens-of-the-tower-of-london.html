The Ravens of the Tower of London

Ravens are kept at the Tower of London under the care of a Yeoman Warder titled the Ravenmaster, and tradition holds that if they ever leave, the Crown will fall and Britain with it. The custom is popularly credited to Charles II. The written record tells a different story. No reference to ravens at the Tower has been found before 1883, and the prophecy itself is first attested in 1944.

The Legend

The Tower of London keeps a flock of ravens on the South Lawn, tended by a Yeoman Warder who holds the title of Ravenmaster. The tradition attached to them is one of the best known superstitions in Britain. If the ravens ever leave the Tower, it is said, the Crown will fall and the kingdom with it. Six birds are the working minimum, usually with a spare, and their wing feathers are trimmed so that they stay.

The Story

The story most often told credits Charles II. In one version his astronomer John Flamsteed complained that the birds were fouling his observations from the White Tower, and the king, warned of what would follow if they were destroyed, ordered instead that six be kept for ever. A separate and far older strand connects the site to Bran the Blessed of the Mabinogi, whose name means raven and whose head was buried beneath the White Hill to guard Britain against invasion.

The documentary record supports neither connection. Geoffrey Parnell, the Tower's official historian, and the independent scholar Boria Sax both searched for early references and found none at all. The earliest known evidence of ravens living at the Tower is an illustration of 1883, and the birds appear to have been introduced in the Victorian period as living props for the fortress's grim reputation. The prophecy that the kingdom would fall with them is first recorded in the summer of 1944, when Londoners watched the birds through the Blitz. That makes it one of the youngest national legends in Britain, and one of the most thoroughly believed.

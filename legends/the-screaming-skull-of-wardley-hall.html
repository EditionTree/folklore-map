The Screaming Skull of Wardley Hall

A human skull kept in a niche on the staircase at Wardley Hall is said to answer with storms and uproar whenever anyone tries to remove or bury it. Lancashire tradition long named it as the head of the Restoration rake Roger Downes, an identification the opening of his coffin in 1779 destroyed. It is now accepted as the head of Ambrose Barlow, a Benedictine priest executed at Lancaster in 1641.

The Legend

Wardley Hall is a moated timber framed manor house of about 1500 near Worsley in Salford, Grade I listed and, since 1930, the residence of the Roman Catholic Bishop of Salford. Set into a niche at the head of the main staircase is a human skull, and around it has grown one of the best documented of the English screaming skull traditions. Move the skull, bury it, throw it into the moat or carry it from the house, and the hall is said to answer with storms, crashing noises and misfortune until it is put back.

The Skull on the Stair

The tradition as the Lancashire antiquaries received it named the skull as that of Roger Downes, the last male heir of his family and remembered as one of the more dissolute courtiers of Charles II. In that telling he killed a tailor in a drunken London brawl, was beheaded by a watchman on London Bridge, and his head was boxed up and sent home to his sister at Wardley. John Roby printed a version in his Traditions of Lancashire in 1829, and Harland and Wilkinson gave it again in Lancashire Legends in 1867. The story failed its first real test. When the Downes coffin was opened in 1779 the head was still on the body.

The identification now accepted is a different one altogether. Ambrose Barlow, a Benedictine priest who had ministered secretly in the district for years, was seized while preaching on Easter Sunday 1641 and hanged, drawn and quartered at Lancaster on 10 September of that year. His head was set on a spike in Manchester, and a Catholic kinsman, Francis Downes, is said to have taken it down by night and hidden it at Wardley. A forensic examination in 1960 found the skull of a man of about fifty to sixty years, pierced by a sharp object, which fits Barlow closely. He was canonised in 1970 among the Forty Martyrs of England and Wales, and the skull is now treated at the hall as a relic rather than a curiosity.

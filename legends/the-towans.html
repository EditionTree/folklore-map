The Towans

Beneath these Cornish dunes lies the Lost City of Connor, swallowed by sand along with St Gwithian’s ancient church — while storm winds are said to reveal the turrets of a tyrant king’s castle.

The legend

The Towans — from the Cornish tewyn, meaning sand dune — stretch for three miles along St Ives Bay between the Hayle estuary and Gwithian beach. Beneath their shifting surface lie the remains of the Lost City of Connor, once so significant it served as the administrative seat of the Hundred of Penwith before successive sand inundations forced the courts to relocate to Penzance in 1771. The ruins of St Gwithian’s chapel, built around 490 AD and dedicated to the patron saint of good fortune at sea, were briefly uncovered from the beach and dunes in the early nineteenth century before the shifting sands reclaimed them.

The story

Local legend tells that during fierce gales the turrets of a castle belonging to King Teudar can be glimpsed through the storm. Teudar (sometimes Latinised as Theodoric) was a Breton exile who ruled Penwith from Carnsew near Hayle in the sixth century, and became infamous for martyring Irish missionary saints — including St Gwinear, whom he cast into a pit of serpents, and members of St Breaca’s mission. His cruelty was so notorious that later hagiographers blamed him for persecutions he could not possibly have committed. Beyond legend, the dunes have genuinely swallowed real buildings: sometime after 1650 the farmhouse at Upton Barton was buried overnight when a high dune collapsed, its occupants escaping through the upper windows. The ruins were last seen in the winter of 1808–09, before the sands closed over them for good.

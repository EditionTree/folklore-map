The Witch of East Somerton

A woman accused of witchcraft was said to have been buried alive in the nave of St Mary's Church at East Somerton, and her wooden leg took root and grew into the great oak that still stands inside the ruined church today.

The Legend

In the ruins of St Mary's Church at East Somerton, deep in a Norfolk wood, a massive oak tree now grows where the nave once stood, its trunk rising through walls of knapped flint and ashlar. The tower dates to the 13th century and the nave was rebuilt in the 15th, and the building had fallen out of regular worship by the late 17th century. Local legend says the tree marks the grave of a woman from the village who was accused of witchcraft and buried alive within the church by her neighbours. As she was interred, her wooden leg was said to have been planted with her, and over the centuries it took root and grew into the oak that dominates the ruin today, sometimes called 'the Witch's Leg' or 'the Witch's Finger'.

The ruined church keeps its legend in wood and stone: a grave remembered as a tree.

The Oak in the Nave

The ruin is substantial enough to read as a building rather than a scatter of stones. Its three-stage west tower still stands, missing its belfry stage, braced by diagonal buttresses and lit by pointed lancet windows in the ringing chamber below; three tall windows once lined the nave on each side, and the chancel arch survives with roll and sunk hollow mouldings, though no trace of the chancel itself remains. Large holes have been broken through the walls of the two westernmost bays, and Historic England listed the ruin Grade II in September 1962.

The legend carries its own piece of living folk practice: it is said that anyone who walks around the tree three times will release the witch's spirit. The story has become one of the best known pieces of 'Weird Norfolk' folklore, regularly retold by local newspapers and the Norfolk Folklore Society, and the ruin, oak growing through broken flint walls in dense woodland near Winterton-on-Sea, remains a popular, if eerie, walking destination today.

Sacred Sites Thurstaston, Wirral, Merseyside

Thor's Stone

A rib of red sandstone stands clear of the heather on Thurstaston Common, and Victorian antiquaries made it an altar where Norse settlers cut throats in honour of Thor. The Wirral really was Scandinavian ground, with an assembly site at Thingwall a few miles off, which is what gave the story its footing. An older and quieter local name for the rock was Fair Maiden's Hall, and children were remembered dancing round it once a year.

The Legend

The sacrificial reading was established by the nineteenth century and has proved almost impossible to shift. Geologists and historians now agree that the outcrop is natural, a small tor left by periglacial weathering of the sandstone and afterwards cut about by quarrymen in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. The straight faces that look worked are worked, but by men taking building stone rather than by anyone preparing an altar.

The Story

The Fair Maiden's Hall tradition is the more interesting half and the less often repeated. It was noted in 1888 by the Reverend A. E. P. Gray, who recorded both the name and the annual dance, and it has the ordinary shape of genuine local usage rather than the shape of an antiquarian theory.

A Norse district, a red rock and a nineteenth century appetite for Viking blood were enough between them to produce a legend that now has to be argued down every time the site is written about.

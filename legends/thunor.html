Thunor

The Anglo-Saxon thunder god, cognate with Norse Thor. His name echoes in Thursday and in place-names, a hammer-crack of pre-Christian belief beneath later England.

A visual interpretation of Thunor in Anglo-Saxon England
The Legend

Thunor was the thunder-god of the pagan Anglo-Saxons, the English form of the deity the Norse called Thor and the wider Germanic peoples Donar — a god of storm, sky, strength and the protective power of the thunderbolt. Like his Norse cousin he was bound up with the hammer or the axe and with the great noise of the storm, a defender of gods and men against the forces of chaos, and a friend to the ordinary farmer whose crops the rain and thunder served.

The Divine Presence

Though the conversion of England cut short the writing-down of his myths, his importance is preserved in the very calendar and landscape. Thursday is 'Thunor's day', and his name is stamped across the map of England in place-names such as Thundersley ('Thunor's grove') in Essex, Thunderfield, and Thursley ('Thunor's clearing') in Surrey — many of them marking groves or clearings that were once his sacred sites. The frequency of these names suggests he was among the most widely venerated of the English heathen gods, a deity of the open-air shrine and the holy grove.

Archaeology adds its own witness: tiny hammer and axe amulets, and pots and urns stamped with swastika and wheel marks understood as thunder-symbols, point to a popular cult of protection invoking the thunder-god's power over the home and the dead. After conversion Thunor, like Woden, was demonised and half-forgotten, but his hammer-crack still sounds faintly beneath the surface of English place and time.

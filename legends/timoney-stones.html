Sacred Sites Timoney, County Tipperary

Timoney Stones

Over a hundred standing stones scattered across a Tipperary hilltop in no clear pattern — local tradition calls the central flat stone a 'Druid's altar' and holds that men of note lie buried beneath every one.

The Legend

Of the 121 stones that survive on the slopes of Timoney Hills southeast of Roscrea in County Tipperary — 93 still standing, 28 fallen — no obvious plan governs their arrangement, one of the most enigmatic megalithic landscapes in Ireland. Some stand in threes (a tall stone, a short stone, and a slab), others in pairs aligned north-south or east-west, and sixteen form a rough circle some seventy metres across. Their date is disputed: some scholars place them in the Neolithic or Bronze Age, while others have argued for origins as recent as the 19th century.

The hilltop is scattered with stones, one remembered locally as a Druid's altar.

The Stones on the Hill

Local folklore, recorded by the Irish Folklore Commission in the late 1930s, insists on the site's ancient sanctity. Tradition holds that 'there is supposed to be men of note buried at all those stones', casting the Timoney Hills as the oldest graveyard in Ireland. A large, flat central boulder became known as the 'Druid's altar', and the Reverend Gleeson's 1915 historical account of the area linked similar circular stone arrangements in neighbouring parishes to druidic worship. By the 1860s a more playful tradition had emerged, claiming the standing stones marked out an ancient racecourse with judges perched on the altar stone — a reinterpretation that may say more about Victorian sporting culture than Bronze Age ritual.

Whatever their true age and purpose, the Timoney Stones remain Ireland's largest collection of standing stones and one of its most atmospheric. The absence of definitive archaeological explanation has left the field open to folklore, and the hills continue to resist the kind of neat narrative that more thoroughly excavated sites permit.

Tintagel

The Cornish village inseparable from Arthurian legend, Merlin's sea-cave, and the ruined headland castle where medieval writers placed Arthur's conception.

A visual interpretation of Merlin's Cave beneath the Tintagel headland
The Legend

Tintagel is a village on the rugged north coast of Cornwall whose name is inseparable from the legend of King Arthur. The dramatic setting — a great headland half-severed from the mainland, ringed by cliffs and crashing sea, crowned with the ruins of a medieval castle — seems made for myth, and it was here that Geoffrey of Monmouth, in the 12th century, set the conception of Arthur, fixing the hero's origin forever on the Cornish coast.

Whether or not any historical Arthur ever trod the headland, Tintagel remains the single place most powerfully bound to his legend, where landscape, ruin and story meet above the Cornish sea.

The Cave Beneath the Castle

Beneath the headland, at sea level, opens Merlin's Cave, a great tunnel pierced through the rock by the waves and passable at low tide, which tradition links to the wizard: here, in later romance and famously in Tennyson, the infant Arthur was washed ashore from the sea and received into Merlin's keeping. The village and its surroundings are thick with Arthurian association, and modern times have layered the legend ever thicker — from a Victorian and Edwardian tourism built on the Arthur connection to present-day sculptures and the very names of the streets.

Archaeology has shown that Tintagel was no mere fishing village but an important high-status stronghold in the post-Roman 'Dark Age' centuries, trading with the Mediterranean — a genuine seat of Cornish power at exactly the period to which Arthur is traditionally assigned, which has only deepened the romance. Whether or not any historical Arthur ever trod the headland, Tintagel remains the single place most powerfully bound to his legend, where landscape, ruin and story meet above the Cornish sea.

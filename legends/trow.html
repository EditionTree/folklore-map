Trow

A small, troll-like being from Shetland and Orkney folklore, rooted in Norse tradition. Trows live in mounds, dislike sunlight, love music, and are always one bad bargain away.

The Legend

The Trow is the characteristic fairy-being of Shetland and Orkney, a small, ugly, troll-like creature whose very name derives from the Norse troll, a legacy of the islands' long centuries under Norse rule. Trows are creatures of the night and the earth: they live underground or inside the green mounds and howes that dot the islands, many of them prehistoric burial mounds, and shun the daylight, for like trolls they cannot bear the sun. Grey-clad, mischievous and often malign, they form a shadow-society mirroring that of humans, with their own homes, feasts and dances beneath the ground.

They were famous for stealing fiddle-tunes, and many a Shetland reel was said to have been learned by a human fiddler who overheard the trows playing in their mound.

The Tune Beneath the Mound

Above all the trows loved music. They were famous for stealing fiddle-tunes, and many a Shetland reel was said to have been learned by a human fiddler who overheard the trows playing in their mound; conversely, a gifted musician was in danger of being carried off underground to play for the trows' endless revels. They were also blamed for the full range of fairy mischief: stealing milk and the goodness from food, abducting human babies and brides and leaving sickly changelings or stocks in their place, and bringing illness, 'trow-shot', on people and beasts. The midwinter season of the Yules was their most dangerous time, when trows roamed freely and houses had to be 'sained' and protected against them.

Various charms kept them at bay, iron, fire, the Bible, and crossing running water, which they could not pass. The trows endure as the defining supernatural beings of the Northern Isles, a distinctively Norse-rooted strand of British fairy lore, still woven through Shetland's folk tradition and its great midwinter fire-festival culture.

Uffington White Horse

A huge prehistoric chalk horse cut into the Berkshire Downs. It watches over Dragon Hill, Wayland's Smithy, and a landscape where pagan monument, saint legend, and heroic folklore overlap.

The Legend

The Uffington White Horse is a vast, stylised figure of a horse cut into the chalk of the Berkshire Downs in Oxfordshire — a sweeping, abstract outline of trenches filled with white chalk, stretching some 110 metres across the hillside. It is by far the oldest of Britain's chalk hill-figures, dated to the late Bronze Age or early Iron Age, around 3,000 years old, which makes its original meaning and makers a true mystery — and into that mystery folklore has poured for centuries.

The ancient chalk horse watches over a landscape of saint legend and prehistoric monuments.

The Horse on the Downs

Many traditions have tried to claim it. Some held that it commemorated a great victory — that King Alfred cut it to mark his defeat of the Danes nearby, or that it was older still; others reinterpreted it not as a horse at all but as the dragon slain by St George on the neighbouring Dragon Hill. A celebrated local custom was the 'Scouring of the White Horse', a periodic festival at which the people of the district gathered to clean the chalk lines of encroaching grass — a communal duty bound up with fairs, games and revelry, and essential to the figure's survival, for without regular scouring it would vanish under the turf.

A well-known piece of folk-belief attaches to a flat patch within the figure, the 'eye' of the horse: standing upon it and turning around, or making a wish there, was said to bring the wish true. Set amid Uffington Castle hillfort, Dragon Hill and Wayland's Smithy, the White Horse presides over one of Britain's great ritual landscapes — an enigmatic emblem, three millennia old, kept alive by the hands of the people who scoured it and the legends they told of it.

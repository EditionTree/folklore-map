Unicorn

The national animal of Scotland, a symbol not of innocence but of proud untameability. In Scottish heraldry, the unicorn is always shown chained, because an unchained unicorn is too dangerous and too free to be trusted near a king. It was said only a virgin could tame one, and even then only briefly.

The Legend

The unicorn is the national animal of Scotland, and in Scottish heraldry it carries a meaning quite unlike the gentle innocence of later popular imagery: it is a beast of fierce, untameable pride. Medieval bestiaries held the unicorn to be so wild and strong that it could only be captured by a virgin, and so dangerous that it was the natural enemy of the lion, which made it an apt royal emblem in a kingdom long set against lion-bearing England.

In Scottish heraldry, the unicorn is always shown chained, because an unchained unicorn is too dangerous and too free to be trusted near a king.

The Beast Too Wild for Kings

In the royal arms of Scotland the unicorn is always depicted chained, a gold coronet about its neck and a chain trailing from it. The chain is the heart of the symbolism: only a king could hold so proud and perilous a creature, and even then only in bonds. The beast reached beyond heraldry and into the coinage itself: James III struck gold coins bearing the unicorn in the late fifteenth century, the denomination becoming known simply as the unicorn, with a smaller half-unicorn issued alongside it. When the crowns of Scotland and England were united, the British royal arms set the Scottish unicorn opposite the English lion as supporters, the two old adversaries forever facing each other across the shield.

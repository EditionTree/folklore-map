Up Helly Aa

A modern Shetland fire festival rooted in local identity and Norse revival imagery. Torch-bearing squads process through winter darkness before burning a Viking-style galley.

The Legend

Up Helly Aa is the great fire festival of Shetland, the most famous of all the islands' customs, held in the depth of winter — most spectacularly at Lerwick on the last Tuesday of January — when squads of costumed men parade through the dark streets bearing blazing torches and, at the climax, hurl them into a specially built Viking-style longship to burn it in a roaring blaze. It is a celebration of Shetland's Norse heritage and island identity, drawing thousands to watch the fire against the winter night.

The winter procession ends in flame, turning Norse revival imagery into Shetland identity.

The Galley in the Firelight

Despite its Viking trappings, Up Helly Aa in its present form is largely a creation of the later 19th century, when older and rowdier midwinter fire customs — the burning of tar-barrels and Yuletide revelry that marked the end of the festive season — were reshaped into the organised, costumed torchlight procession and galley-burning seen today, with its 'Guizer Jarl' leading the chief squad in Norse warrior dress. The name is generally taken to mean something like 'end of the holy days', marking the close of the long Yule season.

Beneath the Victorian pageantry lie genuinely ancient roots: midwinter fire-festivals to drive back the darkness and welcome the returning light are among the oldest of human customs, and Shetland's were bound up with its Norse past and the trow-haunted dangers of the Yule tide. Up Helly Aa fuses that deep tradition of midwinter fire with a proud assertion of the islands' Scandinavian inheritance, the burning galley lighting up the longest, darkest weeks of the Shetland year.

Wade and Bell

The giant Wade and his wife Bell built castles by throwing their one hammer to each other across the moors, and Wade paved a road, later mistaken for Roman, so she could cross to milk her cow.

The Legend

In the folklore of the North York Moors, the giant Wade, and his wife, whose name is given as Bell or in some tellings simply Bel, were the builders of the landscape. Bell raised Mulgrave Castle and Wade built Pickering Castle, working at the same time across the moors and sharing a single great hammer that they flung back and forth to one another over the miles between. To let Bell reach the cow she pastured on the far side of the high moor, Wade is said to have laid down a great paved road, the ancient causeway on Wheeldale Moor still called Wade's Causeway.

The Hammer Across the Moor

Other features of the moors were credited to their domestic quarrels: in a fit of temper Wade scooped up a handful of earth to hurl at Bell, and the hollow he tore out became the natural amphitheatre of the Hole of Horcum, while the clod that missed her landed as the hill of Blakey Topping. The Tudor antiquarian John Leland recorded the belief that Wade had been a giant and the owner of Mulgrave Castle as early as the 1530s in his Itinerary, proof the tale was already established local tradition four centuries before it was ever collected as folklore in its own right.

The causeway itself has long been treated as Roman, part of a route running some thirty-three to thirty-five miles from Amotherby near Malton to the coast north of Whitby, linked to the fort at Malton and the camps at Cawthorn. More recent archaeology has complicated that picture: some researchers now argue the visible earthwork may be medieval, or even a much-modified Neolithic or Bronze Age boundary feature reused long after its original purpose was forgotten. Whatever period it actually belongs to, the exposed stretch has been protected since 1912 and cleared of turf in stages over the following decade. Behind the giant may lie a dim memory of a real Wada, a powerful Anglian nobleman of eighth-century Northumbria; folklore simply enlarged him, and his wife, to a scale fit to shape the moors themselves.

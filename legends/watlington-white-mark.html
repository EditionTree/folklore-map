Watlington White Mark

A local squire cut this giant chalk arrow into the hill in 1764 so his steeple-less church would appear, from his window, to wear a spire — though some say the shape is a far older fertility symbol.

The Legend

High on Watlington Hill in the Chiltern Hills above the Oxfordshire town of Watlington, a giant obelisk-and-triangle shape has been cut into the chalk since 1764. According to the most popular account, the local squire Edward Horne was unhappy that the parish church of St Leonard, down in the village below, had no spire. From the window of his home, he had the figure cut into the hillside at exactly the angle needed to sit directly above the church tower, completing — by optical illusion — the spire the building had always lacked.

The Story

Not everyone accepts the squire's-folly story. Some antiquarians, among them the writer H. J. Massingham, argued the mark is far older than the eighteenth century, pointing to its position just below the ancient Icknield Way — the prehistoric ridgeway track that runs the length of the Chiltern escarpment — and suggesting it may once have served as a fertility symbol, comparable to the famously priapic Cerne Abbas Giant in Dorset. On this reading, Horne's 1764 cutting would have been a re-scouring of a much older figure rather than a fresh creation.

Whichever story is true, the White Mark remains one of several chalk figures carved into the Chiltern escarpment, alongside the Whiteleaf Cross and Bledlow Cross. The hill and its mark are now owned by the National Trust, and the viewpoint from which the 'spire' illusion was supposedly designed to work remains a popular target for walkers tracing the story for themselves.

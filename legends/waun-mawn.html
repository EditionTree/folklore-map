Waun Mawn

Dismantled bluestones from this Preseli mountain circle may be the very stones Merlin carried to build Stonehenge — a twelfth-century legend now backed by startling archaeological evidence.

A visual reconstruction of the dismantling of the Waun Mawn stone circle
The Legend

In 1136, Geoffrey of Monmouth wrote that Stonehenge’s stones originally belonged to a monument in Ireland called the Giants’ Dance, and that the wizard Merlin transported them to Salisbury Plain for their healing properties. For centuries the tale was dismissed as pure fantasy. Then, in 2018, excavations at Waun Mawn in the Preseli Mountains of Pembrokeshire revealed something remarkable: the remains of a dismantled stone circle whose diameter of 110 metres exactly matches the enclosing ditch of Stonehenge, and whose orientation aligns on the midsummer solstice sunrise — precisely like its famous successor.

The evidence suggests that Neolithic communities dismantled their great Welsh circle and dragged its stones 150 miles eastward to Wiltshire, carrying not just stone but the cultural memory of their monument.

The Circle That Travelled

Radiocarbon dating placed Waun Mawn’s construction around 3000 BC, shortly before work began at Stonehenge. Of the original stones, only four remain standing; six empty sockets were excavated, their shapes matching specific bluestone pillars now at Stonehenge. The evidence suggests that Neolithic communities dismantled their great Welsh circle and dragged its stones 150 miles eastward to Wiltshire, carrying not just stone but the cultural memory of their monument. The circle they left behind had been the third largest in Britain.

Geoffrey of Monmouth placed the original stones in Ireland, not Wales, but the Preseli Mountains lie on the sea route between Ireland and southern England, and the cultural geography of the deep Neolithic did not observe modern national boundaries. What matters is that a medieval chronicler preserved, in the language of wizardry and giants, a genuine memory of stones that travelled — a folk tradition that survived three thousand years before archaeology caught up with it.

Wayland's Smithy

A Neolithic chambered tomb later claimed by folklore as the forge of Wayland the Smith. It is a perfect example of old stone becoming older story.

A visual interpretation of Wayland's Smithy on the Ridgeway
The Legend

Wayland's Smithy is a fine Neolithic chambered long barrow standing among trees on the Ridgeway, the ancient track along the Berkshire Downs in Oxfordshire, close to the Uffington White Horse. Though it is a burial tomb some five and a half thousand years old, its name and fame come from a much later legend that claimed it as the forge of Wayland — Wayland the Smith, the supernatural master-smith of Germanic and Norse mythology (Völundr), whom the Anglo-Saxons brought to Britain.

It was said that if a traveller's horse had cast a shoe, he need only leave the horse beside the barrow together with a silver coin upon the capstone.

The Coin on the Capstone

The legend that attached to the tomb is precise and charming. It was said that if a traveller's horse had cast a shoe, he need only leave the horse beside the barrow together with a silver coin upon the capstone, and withdraw out of sight; when he returned, the coin would be gone and the horse would be found freshly and perfectly shod, the work of the invisible Wayland. The smith would do the work only if left unseen and properly paid — the unseen craftsman of the old stones, plying his trade for those who knew the custom.

That an Anglo-Saxon legend of a Germanic smith-god should have settled on a far older prehistoric tomb is a perfect illustration of how folklore reclaims ancient monuments, giving a forgotten stone a new and vivid story. The name is recorded from Saxon times, showing how early the association was made. Set on the lonely Ridgeway near the White Horse and Dragon Hill, Wayland's Smithy is a classic case of old stone becoming older story — a burial mound transformed by legend into the workshop of a mythic smith.

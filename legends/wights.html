Wights

Spirits that inhabit places, objects, and the land itself in Norse and Anglo-Saxon tradition. Some are protective, some malevolent. The land-wights of Britain were the spiritual guardians of the island that Norse settlers had to negotiate with before they could truly belong here.

The Legend

In the older Norse and Anglo-Saxon belief that underlies much of northern English folklore, a 'wight' (from Old English wiht, a being or creature) was a spirit dwelling in the land, in a place, or in an object. The landwights — the land-spirits — inhabited particular hills, stones, springs and homesteads, and the prosperity of a farm or a district was bound up with keeping on good terms with them.

The Haunting at the Heart of the Tale

Some wights were protective guardians of house and ground, to be honoured with small offerings and not offended by ill conduct or foul language; others were dangerous, and the barrow-dwelling dead were a feared kind of wight in their own right. The belief left deep traces: the courtesy shown to the spirit of a place, the reluctance to disturb an old stone or thorn, and the sense that the land itself was inhabited and watchful, all descend from the old conviction that one shared the countryside with unseen wights who could help or harm.

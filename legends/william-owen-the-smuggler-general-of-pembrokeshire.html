William Owen, the Smuggler General of Pembrokeshire

Born to a respectable farming family in Nevern in 1717, William Owen ran away to sea as a teenager and became one of Wales's most notorious smugglers, running brandy and salt across Cardigan Bay, leading an armed 'siege' of a Cardiganshire mansion, and surviving one murder trial only to hang after a second.

The Legend

William Owen was born in January 1717 in Nevern, Pembrokeshire, the son of a well-to-do farmer who hoped his clever son would become a clergyman. Instead Owen ran away to sea as a teenager and, sailing as far as the Caribbean and trading blows with Spanish vessels along the way, built himself into one of the most successful smugglers on the Welsh coast, running brandy, salt and other contraband across Cardigan Bay between the Isle of Man and the harbours of Pembrokeshire and the Llyn Peninsula.

The Figure Behind the Legend

His most colourful exploit came when he led an armed band of Pembrokeshire and Cardiganshire men, gathered to support a friend, 'Mr Parry', in a dispute with the powerful Johnes family of Aber-mad, in a march on the Johnes' mansion near Aberystwyth. Nominated 'general' of the assembled men, Owen led them to the gates of the great house, but at the sight of its armed and well-manned defences his troops lost their nerve and the siege collapsed without a shot fired. He was also implicated in a corn riot in 1741, part of a wider pattern of coastal disorder that ran alongside his smuggling.

The law caught up with him twice. In spring 1744 a customs officer was killed during a confrontation over contraband in Cardigan Bay, and Owen stood trial for it at Hereford in March 1745. He had, by his own account, 'gained the affection of the country very much', and after twelve hours of proceedings a jury acquitted him outright, though the surviving court record lists his conviction on the lesser charge of manslaughter. His wife died at sea in a storm not long after. Then, in 1746, a fencing master named James Lilly was found shot dead on the road, and witnesses placed Owen at the scene with a pistol in hand. Tried at the Carmarthen Great Sessions in April 1747, he was convicted on circumstantial evidence and hanged on 2 May, aged thirty.

While awaiting execution, Owen dictated or wrote his life story to the Reverend John Davies, rector of Cardigan; the manuscript, some eighteen thousand words on vellum, survives at the National Library of Wales alongside a transcript of his final trial, the only surviving primary account of the case. His exploits were dramatised on stage in towns across Wales within his own lifetime, and his confession remains one of the best-documented smugglers' lives of eighteenth-century Wales.

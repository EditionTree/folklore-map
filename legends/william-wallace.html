William Wallace

Guardian of Scotland, Wallace defeated the English at Stirling Bridge in 1297 and became the symbol of resistance so enduring that the English king broke all rules of war to make his execution a maximum public spectacle. The folk tradition grew far larger than the man.

A visual interpretation of Wallace commanding at Stirling Bridge
The Legend

Sir William Wallace, the Guardian of Scotland, rose from obscurity to lead the resistance against Edward I of England, and his victory over a far larger English army at Stirling Bridge in 1297 — luring them onto the narrow crossing where their numbers counted for nothing — made him the enduring symbol of Scottish freedom. Defeated the next year at Falkirk, he waged a fugitive war until his betrayal and brutal execution in London in 1305.

Wallace defeated the English at Stirling Bridge in 1297 and became an enduring symbol of resistance.

The Victory That Became a Legend

Death turned the man into legend. The fifteenth-century poet Blind Harry's epic Wallace swelled his deeds into national myth, and the landscape of central Scotland is studded with his memory — Wallace's Oak at Elderslie, the caves and crags where he is said to have sheltered, the great Victorian monument that crowns the Abbey Craig above Stirling Bridge. Whatever the historical man, the legendary Wallace became the patriot-hero against whom all later Scottish nationhood measured itself, the commoner who defied an empire.

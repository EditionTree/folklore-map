Witch-hare of Dartmoor

A Dartmoor folk tale tells of a witch able to take the shape of a large hare. The transformed witch is pursued after stealing food, but her uncanny speed and shifting form mark the story as part of the moor's witchcraft tradition.

A visual interpretation of the Dartmoor witch-hare tradition
The Legend

The shape-shifting witch-hare is one of the most widespread of all British witch beliefs, and Dartmoor keeps its own vivid version. The tales tell of an old woman able to take the form of a great hare — to steal milk by sucking the cows, to poach, or simply to torment a neighbouring farm — who is repeatedly coursed by hounds but always escapes, slipping back into her cottage just as the dogs close in.

On Dartmoor, the witch-hare runs between cottage, field and old beliefs about shape-shifting.

The Hare on the Moor

The witch is unmasked in the usual way: the hare can only be harmed by a charmed shot, traditionally a slug of silver — a melted-down button or sixpence — and when at last the creature is wounded by such a shot and tracked to the old woman's door, she is found within nursing a fresh wound in the very limb where the hare was hit. The proof of the wound is the proof of the witch. Found across Devon, Cornwall and beyond, the Dartmoor witch-hare ties the everyday suspicions of the farming community — soured milk, vanished game, an unpopular old woman — to the deep folk-belief in transformation.

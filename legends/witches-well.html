Witches' Well

A cast-iron fountain on Edinburgh's Royal Mile marks where hundreds were strangled and burned for witchcraft, its plaque carved with a serpent and the intertwined faces of good and evil.

The Legend

The Witches' Well is a small memorial in Edinburgh, set into the wall at the top of the Royal Mile by the Castle Esplanade, marking the place where many of those accused of witchcraft in Scotland were strangled and burned. It is the only monument of its kind in the city, and stands in remembrance of a dark chapter of Scottish history: between roughly the 16th and 18th centuries some 3,000 to 4,000 people, the great majority of them women, were tried for witchcraft in Scotland, and many hundreds were executed, a number of them on the Castlehill where the well now stands.

most of those who suffered were not witches at all but innocent victims of fear and superstition.

The Memorial on the Wall

The memorial takes the form of a cast-iron drinking fountain and a relief plaque, designed at the end of the 19th century, whose imagery deliberately reflects the ambiguity of the witch-trials: it bears a serpent, the heads of a foxglove plant, and the faces of good and evil intertwined, acknowledging that those condemned were sometimes healers and wise-women and sometimes thought malevolent, and that great injustice was done. A small plaque notes that most of those who suffered were not witches at all but innocent victims of fear and superstition.

The Witches' Well is a place of sober commemoration rather than legend in the storytelling sense, but it is a powerful focus for the memory of Scotland's witch-hunts — among the most intense in Europe — and for the named women, like those of North Berwick, Janet Horne and so many others, who died on charges of witchcraft. It marks the meeting-point of folklore and history, where the long tradition of the Scottish witch is grounded in the real and terrible record of the trials.

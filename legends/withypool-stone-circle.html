Withypool Stone Circle

A ring of nearly a hundred small stones on Exmoor moorland — where the pixies come to thresh at harvest, and where no one has ever counted the stones twice and arrived at the same number.

A visual interpretation of Withypool Stone Circle
The Legend

Withypool Stone Circle stands on the upland moor above the village of Withypool in Somerset, approximately forty-five metres in diameter and containing around a hundred small stones, most worn low into the heather. It is one of only two prehistoric stone circles on Exmoor — the other being the Porlock Stone Circle — and has been a scheduled ancient monument since 1928. The site dates broadly to the Bronze Age, and like most Exmoor monuments has no surviving written record of the ritual that originally organised its construction. Historic England records that there were perhaps a hundred and eight stones originally; by 1905, thirty-seven remained visible above ground, with evidence that more lie just beneath the turf.

The Exmoor ring was said never to yield the same count of stones twice.

The Uncountable Circle

The counting tradition documented in the existing record holds that no one can count the stones three times and arrive at the same number twice — and that attempting to do so brings misfortune. This echoes a widespread British folk belief associated with large stone circles: at the Rollright Stones in Oxfordshire, at Stanton Drew in Somerset, and at Long Meg in Cumbria, the same motif recurs. It belongs to a deeper tradition in which prehistoric monuments resist counting, confuse travellers, and punish those who try to take inventory of the sacred. To count the dead is to disturb them; the stones do not consent to be numbered.

The pixie traditions of the Withypool area, documented by Jack Hurley in Legends of Exmoor (1973), add a second layer. According to accounts preserved from the farming community, pixies came to Withypool farms on harvest nights to thresh the corn, performing the labour silently and without payment. When a farmer's wife, curious to see them, left out gifts of clothing in gratitude, the pixies were offended — in the standard logic of brownie folklore, gifts of clothes mean dismissal — and they never came again. The stone circle and the pixies of the surrounding farms belong to the same imaginative landscape: a moor where the boundaries between ordinary farmland and something older and wilder remain permeable.

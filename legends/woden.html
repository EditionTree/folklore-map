Woden

The Anglo-Saxon form of Odin, remembered in royal genealogies, place-names, and Wednesday. He stands where god, ancestor, wanderer, and war-lord blur into one shadowed figure.

The Legend

Woden is the chief god of the Anglo-Saxon pagan pantheon, the English form of the deity known to the Norse as Odin and to the continental Germanic peoples as Wodan. Though the conversion of England to Christianity swept away the open worship of him before much of his mythology could be written down, his deep importance is everywhere implied. He stands at the head of the royal genealogies of nearly every Anglo-Saxon kingdom, Wessex, Mercia, Kent, Northumbria and others all traced their kings' descent from Woden, making him the divine ancestor of English royalty itself. The earliest documentation of this royal descent is Bede's Historia Ecclesiastica Gentis Anglorum, completed in 731, with the same genealogies also preserved in the Anglian collection and the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, where Woden is typically placed nine generations back from each dynasty's founder.

The Divine Presence

From the better-recorded Norse traditions and scattered English evidence, Woden emerges as a complex and shadowed figure: a god of war and victory, but also of wisdom, poetry, frenzy, magic and the dead, a wanderer who gives and withholds success in battle, and who is bound up with runes and hidden knowledge. The Old English Nine Herbs Charm shows him wielding magic against poison and disease, a rare native glimpse of the god in action. His name carries the sense of fury or inspired ecstasy, the divine madness of the seer and the warrior alike.

His memory is stamped across the English landscape and calendar. Wednesday is 'Woden's day'; place-names such as Wednesbury, Wednesfield, Wansdyke (Woden's dyke) and Woodnesborough preserve his name in the soil. After conversion he was demoted in folk memory, sometimes recast as a wild huntsman or a devil riding the night sky, but he never wholly vanished, a god, ancestor, magician and lord of the slain whose shadow lies under the surface of English tradition.
